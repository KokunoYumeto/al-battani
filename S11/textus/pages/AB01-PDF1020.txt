% Printed p. 132: chapter 41 continued: the half sixth is added to the part or subtracted from it, according to whether the part is an excess or a defect and the minutes of the third table are more or less than thirty; the part is then subtracted from 11;45 when it is an excess over 12;11 and added to 11;45 when it is a defect: the result is the corrected arc of vision, by the excess or defect of the moon's light at its distance from the earth; if the distance between the sun and the moon in degrees of setting, kept before, equals the corrected arc of vision or exceeds it, the new moon is seen, and if it is less, it is not seen in that place; the clearness and purity of the air help the sighting and its thickness and turbidity hinder it, with the difference of men's eyes in strength and weakness; the twilight may be thick and then thin out before the moon sets, so that the new moon is seen after the time of sighting on which one works: one should not despair of the sighting until the moon is known to have gone down below the horizon; for these reasons it can be seen in one place and not in another, also from the difference of the rising and setting times of the signs in the places, in length and shortness; then the opinion of the ancients, that it is not seen at less than a day and a night: with the moon's least motion and the sun's greatest the moon's distance from the sun in a day and a night is ten and a half and a third degrees, the arc of vision on this reckoning; with the moon's greatest motion and the sun's least it is about thirteen and two thirds degrees. 21 lines, five notes in three lines.
