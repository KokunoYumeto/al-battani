% Printed p. 152: chapter 43 continued: the end of the sides of the darkening, and the eclipse of the moon by the table. [The angle of the longitude known for the time of] the middle of the eclipse is taken on the side named in its derivation for the parallax of the moon and laid off from the azimuth of the ascendant of the middle of the eclipse towards the side opposite to the moon's latitude, if the moon is towards the east, and from the azimuth of the setting point of the middle of the eclipse, likewise towards the opposite side, if it is towards the west: where the count ends on the circle of the horizon, towards that degree passes the inclination of the darkening at the middle of the eclipse. This holds when the moon's latitude is northern; when it is southern and the moon in the eastern region the angle is laid off from the azimuth of the setting point, and in the western region from the azimuth of the ascendant, towards the side opposite to the moon's latitude. To know the eclipse of the moon by the table, approximately: the true latitude of the moon for the time of the opposition is entered into the two tables of the lunar eclipse, for the farthest and for the nearest distance. If it is found in the table of the nearest distance alone, the digits and the minutes of the fall beside it are taken, and of each as much as the minutes of the shares of the distance in the third table, written beside the corrected anomaly of the moon for the time of the opposition in the tables of the equation, are of sixty minutes: the result is the digits of the eclipse and the amount of the fall. If the latitude falls in both tables, what stands beside it in each is taken, of digits, fall and stay (if the moon has a stay), each set down in its place; of the differences between the two tables in digits, fall and stay as much is taken as the minutes of the third table beside the anomaly of the moon are of sixty, and each result is always added to its counterpart from the first table, that of the farthest distance: the sums are the digits of the eclipse of the diameter of the moon, the amount of the fall and the amount of the stay. If the digits are less than 12 the moon is not wholly eclipsed and has no stay; if more than 12 it is wholly eclipsed and has a stay, as far as it enters the darkness; if exactly 12 it is wholly eclipsed and no more. The minutes of the fall, and of the stay if there is one, are divided by the gain of the moon: the hours of the fall and of the stay. The notes give two readings of the codex.
