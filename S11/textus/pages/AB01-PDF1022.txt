% Printed p. 130: chapter 41 continued: the sighting is known from the arcs of the equator between the sun and the moon at sunrise or sunset, observed in one clime and then known in the others; the arc of vision, as found by observation, is about twelve degrees of the times of the equator; the moon's motion from the sun in a day and a night is twelve degrees and eleven minutes, which agrees with the observed amount; twelve times are about four fifths of an hour, and the moon outruns the sun by about two fifths of a degree in that part of an hour; so when the sun sets with eleven and a half and a quarter times of the equator between it and the moon, the moon does not set before the twelve degrees and eleven minutes are completed, and the mean arc of vision is eleven and three quarters degrees of the rising and setting times of the signs in the places; the lit part of the moon's disc at that distance is about four fifths of a twelfth of the disc; the moon may be farther from the sun or nearer at the times of sighting, and nearer to the earth or farther by its place on the epicycle, so the new moon is not seen from one fixed arc; then the rule begins: to know whether the new moon will be seen, the sun and the moon are computed for sunset on the second day after the conjunction, the twenty-ninth of the Arabic month, with the moon's true latitude and its parallax in longitude and latitude, to get the apparent place of the moon. 22 lines, three notes in two lines.
