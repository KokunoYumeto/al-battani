% Printed p. 153: chapter 43 continued: the times of the lunar eclipse by the table, the eclipsed part of the moon's circle, and the sides of the darkening by the tables. [The hours of the fall are taken] from the hours of the opposition: the remainder is the hours of the beginning of the eclipse; added to the hours of the opposition they give the hours of the end of the clearing; the hours of the middle of the eclipse are the hours of the opposition. If the moon has a stay, the hours of the fall and of the stay together are taken from the hours of the opposition, which gives the beginning of the eclipse, and added to them, which gives the end of the clearing; the hours of the stay alone, taken from the hours of the opposition, give the beginning of the stay, and added to them the beginning of the clearing, approximately. If the digits of the eclipse are less than 12 they are entered in the table of the magnitudes of the eclipse, in the line of the number, and what stands under them in the second table, on which the magnitudes of the eclipse of the moon are written, is the part of the moon's circle that is eclipsed, in the measure in which its area is 12. To know the side from which the darkening of the eclipse begins and the side where the clearing ends, the digits of the eclipse of the moon's diameter are entered in the line of the number of the digits in the tables of the inclination of the darkenings, and what stands beside them is taken in the third table, and in the fourth also if the moon has a stay: the result of the third table is the inclination for the time of the beginning of the eclipse and of the end of the clearing, that of the fourth the inclination for the time of the stay and of the beginning of the clearing. Then, in the circle of the azimuth in which the seven circles for the seven climates are drawn, the azimuth written under the rising and the setting sign in the given climate and under the sign that follows it is taken; the difference between the azimuths of the two signs is multiplied by the degrees of the ascendant and divided by thirty, and the result is added to the azimuth of the rising sign if it is the smaller, and taken from it if it is the greater: the azimuth of the rising or of the setting point after this is the azimuth of the rising and of the setting degree of each time. The azimuth of the setting point is like that of the ascendant, on the opposite side: if that of the ascendant is northern, that of the setting point is southern, so that either is known from the other; the side of the azimuth is known from the drawings of the circle, in the sides written on it where the summer and the winter risings and settings fall: the summer ones are northern, the winter ones southern. Then the parts of the third table are laid off from the azimuth of the ascendant of the beginning of the eclipse towards the side opposite to the latitude, that is the latitude of the moon, and likewise from the azimuth of the setting point of the end of the clearing towards the side opposite to the moon's latitude.
