% Commentary on chapter 57 b) continued: (this instrument gathers in one the uses) of all the two Greek instruments, the spherical astrolabe (note 1: ὄργανον ἀστρολάβον of Ptolemy V, 1, of Proclus etc. Cf. Tannery, Recherches sur l'histoire de l'astronomie ancienne, pp. 70-73) and the solid sphere (note 2: στερεὰ σφαῖρα, Ptolemy VIII, 3 (ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 92-97); it represents the celestial sphere and has three rings (of the solstitial colure, of the meridian, of the horizon)). Al-Battānī perhaps first invented and built it, which the skill of the craftsmen could not lack; for at that time the best instruments of all were built especially in the city of Ḥarrān, from which al-Battānī came. The author of the book al-Fihrist (note 3: Kitāb al-Fihrist, p. 285; translated into German by H. Suter, Das Mathematiker-Verzeichniss im Fihrist, in Abhandlungen zur Geschichte der Mathematik, vol. VI, Leipzig 1892, pp. 41-42 (= Supplement to vol. 37 of the Zeitschrift für Mathematik und Physik)) has handed down to us the names of the chief makers, among whom al-Battānī's father too is perhaps to be counted (note 4: cf. what I said in the preface). Below, Schiaparelli's figure of the armillary sphere.
