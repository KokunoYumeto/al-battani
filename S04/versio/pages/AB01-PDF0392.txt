% Commentary on chapter 52 a) II concluded (Abraham bar Ḥiyyā): a great argument against them is that they said the fixed stars go forward and back 8°, whereas by al-Battānī's observation they have advanced 15° from the place they held at the time of the ancient observation; so the opinions of the Indians are overturned if what al-Battānī says is true. III. Al-Battānī names only the ancient astrologers, though, as shown, there were Arabs who held the doctrine, mostly received from the Indians (note 1: already in 499 Āryabhaṭa held the trepidation; most later Indians followed it, few a continuous precession; Colebrooke, Whitney, Th. H. Martin; the arc of oscillation was taken variously, 48°, 54°, etc.): so Ḥabash in his first book (not in his later ones), in which, after Ibn al-Qifṭī, he followed the computations of the Indian book as-Sindhind (Siddhānta); so Muḥammad ibn Ismā‘īl at-Tanūkhī (note 2: of uncertain date, but before 1250; Suter), who «travelled through various regions and visited India, whence he brought strange notions of astronomy, as the motion of advance and retreat [the trepidation] and others» (note 3: Ibn al-Qifṭī in Casiri, Bibliotheca Arabico-Hispana Escurialensis, and in Sédillot's introduction to Ulugh Beg); so finally Ibn al-Adamī (note 4: Suter), who in a book published after his death by a pupil in 308 H. (beginning 22 May 920) computed the motions of the stars after the method of as-Sindhind and said things about the motion of advance and retreat of the sky that nobody had set out before; before this book appeared, things were heard about that motion that could be neither understood nor reduced to a fixed rule; when it appeared, the form of that strange motion could be understood, and many weighed the doctrine attentively. Ṣā‘id ibn al-Ḥasan al-Andalusī (note 5: Suter; he died in Shawwāl 462 H., July-August 1070), judge of Toledo, says: after reading this book things appeared to me about that motion that I think have appeared to nobody yet, and I noted their causes in my book called Correction of the motions of the stars (note 6: Ibn al-Qifṭī in Casiri; Sédillot; Nallino completed the Arabic text from the codex of the Lincei library, f. 92 r.-v.). Some Arabs came to the hypothesis of trepidation by the Greek way: Thābit ibn Qurrah, who, as seen on pp. 298 and 301-02, chiefly from Theon's words and the uncertainty of the old observations, founded a new theory (note 7: Delambre, pp. 73-75) to explain the variations of the obliquity of the ecliptic, the various lengths of the year and the discrepancies in the motion of the fixed stars. Alpetragius (p. 299) cites the authority of the fictitious and very ancient philosopher Hermes, whose apocryphal or pseudepigraphic works the Arabs had translated from Greek; finally Augustinus Ricci says he learned from his master Abraham Zacut (note 8: a Hispano-Jew of the end of the fifteenth century, who drew his astronomy from Arabic and Hebrew books; this he took from Isaac Israeli's Yesod ha-‘olam, written at Toledo in 1310) that the Arabic doctrine of trepidation rests on two observations of stars that Hermes made 1985 years before the epoch of Ptolemy's catalogue, by which the longitudes were 7 degrees greater than Ptolemy's (note 9: A. Ricius, De motu octavae sphaerae, Paris 1521, p. 23, the place noted by Riccioli and Th. H. Martin; Ricci was born at Casale in Piedmont).
