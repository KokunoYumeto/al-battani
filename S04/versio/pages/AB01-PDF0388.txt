% Commentary on chapter 52 a) I continued: so also Alpetragius, al-Biṭrūǵī, in the Latin version made from a Hebrew one (note 1: Alpetragii Arabi planetarum theorica, Venice 1531, by Calo Calonymos; he lived in the twelfth century): the predecessors, as Hermes, and their successors who deal with images [astrologers, ἀποτελεσματικοί] say that these stars have a motion sometimes in the order of the signs and sometimes against it, as if it were self-evident or handed down; when later men came, the Chaldaeans and those who found the motion of these stars before Bactenzar [Nabonassar], to prove the truth of what the first had found, they did not find that motion and dropped it. He adds that Hipparchus and Ptolemy deduced a continuous precession from the observations of Timocharis, Aristyllus and Menelaus, and goes on: someone after Ptolemy, Taun [Theon] of Alexandria (note 2: he ascribes to Theon himself what Theon only reports of certain astrologers), held that the fixed stars have a motion of access and recess of 8° each, and besides a motion in the order of the signs of one degree in a hundred years; later men rejected this motion, finding the places of the stars by observation elsewhere than where they stood before, some adding and some subtracting according to the times; further, Albategnius declared that the fixed stars move from the vernal equinox in equal times with a different course, and so omitted this motion. Likewise Ibn al-Qifṭī (d. 646 H., beginning 25 April 1248) thinks that Ḥabash (note 3: Ibn al-Qifṭī in the notes to Flügel's Fihrist; Ḥabash professed the trepidation only in his youthful tables, after the Indian doctrines; in later books, as in the Berlin codex, he used a continuous precession) adapted the Indian notion of trepidation to the precepts of Theon of Alexandria; and Barhebraeus (note 4: his Syriac Chronicle, ed. Bruns and Kirsch 1789 and Bedjan 1890; his Syriac Astronomy) says of Theon: among his books celebrated throughout the world is the book of the Canon, in which he set out how the change of the tropic points would be computed if they advanced one degree in 80 years up to eight degrees and then went back; which Ptolemy did not admit, since without this addition the computations agree with the motions found by instruments of observation (note 5: Barhebraeus puts Theon in Ptolemy's age, under Antoninus; Lippert). II. Letronne (note 6: Nouvelles recherches sur le calendrier des anciens Égyptiens, 1864) and Th. H. Martin (note 7: his memoir on whether the precession was known before Hipparchus, 1869; Martin knew al-Battānī's passage only from Letronne) have weighed this passage of al-Battānī, but wrongly, thinking that al-Battānī spoke of Arabic astrologers and counted Ptolemy among those who held the trepidation true.
