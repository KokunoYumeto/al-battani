% Commentary on appendix A b) continued: (in the interval of 30 years the signs grow) by 5 units. Since, according to the Arab astronomers, the epoch of the hijrah was a Thursday, the sign of the first year of the hijrah is 5; so the sign 1 is supposed to belong to a fictitious year 0. After 210 complete Arab years the signs of the years always return to their former order; so when the first 210 (or 7 × 30) are known, all the rest are known. Hence al-Battānī could make only two tables, in one of which the first thirty years from the hijrah are written, in the other the first seven multiples of thirty. A year over 30 is represented as the sum of two quantities, one of which is 30 or x × 30, the other 0 or a number less than 30. The term 30 or x × 30 is an interval of years counted from a fictitious year 0 to which the sign 0 belongs; the term less than 30, on the contrary, is counted from the hijrah, or from the fictitious year 0 whose sign is 1. So the sign of the thirtieth year of the hijrah is greater by one than the sign of the last year of the thirty-year cycle taken by itself. This was why al-Battānī lessened the signs by a unit in the table of collected years; the collected years are thus regarded as any intervals of years to be added to the years written singly. But if the collected years indicate the interval of time elapsed from the hijrah, their signs must clearly be increased by one, as al-Battānī teaches. In doing so al-Battānī followed another method than the authors of the Tabula Probata, as al-Khāzinī testifies, Vatican codex, f. 110 v. II. Here and in the table vol. II, p. 7 the codex of the Escorial counts among the leap years those which hold the places ..... VIII, ..... XIX, ..... XXVII in the thirty-year cycle. In both places I have restored ..... VII, .... XVIII, ..... XXVI, as almost all Arab astronomers have and as the Muslims always accepted in civil use; for the numbers of the codex evidently come from a most foolish glossator, who worked so stupidly that he did not notice that his corrections conflicted both with the signs of the years themselves in the table vol. II, p. 7, and with all the tables vol. II, pp. 9-18, 20 and 25! The glossator perhaps borrowed his order of intercalation from the method of the astronomer Ḥabash, who put 8, 11, .... 19, ..... 27 for 7, 10, ..... 18, ..... 26. Besides the Berlin codex of Ḥabash, in which all the celestial motions in Arab years are computed according to such a method of intercalation, cf. what al-Bīrūnī tells of Ḥabash, Chronology, pp. 197-98 of the text, 178-80 of the translation (and Sachau's note, p. 416). On appendix B. a) pp. 145-146: that the era of Dhū 'l-qarnayn, or of the Seleucids, according to al-Battānī begins on 1 September (aylūl), not on 1 October 312 before Christ, I have already noted in note a to chapter XXXII (p. 242) and elsewhere. The table vol. II, p. 8 covers only the first 28 years, for after 28 years have passed the signs return to their former order. But why al-Battānī lessened the signs of the years and months by a unit I do not understand; for afterwards he must add a unit again to the sign taken from the table. It would have been much more convenient to put the signs in the table without lessening them. Al-Battānī's rule, moreover, namely always to add a year to the given years, is false. It would be true if the Roman years were always common; for since in the interval of a common year the signs grow by one unit, to seek the year x + 1 in the table is the same as to increase by one the sign found for the year x. But in a leap year the signs grow by two units; so al-Battānī's precept becomes false whenever the given year x is a leap year, for the sign of the year x + 1 is then the sign of the year x increased by two units. The true rule was always to enter the table with the given year x and to add a unit to the corresponding sign.
