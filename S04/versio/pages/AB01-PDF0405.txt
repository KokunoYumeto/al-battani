% Commentary on chapter 55 b) continued: the proportion ca : (semidiurnal arc of A − semidiurnal arc of B) = distance of A from the meridian : semidiurnal arc of A, whence ca = (semid. A − semid. B) × distance of A from the meridian / semidiurnal arc of A. If the distance is given in temporal hours (note 1: as the author says on pp. 132-33, the temporal hours of distance from the midheaven are the difference between the right ascension of the star and that of the midheaven, divided by the horary times of the star), of which the semidiurnal arc contains six, ca = (semid. A − semid. B) × hours of distance of A / 6. From the precepts of chapter XIX: semidiurnal arc of A = 90° + the difference between the right and the oblique ascension of A, and the same for B; so (semid. A − semid. B) = (oblique ascension A − oblique ascension B) − (right ascension A − right ascension B). With α the difference of the right ascensions and β that of the oblique, al-Battānī's rule (note 2: p. 133, l. 29, for «cognoscuntur. Idest» read «cognoscuntur; idest»; and l. 31 for «deprehende; si gradus» read «deprehendes. — Si gradus») is (α) direction = α ± (β − α) × temporal hours of distance of A / 6, where (α − β) is put for (β − α) if α > β; the second term of the second member has the sign − when α > β, + when α < β. This holds if A and B are in the eastern hemisphere. When they are in the western, for the oblique ascensions of A and B the oblique ascensions of (A + 180°) and (B + 180°), that is the descensions of A and B, are to be taken; why, the figure here shows. If A and B are in different hemispheres, one proceeds as al-Battānī teaches on p. 134, l. 16-20: direct B to the upper or lower meridian (whichever of the two lies between B and A); this direction is the difference between the right ascension of B and that of the midheaven or the cardine of the Earth; to this difference add the quantity Dc, that is the temporal hours of distance of A from the same meridian multiplied by the horary times of B (note 3: the codex and Plato do not mention this multiplication; but if it is neglected the precept is false, for Da, not Dc, is known; see below on the proof of al-Battānī's second rule); the direction sought results. This method of computation agrees with the computation of radiations which the Arabs often call Ptolemaic (pp. 309-10), except that there for α and β are taken the arcs of the ecliptic (continued). The left column of the notes begins with the end of Schiaparelli's note 3 of p. 315: «... exceed (25° or 30°) of declination, this error is not so great, especially in the climes of the cities of ar-Raqqah, Antioch, Baghdad; it is even smaller in the more southern places of the Earth near the equator. — Schiaparelli.]» That Ptolemy and other Greeks already used this approximation will appear from p. 317.
