% Commentary on chapter 49 b) concluded (Schiaparelli): all this probably comes from rough computations and observations: the Arabs before al-Battānī held that stars of the first magnitude appear 1 hour (15°) before sunrise and after sunset, those of the sixth 2 hours (30°), and put the other orders in between. Ulugh Beg (p. 143 text, 193 transl.) considers the depression of the Sun: about 12° for the first magnitude, 14° for the second, and so on by 2° to 22° for the sixth, true for stars near the ecliptic (note 1: nothing in the other published Arabic astronomers; Delambre on Ibn Yūnus: twilight, depression 18°; Ibn al-Haytham, De crepusculis: 19°). Chapter 50, a) p. 120, l. 18: the distances of the planets «after the later sages after Ptolemy»: the codex has «ante», which does not fit «recentiores»; the doubt goes, since all these computations rest on Ptolemy's eccentricities and epicycles (note b). The theory rests on contiguous spheres: the least distance of a higher planet equals the greatest of the one below; held by all known Arabic astronomers, and in the 15th century by ‘Alī Qūshǵī (Delambre p. 198); discussed philosophically in the epistles of the Ikhwān aṣ-ṣafā’ of Basra (10th century; note 2: Dieterici, Posen 1864). Before al-Battānī al-Farghānī, chapter XXI (Golius p. 80), quoted: Ptolemy gave only the distances of the Sun and the Moon, and the eccentricities and epicycles; taking the greatest distance of the Moon equal to the least of Mercury, and so on, the greatest of Venus equals the least of the Sun, which Ptolemy explained, since there is no void between the spheres. Al-Farghānī (pp. 81-82) gives the distances in Earth radii and Arabic miles, the radius of the Earth 3250 miles (note 3: Nallino showed these miles are 1973 m); before A.H. 301 (began 6 Aug. 913) Ibn Rosteh (pp. 18-20) gives somewhat different numbers, with the radius of the Earth 3818 miles (begun).
