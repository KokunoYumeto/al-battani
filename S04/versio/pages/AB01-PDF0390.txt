% Commentary on chapter 52 a) II concluded: the grammarian or astrologer (who put the solstice at 7° 1/2 of Cancer) probably lived after the Greek astronomers knew the use of degrees, after the middle of the second century B.C. Bouché-Leclercq shows that Manilius (first century), Pseudo-Manetho (second and third) and other routine astrologers began the four seasons from the eighth degrees of the signs; to them Columella (first century) and Hyginus can be added (note 1: Hyginus, Astronomicon, ed. Bunte, p. 101: the Sun begins to rise at the eighth part of all the signs; Achilles Tatius, Isagoge, ch. 23: some put the solstice at the beginning of Cancer, some at the eighth part, some at the twelfth, some at the fifteenth). But the origin of the use is to be sought among the old Chaldaeans: Kugler (Die babylonische Mondrechnung, 1900) found from the cuneiform tablets that the Babylonians, already in the fourth and third centuries B.C., put the four points of the year at 8° 15′ (sometimes 10° and 8° 0′ 30″) of Aries, Cancer, Libra and Capricorn in the fixed Babylonian ecliptic, at 5° of these signs in our moving ecliptic and Hipparchus'. Al-Bīrūnī (Chron. pp. 325-26 text, 322 transl.) already saw that the 8 degrees of the astrologers professing the trepidation are to be connected with the Chaldaean way of beginning the seasons, and erred only in thinking the doctrine of trepidation very old: «the authors differ on the beginnings of the four seasons. Ptolemy, in his Introduction to the spherical art (al-madkhal ilà ’ṣ-ṣinā‘ah al-kuriyyah), says the Greeks put them at the entry of the Sun into the equinoctial and solstitial points. The Chaldaeans are said to have put them 8° after the equinoxes and solstices, which I think is said because their computations (in the tables ascribed to them) lagged behind what the Greek observations and tables required; and they fixed this amount of 8° because they thought the difference arose from a motion of the sphere forward and back, of greatest amount 8°. God knows best what they meant. The best and most fitting explanation of this motion is in zīǵ aṣ-ṣafā’iḥ by Abū Ǵa‘far al-Khāzin (note 2: died between about 350 and 360 H., 961-971) and in the book on the motions of the Sun by Ibrāhīm ibn Sinān (note 3: grandson of Thābit ibn Qurrah; died aged 38 in Muḥarram 335, August 946)». Al-Battānī, p. 127, l. 14, puts those astrologers before Hipparchus; Letronne and Martin show they lived after Hipparchus but perhaps before Ptolemy. Another sign: their precession, 1° in 80 years or 45″ a year (note 4: not 40″, as Letronne and Martin think; see p. 206), differs much from Ptolemy's 36″; if Hipparchus' tropical year is compared with his sidereal year, the annual precession is 46″,807, 1° in about 77 years; the astrologers seem to have taken this Hipparchian precession and lessened it insensibly to simplify the computations. Thābit ibn Qurrah, who in the epistle mentioned on p. 298 is uncertain about the true motion of the fixed stars, in a later (it seems) booklet on the motion of the eighth sphere proposes an ingenious doctrine of trepidation and cites this chapter of al-Battānī (note 5: Vatican Latin codices 4275 and 4083; Delambre, p. 74, used a Paris codex): «and that is what Ptolemy received...» (continued).
