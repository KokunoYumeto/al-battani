% Commentary on chapter 52 a) III concluded: in the fourth century H. (tenth century) Abū Ǵa‘far al-Khāzin (note 1: not to be confused with al-Khāzinī) and Ibrāhīm ibn Sinān followed the doctrine of trepidation; after that time the astronomers of the eastern caliphate, from Egypt to farthest India, rejected it (note 2: Naṣīr ad-dīn aṭ-Ṭūsī, at-Tadhkirah, tract II, ch. IV, and his commentator Qāḍī-zādeh). Not so in western Africa and Spain, where Arzachel (az-Zarqālī), Ṣā‘id al-Andalusī, Alpetragius and Abū ‘Alī al-Ḥasan (Aboul Hhassan) tried to explain by the trepidation the discrepancies between the oldest and the Arabic observations; from their writings the Latin Alfonsine Tables (1251) and many European astronomers down to the middle of the sixteenth century took it over. b) p. 127, l. 12-13: «the tropical year of 365d 6h 12m used by those astrologers»: it is never mentioned by Greek writers; it does not differ from the year al-Battānī, in chapter XXVII, ascribes to «certain Egyptians and Babylonians», discussed at length on pp. 204-209. Those Egyptians and Babylonians are probably just these astrologers, whom al-Battānī wrongly puts before Hipparchus. The Orientals often ascribe the invention of the trepidation to Hermes Trismegistus and the very ancient Chaldaeans and Egyptians, as the passages of al-Bīrūnī, Alpetragius, Abraham bar Ḥiyyā and Ricci cited in note a show; perhaps not false, if for the very ancient Chaldaeans and Egyptians one puts the Chaldaeans and Egyptians who lived after Hipparchus. Chapter 53 a) p. 128, l. 12 sqq.: Nallino used the name «revolution of the years» because it is current among our astrologers; it would be clearer to say «the conversion of civil years into tropical years». The problem is very frequent in astrology: someone is born in the year 1100 of Dhū 'l-qarnayn, 26 Aylūl, at 17h 45m, the Sun in degree x of the ecliptic; the moment is sought when in the year 1126 (completing the 26th year of life, or beginning the 27th) the Sun returns to the same point. This computation is called the revolution of the years of nativities (taḥwīl sinī al-mawālīd); when it concerns not a man's life but the fortunes of cities, peoples and nations, mostly reckoned from the great conjunctions of the planets, it is called the revolution of the years of the world (taḥwīl sinī al-‘ālam). Al-Battānī treats the same matter more briefly in appendix E, p. 148. The mathematical computation is very easy and set out in all the astronomical books of the Arabs; but the astrological predictions to be drawn from it make an immense, very obscure and intricate matter, so that very many Arabs (note 3: ten of them, before al-Battānī, are named by Suter; Latin versions of the books of Messahala (Mā shā’ Allāh) and Albumasar (Abū Ma‘shar) are printed) wrote special books on the revolutions of the years. The Arabs themselves... a book of the philosopher Hermes on the revolutions of nativities (continued).
