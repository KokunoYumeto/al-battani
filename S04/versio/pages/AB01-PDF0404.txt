% Commentary on chapter 55 b) continued: Henry Bate (see p. 308, note 5), who had read al-Battānī's work attentively, says on f. d2 r.: «the science of the ascensions of the signs between the quarters of the sky is the same as the science of the directions, since when one is known the other is known». Al-Battānī treated these ascensions in the spaces between the cardines in a special book, as said in the preface. The reason for publishing it was doubtless the ignorance of the astrologers, who, little versed in mathematics, erred badly in the computation of directions. Some perhaps thought the direction to be the difference of the right ascensions of the significator and the promissor, which is true only when the degree to which the direction is made is on the upper meridian (the cardine of the midheaven) or the lower (the cardine of the Earth) (note 1: for then the circle of position of the degree to which the direction is made is the meridian itself); others took the difference of the oblique ascensions or descensions, which is true only when the degree to which the direction is made is in the ascendant or the descendant (setting point) (note 2: for then the circle of position to which the planet is directed is the horizon itself); others again, like Ptolemy himself, though they rightly combined right and oblique ascensions, neglected the latitude of the planets. Let A be the «preceding place», the significator planet or apheta, to which in the direct direction the «following place» B (the promissor or anaereta) must be directed in the order of the signs. Both lie outside the cardines; for if B holds a cardine, the problem reduces to the difference of right or oblique ascensions, as just said. Let NAS be the circle of position drawn through A, P the pole of the equator; we must find how many degrees the arc bc of the equator contains which B traverses to reach C (figure). Al-Battānī gives two ways of computing bc, whose proof Nallino gives here. I. Let EE', FF' be the arcs of the parallels which B and A traverse in the daily motion of the sphere from the horizon to the meridian; through E and F let circles of declination be drawn to the equator produced; the arc Dβ of the equator will be the semidiurnal arc of the planet B, the arc Dα the semidiurnal arc of the planet A. Since Pb, Pc, Pa are circles of declination, the arc of the direction sought, bc, will be: bc = (♈a − ♈b) − ca = (right ascension of A − right ascension of B) − ca. If A were on the meridian the arc ca would vanish, for the circle of position would become the meridian itself, on which A and C would fall. If on the contrary A occupied the horizon at F, the circle of position would become the horizon itself, on which C would also fall; so the arc ca would reach its greatest value, βα, which is the difference between the semidiurnal arc of the planet A and that of B. Since the motion of the sphere is taken to be uniform, the author holds that the arc ca grows the more the farther A is from the meridian; this is what al-Battānī says on p. 132, l. 10-36. So according to him this proportion arises (note 3, by Schiaparelli in brackets: it is clear that this theorem cannot be reduced to a geometrical reason; for if it were true, the circle of position NAS would have to divide all the arcs of the parallels intercepted by the meridian and the horizon in one and the same ratio. The author's principle departs most from the truth for stars that culminate low near the horizon, whether in the northern or the southern quarter, not to speak of stars near the pole, for which al-Battānī's rule falls plainly into absurdity.
