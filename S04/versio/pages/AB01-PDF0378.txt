% Commentary on chapter 50 a) concluded: what Ibn Hibintā reports of al-Battānī agrees badly with the Escorial codex and Plato (note 1: the codex has «and he makes it like the Earth eighteen of it»; read «times», مره, for منه); what he reports of Ptolemy is not in his known works. The Greeks: Schiaparelli points to Proclus' Hypotyposes, where some, holding that there is no void and all intervals are filled by intermediate spheres, computed the distances of the Moon, Mercury, Venus and the Sun: from the greatest distance of the Moon 64ʳ 10′ and the least of the Sun 1160ʳ (from the Almagest), with Mercury's apogee to perigee 91ʳ 30′ : 34ʳ 4′, the apogee of Mercury 277ʳ 33′; with Venus' perigee to apogee 15ʳ 35′ : 204ʳ 25′, a fourth term 1150ʳ, nearly the least distance of the Sun (1160). Such computations, from the Almagest, could easily be ascribed by the Arabs to Ptolemy himself; Simplicius (later than Proclus) already did so: the greatest distance of Venus nearly the distance of the Sun, the greatest of Mercury nearly the least of Venus, the greatest of the Moon nearly the least of Mercury, «demonstrated in Ptolemy's Syntaxis» (note 4, the Greek). Simplicius, Proclus' pupil, perhaps knew the Almagest from a compendium like the Hypotyposes. So Kitāb al-manshūrāt, named only twice by al-Bīrūnī, may mean «the book of things set out [on astronomy]», a version of the Greek Ὑποτυπώσεις: a work of Proclus, not Ptolemy; some Arabs fell into the error Simplicius had made. b) pp. 120-122 (Schiaparelli): I. the diameters of the planets relative to the Earth: with Δ the apparent angular diameter of the Sun and R its mean distance, its linear diameter is RΔ (begun).
