% Commentary on chapter 57 b) continued: since the description of the instrument in al-Battānī's text is somewhat obscure, I think it worth while to set it out much more briefly and clearly; my words are best illustrated by the picture that Schiaparelli most kindly drew. But first I ask that in the text, vol. III, p. 210, l. 2, some words left out in the printing be restored: after «ilà hādhā al-mawḍiʿ» instead of the word «fa-waṣafnā» put «wa-ataynā ʿalà kull mā yuḥtāj an yuqaddama dhikruhu bi-qadr aṭ-ṭāqah fa-narà an nakhtim fī hādhā al-mawḍiʿ bi-waṣfinā». So the translation, vol. I, p. 139, l. 10 is to be corrected thus: «Since in this book we have come to this place and have brought, according to our powers, all whose mention it was necessary to give first, it seemed good to us to close [the book] at this place by describing the instrument» etc. The celestial globe, hung at the two poles of the equator by two axes, stands within the smallest of all the rings, which represents the movable meridian AAA; this meridian turns in its own plane within the fixed meridian BBB (whose rim bears the numbers of the degrees), so that the instrument can be fitted to any terrestrial latitude. Both meridians are of the same thickness and well fitted to each other, so that they seem to make a single ring. The fixed meridian is joined immovably with two rings HHH and CC, which represent the horizon and the prime vertical; the outer diameters of these three rings are equal, so that a single spherical system arises, into which, along the lines marking zenith and nadir, two spikes or pins are fixed, making two poles. In this way the axis arises about which the whole system turns within the largest ring DDD; this turns freely about the same axis SS, represents any vertical and gives the count of the degrees on its rim. Its office is to carry the index of the solar rays E, which, by means of two pinnules, can be set in the direction of the rays as the figure shows. [To the index is further joined a needle F, which can be pushed towards the centre of the machine until it touches the globe and shows on it the direction of the Sun, or drawn back so as not to hinder the rotation of the inner rings with respect to the largest outer one. The index of the rays is carried by a slider G, or movable appendage, which moves in a wide circular slot L of the largest ring; so it can be raised at will above the horizon, according to the various altitudes of the Sun. The whole instrument, by means of its two pins or axes, lower and upper, is fitted to any support of wood or iron, as al-Battānī indicates. However, if it is not too heavy, it can be hung from a ring joined to the upper axis and carried by hand; then the weight of the instrument itself keeps the axis vertical. But in this case I think it necessary to balance the index of the rays with another piece of the same weight moving in a slot of the largest ring made in the azimuth opposite to the index. This double way of supporting the armillary sphere, either on a fixed support or by hand, al-Battānī indicates, but somewhat confusedly. All the circles of the sphere are present in this instrument, so that all problems requiring the use of the armillary sphere can be solved, as al-Battānī shows with some examples on p. 142. — Schiaparelli.] c) pp. 143-144: the triquetrum or parallactic rules. The name of the instrument in al-Battānī is al-ʿiḍādah aṭ-ṭawīlah «the long alidade» or al-ʿiḍādatāni aṭ-ṭawīlatāni «the two long alidades»; among other Arabs dhāt ash-shuʿbatayn «[the instrument] with two branches», with which the two movable staves or rules can be compared. Barhebraeus, Astronomia p. 205 of the text, renders the Arabic name in Syriac ōrganōn trayānāy peshḥā «instrument with two forked branches» (less correctly «instrument à deux fentes» in Nau's French translation, p. 180).
