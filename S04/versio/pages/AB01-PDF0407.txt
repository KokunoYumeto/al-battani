% Commentary on chapter 56 a) p. 137, l. 12-19: to compute the azimuth of the qiblah. Let A be the given city (longitude λ′, latitude φ′), B Mekkah (longitude λ, latitude φ), BC the arc of the terrestrial meridian through Mekkah, and AB the arc of the great circle joining the two cities, that is, measuring their distance. Let the arc AC of a great circle be drawn perpendicular to the meridian BC, so that it represents the line of east and west, from which the Arabs begin to count the azimuth. The angle BAC is then the azimuth of the qiblah sought. Al-Battānī puts: (α) sin azimuth = R sin (φ′ − φ) / sin distance, which is only approximately true, since BC cannot be exactly the difference of the latitudes φ′ − φ. He further teaches: (β) sin distance = √(sin² (φ′ − φ) + sin² (λ − λ′)), which is false in a spherical figure. The true formula for computing the azimuth of the qiblah would have been: cos azimuth = cos BAC = R sin AC / sin AB = R sin (λ − λ′) cos φ′ / sin distance. The distance should have been found by the same rule by which al-Battānī in chapter XXVI teaches to compute the distance between two stars of different latitude; this rule, crd distance = √(crd (λ − λ′) cos φ / R × crd (λ − λ′) cos φ′ / R + crd² (φ′ − φ)), reduces very easily, as I showed on pp. 202-3, to our cos distance = sin φ sin φ′ + cos φ cos φ′ cos (λ − λ′). Hardly anyone will believe that al-Battānī taught false formulas, when he himself had already used true ones in the other similar problems. But why he did so is not hard to understand. Knowledge of the azimuth of the qiblah was needed only by architects who had to build the temples of the Muslims; for in the inner wall of the temple a niche is placed, called miḥrāb, which shows the direction of the city of Mekkah, towards which those praying must always turn their face, by the precept of the Qur'an. Since an error of a few degrees is then of very little moment, and since those architects were mostly not much versed in trigonometric computations, al-Battānī wished to give them formulas only approximate, but much easier than the true ones. When the difference of the longitudes is small, and the latitude of the given city not so great that the arcs of the parallel differ much from the arcs of the great circle, the error is almost insensible; in Syria and Mesopotamia it reaches about 3° or 4°. Moreover, he had already taught the approximate formula (β) on p. 87, l. 8-11 to compute the distance between the Sun and the Moon «approximately», but warning that the «true» solution of the problem was the one he had set out in chapter XXVI. For the use of Muslims who must travel through desert regions, so that they know in which direction the face is to be turned in the canonical prayers, circles divided into some sectors are commonly drawn (continued).

@obs body L16-L17: the minus sign (9.5 pt) is printed as a dash and set as a minus sign. The first member of the «Vera formula» is not exact: in the triangle ACB, right-angled at C, cos BAC = cos BC · sin AC : sin AB (= R tan AC : tan AB), while R sin AC : sin AB is sin ABC, the sine of the angle at Mekkah. With the sines to the radius R, as in (α), sin AC = sin (λ − λ′) cos φ′ : R, so the last member is R times the middle one and should read sin (λ − λ′) cos φ′ : sin distantiae; it too gives sin ABC. Further, AC, perpendicular to the meridian of Mekkah, is not the east-west line of the city; the angle of the qiblah with that line has the cosine sin (λ − λ′) cos φ : sin distantiae, with the latitude φ of Mekkah in place of φ′. In the example BAC = 72.55°, while R sin AC : sin AB gives 72.15°; AC makes 87.49° with the meridian of the city, the qiblah makes 70.04° with its east-west line, and (α) with the distance of (β) gives 68.84°, 3.7° less than BAC, as L33 says (3° or 4°).
