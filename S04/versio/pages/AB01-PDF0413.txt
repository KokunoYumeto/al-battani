% On appendix D. a) p. 147: [No one will ever believe this whole chapter, a monument of crass ignorance, to be al-Battānī's work, especially since it conflicts with the true precepts of chapter XLVI (p. 114). The interpolator foolishly interpreted the meaning and use of the tables of the stations of the planets, which are in the Almagest XII, 7 (ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 354-55) and, with a small change in the numbers of Mars, in al-Battānī, vol. II, pp. 138-39. The basis of the tables is this: since the centre of the epicycle moves on the circumference of the eccentric circle with respect to the Earth, the ratio of the radius of the epicycle to its geocentric distance varies in the various places of the eccentric; it is greatest at the perigee, least at the apogee. So the places and times of the stations cannot be computed on one and the same numerical basis in the different places of the eccentric, but for each value of the longitude on the eccentric particular elements must be taken; the stations correspond to points of the epicycle different from each other and to different values of the anomaly. These values Ptolemy gives in tables for all positions of the centre of the epicycle on the eccentric. The interpolator, who perhaps did not know all this, interpreted the tables absurdly. Ptolemy says that Saturn, when the centre of the epicycle holds the apogee of the eccentric, makes its first station at 112° 45′ of anomaly, the second at 247° 15′; but when the centre of the epicycle is at the perigee, the first at 115° 29′, the second at 244° 31′. Dividing these numbers by the daily motion of the anomaly, one finds the numbers of days that pass from the time of the apogee on the epicycle to the time of the first and second station. Our interpolator, on the contrary, says that Saturn is direct from the apogee of the epicycle to the anomaly 112° 45′, whatever the position of the planet in the zodiac; then from the anomaly 112° 45′ to 115° 29′ it stays; thence it goes back to the anomaly 244° 31′; again it stays to the anomaly 247° 15′; at last it goes forward with direct motion to 360°, that is, until it returns to the apogee of the epicycle. Nor did he notice how many absurdities arise from this interpretation, especially in Mars, to which he dares to give a station of 25 1/3 days! — Schiaparelli.] That the chapter is not genuine appears also from the use of the word al-Kātib to designate Mercury; cf. what I said on p. 291, in note d on chapter L. I could emend the numbers only according to the mind of the foolish interpolator. So I derived the degrees of anomaly from the table of stations, vol. II, pp. 138-39, and dividing them by the mean daily motion of the anomaly of each planet I found the numbers of days. On appendix E. a) p. 148: he explains the use of the tables vol. II, pp. 187-88, as he had promised to do in chapter LIII, p. 129, l. 9-11.
