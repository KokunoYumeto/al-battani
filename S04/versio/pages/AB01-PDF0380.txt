% Commentary on chapter 50 d) concluded (Schiaparelli): the 39° 48' recur as a constant apparent diameter of the Sun (see note c). The diameters of the planets at the greatest, mean and least distances should increase from one to the next; here the mean diameter is nearly always smaller than the others, and for Venus greater. The interpolator meant to give apparent diameters, as his explanations and the numbers of the Moon show; these (but for the obvious error at the mean distance) are probably the apparent diameters of the Moon after some astronomer other than al-Battānī. From al-Battānī's principles in the earlier part of the chapter, with his greatest, mean and least distances and the diameters at the mean distances in fractions of the Sun's mean diameter 32' 30", Schiaparelli finds the apparent diameters (table: apogee, mean, perigee: Moon 29' 30", 32' 25", 35' 20"; Mercury 1' 30", 2' 10", 3' 54"; Venus 1' 53", 3' 15", 12' 6"; Sun 31' 20", 32' 30", 33' 40"; Mars 0' 56", 1' 37", 6' 30"; Jupiter 2' 12", 2' 42", 3' 32"; Saturn 1' 33", 1' 48", 2' 10"), very far from the numbers of the codex and of Plato. Nallino: a further sign of the interpolation of l. 20-30: here alone in the whole work Saturn is called not Zuḥal but al-Muqātil, «the fighter», and Mercury not ‘Uṭārid but al-Kātib, «the scribe», a name found otherwise only in appendix D (p. 221, l. 15 of the text, p. 147, l. 30 of the translation), which will be shown spurious. Both names are unknown to the Arabic astronomers between the Nile and the Ganges and to the lexica, but known to the Arabs of north-western Africa and Spain: Dozy, Supplément II, 308 and 442, took them from a twelfth-century Latin-Arabic glossary of southern Spain (note 1: Seybold's edition, Berlin 1900); African writers on astronomy, al-Akhḍarī and al-Wānsharīshī, give examples (note 2: Saḥnūn al-Wānsharīshī's commentary on al-Akhḍarī's poem, Cairo 1314 H.; al-Akhḍarī finished his astronomical poem at twenty, in Dhū 'l-qa‘dah 939 H., June 1533, in the province of Algiers); and the Latin codex Paris 16208, of the twelfth or thirteenth century, has a version made in Spain of an astrological booklet of the Hispano-Arab astronomer az-Zarqālī (note 3: the title, «Liber Arçhachelis introductorius ad librum iudiciorum Arabum»), which begins «Almicatil i. Saturnus». So the user of these names was a western Arab, and the copyist of the archetype of the Escorial codex was western too, as the additions at the end of the geographical tables and the inscriptions of the tables of ascensions show (continued).
