@p 0023b004..0023b011
Nothing certain is known of al-Battānī's boyhood or of his domestic affairs. I do not doubt that he was born before the year 244 H (beginning 18 Apr. 858), since he had already begun to observe in the year 264 and three years later had completed some of his chief observations. His father seems to have been the Jābir ibn Sinān al-Ḥarrānī[^23.2] who is mentioned by al-Nadīm, p. 284 (Suter's translation p. 42), among the famous makers of astronomical instruments[^23.3]. It is therefore no wonder that al-Battānī, perhaps taught the rudiments of the science by his father himself, not only attained the greatest excellence in observing the stars but also devised a new and very convenient armillary sphere.
@p 0023b012..0023b020 + 0024b001..0024b008
Ḥarrān[^23.4], *Ḥarrān* in the Assyro-Babylonian cuneiform inscriptions, *Ḥārān* in the Old Testament and in the books of the Syrians, called *Carrhae* (Κάρραι) by the Romans, was once a very famous city, from the most ancient times the chief seat of the cult of the god *Sīn*, that is, the Moon[^23.5]; it stubbornly resisted the Christian religion, and for this reason it was often called by the Syrians *mdītta d-ḥanpāyē* or *d-ḥanpē*, «the city of the pagans». Full of Greek learning and abounding in very learned men and in the most liberal studies, it kept the cult of the pagan gods even in the first four centuries after the hijra[^23.6], while its learned men professed metaphysical doctrines in which Oriental and Greek elements were mixed together, as among the Gnostics and the Alexandrian philosophers[^23.7]; the people of Ḥarrān, however, from the year 215 H, AD 830, so that they might profess their religion freely on paying only the yearly tribute (*jizya*) that Muslim law imposes on the «people of the book»[^24.1], declared by a most audacious fraud that they were *Sabians*, not pagans. For the Sabians (*Ṣābiʾ*, pl. *Ṣābiʾūn*), who are counted in the Qurʾān among the «people of the book», differed entirely from the people of Ḥarrān[^24.2]: they were Elkesaites and Mandaeans, both sects of Gnostics who lived mainly in Babylonia and had taken over many pagan, Jewish, Christian and Persian rites together[^24.3]. That even the oldest commentators of the Qurʾān did not know what these Sabians really were is very clear from al-Ṭabarī, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān fī tafsīr al-Qurʾān*, Cairo (al-Maymaniyya press), 1319 H ff., vol. I, pp. 242–243; which made it less difficult for the people of Ḥarrān to lead the caliph himself into error.
@p 0024b009..0024b012 + 0025b001..0025b009
Because among the names given to al-Battānī there is also the epithet Sabian (*al-Ṣābiʾ*), some, both Arabs and our own scholars[^24.4], have thought him an adherent of the sect of the *Sabians*, although, as we have seen, it had not escaped Ibn Khallikān and Abū l-Fidāʾ that this could hardly be reconciled with the name Muḥammad, which is used only by Muslims[^24.5]. The difficulty is resolved if we consider that relative names (*nisba*), as I noted above, are sometimes passed on from father to son[^25.1]; al-Battānī, then, could have been called a Sabian not from his own religion but from that of his father and forefathers. There is no lack of examples of men who, though born Muslims, took for this reason the name *al-Naṣrānī* «the Christian», *al-Majūsī* «the Zoroastrian», *al-Isrāʾīlī* «the Israelite». Likewise, in the sixth century H, mention is made of a qāḍī, or Muslim judge, Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Bakhtiyār ibn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm ibn Jaʿfar al-Mandāʾī[^25.2], whose forefathers had already left the religion of the Mandaeans in the fourth century. Lastly, it is not impossible that the name Sabian was given him by later writers for no other reason than that he came from Ḥarrān, whose learned men had for the most part been Sabians until the end of the fourth century of the hijra.
@p 0025b010..0025b015
Someone may perhaps suspect that al-Battānī embraced the Islamic faith late, and so took the name Muḥammad in place of the name given him by his parents. But in that age, as far as we can judge from very many examples, those who embraced Islam did not change their name unless it was wholly repugnant to the Arabic language or to the Muslim religion. That certainly did not happen with the proper names of the people of Ḥarrān; therefore neither Thābit ibn Qurra, nor his son Sinān, nor other Sabians who became Muslims at an advanced age changed their names.
@p 0025b016..0025b018
However that may be, the most certain testimony to al-Battānī's Muslim faith is the preface of the astronomical work itself, in which many verses of the Qurʾān are quoted, and the Muhammadan phrases scattered throughout the book, especially at the end of every chapter.
@p 0025b019..0025b026
It is known that in the third century of the hijra, the eighth AD[^n], several people of Ḥarrān settled in the city of *al-Raqqa*, and that many of them kept the Sabian religion. Al-Raqqa[^25.3] was at that time a most flourishing city on the left bank of the Euphrates, to which three other towns were so close — *al-Raqqa al-sawdāʾ*, that is, «the black» (on the right bank of the Euphrates), *Wāsiṭ al-Raqqa* and *al-Rāfiqa* — that the aggregate of these four cities was very often designated by the name al-Raqqa. It is mentioned hundreds of times by the historical writers of the eighth, ninth and tenth centuries AD; the caliph Hārūn al-Rashīd (AD 786–809) had built himself a palace there, so as to be able to stay in the city more often, and Chesney saw its ruins in 1836[^25.4].
