VII. It remains for me to say briefly *how I have edited al-Battānī's work and translated it into Latin*.
@p 0071b028..0071b038 + 0072b001..0072b002
In editing the Arabic text I have brought the Maghribī spelling of the codex, which departs not a little from the author's (who most certainly used the *naskhī* script), back to the true rules of orthography; I have therefore removed all the peculiar usages listed above. I have often added the vowels and other auxiliary signs, to make the interpretation easier; I have also tacitly corrected the errors in the agreement of the numerals with the gender of the nouns, since there was no reason to attribute them to the author himself. Everything else in which I thought I must depart from the authority of the codex I have always indicated in the notes[^71.3]. It is well known that some letters have one numerical value in the *naskhī* script, which al-Battānī used, and another in the Maghribī script in which the codex is written. In the text of the chapters, in which few numbers expressed by such letters occur, I have always changed the usage of the codex to the author's; not so, however, in the tables that I chose to print in Arabic too (vol. III, pp. 228–79): for, since I had decided to correct their numbers only in the translation, had I changed the numerical usage of the codex the reader would not have had a faithful image of the Escorial manuscript, nor would the causes of the corruptions have readily appeared.
@p 0072b003..0072b020
In my version I have followed the Arabic text closely, as far as could be done without excessive obscurity arising. What I thought should be added, either because it had plainly dropped out of the codex or because it helped to explain the sense, I have always enclosed in square brackets [ ]. Sometimes, however, but only in setting out *mathematical formulas*, I judged that things should be told in fewer words, so as not to weary the reader excessively. For, to give an example of the most compressed passages, had I translated those formulas too word for word, the last three lines of p. 13 and the first of p. 14 would have run thus: «If you wish to know the ascensions of any degree of the signs in the right sphere, take the whole declination, namely 23° 35′, and find its sine, that is, the sine of the whole declination; then subtract the whole declination from 90° and find the sine of what remains, which will be the sine of the complement of the whole declination. Then take [the interval] from the beginning of Aries to the degree that you wish, and find the declination of that degree; find the sine of what it is, which will be the sine of the declination of the degree. Then subtract the declination of the degree from 90, and find the sine of what remains, which will be the sine of the complement of the declination of the degree. Then multiply the sine of the declination of the degree by the sine of the complement of the whole declination; divide what results by the sine of the whole declination, and multiply what comes out by half the diameter, that is, by 60; divide what results by the sine of the complement of the declination of the degree. Find the arc of what comes out; what the arc is will be the amount of what rises in the right sphere from the beginning of Aries to the degree that you wanted».
@p 0072b021..0072b026
Except in very few places, and those small ones, no help, as I have said, could be taken from the printed version of Plato, which is far more corrupt than the Escorial text and derives from the same archetype. I was therefore unwilling to furnish the version or the text with its most obvious errors by way of a critical apparatus. Plato's numbers, however, I have always reported in the notes, because Halley, Delambre and others had used those numbers in their dissertations; nor have I ever passed over the places in which Plato's version could bring any profit.
@p 0072b027..0072b031
In my notes some things will be superfluous to some readers, others to others. I preferred to say too much rather than too little, knowing that this book will perhaps be read by men devoted to studies very different from one another, and that things most familiar to an astronomer are extremely hard and difficult to understand for those who give themselves to Arabic letters. So that my formulas should not be confused with the author's, I have always marked al-Battānī's formulas with Greek letters, except on p. 219, where space was lacking.

It is now a pleasant duty to give public thanks to those who have favoured my work: namely to the Augustinian Fathers in charge of the Escorial Library, who in the autumn of 1894, with the greatest liberality, made easier for me the task of partly copying and partly photographing the codex; likewise to Father Juan Lazcano, carried off by an untimely death on 17 December 1899, who always most willingly collated with the codex again everything that, after I had left the Escorial, seemed doubtful to me in my copy.
@p 0073b013..0073b028
But above all I must, with my whole heart, give the greatest thanks again and again to the most distinguished brothers Celestino and Giovanni Schiaparelli. For the former, Professor of Arabic letters in the University of Rome, a man in whom uprightness of mind, the greatest modesty and the widest learning admirably vie with one another, chose to propose me, known to him only through a few writings, as translator and editor of al-Battānī's work. The latter, gladly complying with his brother's wish, not only most kindly provided the means for me to go to Spain and to have the book printed at the expense of the *Observatory of Milan*, of which he was the director, but also showed himself ready to resolve the doubts in which I had been caught in composing my work. For, trained in philological studies, I should not have dared to undertake that work had I not known that I could enjoy the help of so great an astronomer. Indeed, that man, the glory of Italy, with the great kindness that is his, read through my version and the commentary written by me attentively, and added other things to them, which, enclosed in heavy brackets [ ] and marked with his name, are the finest ornament of this book. He also undertook the most tedious task of examining once more the numerical tables, first corrected by me, as I shall say better in vol. II, although he had to attend to many and far weightier affairs. If, then, scholars gain any profit from al-Battānī brought to light again, let them know that it is owed above all to those two eminent brothers.
