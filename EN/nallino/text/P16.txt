That al-Battānī did this because he envied the astronomers of his own time I think hardly credible, since he was a man of such modesty that he not only did not proclaim his merits but hid them almost entirely, and avoided occasions of speaking of himself. I think it was rather from an excessive reverence for Ptolemy, compared with whom he did not judge the observers of his age worth mentioning. Nor perhaps did al-Battānī lack a particular reason for praising Ptolemy beyond measure. For in the second, third and fourth centuries after the hijra many Muslims of different nations, in order to weaken the supreme authority of the genuine Arabs in public affairs, had begun to extol to the skies the glory of the ancient nations, and to set the barbarism of the wandering Arabs against it. They were called *Shuʿūbī*, pl. *shuʿūbiyya*[^52.5]. Those who were of Persian stock argued that the sciences, literature, institutions, customs and history of the Persians had far surpassed those of the Arabs even in the most ancient time; and they tried to disparage the Arabic language itself in every way, with arguments not rarely foolish. Others, born of the Coptic nation, boastfully celebrated the monuments of the ancient Egyptians and with invented fables composed wonderful annals of the ancient kings[^52.6]; others, lastly, born especially in Mesopotamia and belonging to the Aramaean people, fraudulently proclaimed in pseudepigraphic books the greatness of the Chaldaeans, or, as they also said, of the Nabataeans. Among these was that Abū Ṭālib Aḥmad ibn al-Zayyāt, *perhaps not unknown to al-Battānī*, whom I mentioned above, p. xviii (and n. 2). It is no wonder that the learned men of *Ḥarrān* too, who had vigorously defended the sciences and the philosophy of the Greeks against the Christian and the Muslim doctrines, unjustly despised the new men, so that the glory of Hellenism might shine the brighter by contempt for them[^53.1]. Nor should it be passed over that particular traditions on *astrology* also flourished among the people of Ḥarrān; for in the astrological collections of an unknown Byzantine author, in which the Arabs Ἀπουμάσαρ (Abū Maʿshar) and Μασάλα [Mā shāʾ Allāh] are also quoted, a short chapter CCCLXXXIII, excerpted from Abū Maʿshar, is entitled περὶ τῆς μεθόδου τῶν Χαρανιτῶν ἤτοι ἐναλλαγῆς τοῦ κοσμικοῦ ἔτους, «on the method of the *people of Ḥarrān*, or on the revolution of the year of the world»[^53.2]; and among the forbidden astrological books kept in the Library of the imperial palace of Constantinople is counted καὶ ἡ βίβλος τοῦ Χαρανιώτ[ου], ἥτις εὑρίσκεται παρ' Ἑβραίοις, «and the book of the *Ḥarrānian*, which is found among the Hebrews», perhaps, as the editor of the catalogue notes, the same *ktābā d-ḥārānāyē*, «book of the people of Ḥarrān», that is mentioned by Syriac writers[^53.3].

There is no one who does not see at once that there is a great difference between the Almagest and al-Battānī's work. The former seeks to comprise the whole astronomy of the ancients, so that one who wishes to learn about the heavens should need no other books; which it would achieve if many things said obscurely, and most tedious demonstrations always very inopportunely mixed with numerical examples, did not deter the reader rather than help him. Al-Battānī's book, on the contrary, like all those that the Arabs call *zīj*, was not written for the use of beginners too[^53.4], but for those who already know the elements of cosmography well and wish to give themselves to celestial observations and computations. Therefore it mostly leaves out the definitions; it gives the rules of computation almost everywhere without demonstrations; it often also neglects to teach how the tables are constructed, so that the chapters of the book are for the most part nothing but an account of the use of the tables. Al-Battānī, however, did not manage to pursue this purpose of his in an even way; and so it came about that his work does not appear polished and perfect in every part. Such unevenness sometimes arises for just reasons; for, to give a few examples, in chapters XII and XXVI he probably demonstrates formulas geometrically, contrary to his custom, because he himself had been the first to find those rules; and in chapters XXVIII and XXX he sets out Ptolemy's constructions at length to show in what way he had changed the Ptolemaic numbers. But elsewhere he gives notions superfluous for a man already instructed in the rudiments; and elsewhere, from an excessive zeal for brevity, he leaves out things that would not have been useless, such as the theory of the five planets, which is very intricate in Ptolemy. Al-Battānī's rules and tables are indeed quite sufficient for computing the motions of the five planets; but in setting out the theory of their motions it lacks a balanced proportion with the doctrine of the Sun and the Moon[^53.5].
@p 0053b035 + 0054b001..0054b007
In this too he departs from the *Almagest*, that, like all the other Arab astronomers, al-Battānī also sets out problems of spherical astronomy that serve only for astrological computations, which Ptolemy and Theon always leave out: so chapter XXXV, on setting up the celestial houses, and LIII–LV, on computing the revolutions of the years, the radiations of the planets and the ascensions between the pegs. I would certainly not count this a fault in the Arabs; for in this way they fostered the study of mathematical and celestial matters, which seems to have been quite small among the Greek astrologers, though these perhaps founded the art of divination on fixed philosophical principles much more than the Arabs.

Al-Battānī's Arabic style, at first sight very easy to understand, not rarely appears extremely obscure and intricate when it is looked at more closely. The sense is sometimes made out only with difficulty, as I have shown more than once in the notes; the circuit of the words, or *period*, as the Greeks say, becomes too long and intricate in some places, and the lack of short vowels and of punctuation in the Arabic codices makes it still more obscure. It is enough to mention here a great part of the preface and the beginning of chapter LV. Ḥabash, al-Battānī's contemporary, is much clearer. Some obscurity may arise from certain technical terms not always used in one and the same sense, as I noted of the word *ikhtilāf* in the doctrine of the Sun (note, p. 213) and of *mora* in the eclipses (note, p. 275); words which, for the rest, the Greeks too already often used confusedly. On p. 56, l. 9, the mean distance is called *al-buʿd al-awsaṭ*, which also designates the mean apogee of the epicycle. If the demonstration of chapter XXVI (the interpolations removed) had been set out in a more exact wording, it would undoubtedly not have been censured by Regiomontanus and Delambre as false and wholly unworthy of a mathematician.

Nor can some errors be passed over in silence. He reduces Ptolemy's distance of the Sun from the Earth incorrectly, relying on better observations than those of the ancients but inopportunely following the Greek method of computation (ch. XXX); he removes Ptolemy's error in the correction of the angles by which the parallaxes are determined in part, not altogether, an error which, however, was of no weight at that time (p. 82, and the notes, pp. 259–62); on p. 104 (and 105, 110, note p. 278) he draws some false conclusions from good premises; he does not always explain the lunar mansions of the Arabs correctly (p. 125, notes pp. 295–296); in the increment of the longitudes of the fixed stars over the catalogue of the Almagest he falls into a small slip, of no moment at all for his purpose (p. 124, notes pp. 293–94); he also says other things not well enough on p. 58, ll. 16–18 (n. p. 235), p. 78, ll. 25–26 (n. 254), and pp. 145–46 (n. 323). It grieves us most that the eminent astronomer acquiesced in what had been wrongly handed down by the old Greek-Syrian geographers, and did not refute their very serious error of geometry, p. 17 (notes 165–66).

@p 0054b035..0054b040 + 0055b001..0055b007
The author testifies that he gave constant care to celestial observations, and that he measured the courses of the Sun, the Moon and the five planets, and also the places of certain fixed stars, with the greatest diligence over many years[^54.1]; and that he knew of no error that he had not emended (p. 66). That he made his fundamental observations in the city of al-Raqqa I have said on p. xvii of this preface. He mentions here and there the instruments that he used. The *astrolabe* occurs on p. 91, ll. 4–5 and 40, in a problem that does not require a very exact measurement; on the same p. 91 the use of *tubes* is recommended. For exact observations there serve a very tall *gnomon*, divided into twelve parts (pp. 22–23); a most ingenious *celestial globe*, devised, it seems, by al-Battānī himself, furnished with five armillae (pp. 138–42); the *triquetrum*, or *parallactic rules*, of such length that the measurements taken with them come from a circle whose diameter comprises 10 cubits, or about 5 metres[^55.1]; a *mural quadrant* whose side is not less than a metre[^55.2]; and lastly the *sciothericum*, or *sundial*, both horizontal and vertical, for measuring the temporal hours (pp. 135–38). He notes that the hours are also found with other instruments, but not so exactly as with the sciothericum (p. 138, ll. 31–32).
@p 0055b008..0055b031
What the duties of a good astronomer are, and with what method, diligence and perseverance observations are to be made, al-Battānī teaches on pp. 5, 127, 139, observing very well that in celestial matters the truth is not reached except by the constant labours of many generations of men. He therefore always most prudently refrains from asserting things that are not established with sufficient certainty; nor does he put so much faith in the old observations as to venture to conjecture new theories from comparing them with the more recent ones. He found the obliquity of the ecliptic smaller than the Ptolemaic and the yearly precession greater, and he uses the newly found values, but he never asserts that those variations answer to the truth and are continuous; therefore he rightly will not use the doctrine of the trepidation of the fixed stars to save the discrepancies that could have arisen from the inaccurate observations of the ancients. He thought it better to profess his ignorance candidly than to infer something fictitious from uncertain observations. Philosophical inquiries on the system of the world, on the nature of the celestial spheres and the like he leaves out entirely, as though he did not think that they belong to astronomy. But how far al-Battānī surpassed Ptolemy in making exact observations is shown most certainly by the elements that he established, such as the obliquity of the ecliptic, the apogees of the Sun and the five planets, the length of the tropical year[^55.3], the measurements of the equinoxes, the solstices and the eclipses, and others worthy of the highest praise and perhaps not useless even to the astronomers of today. Likewise the doctrine of the visibility of the new Moon, which al-Battānī was the first to establish, and the very wise use of approximations in certain problems (though at first sight they depart from mathematical truth)[^55.4], show him to be a man most expert in astronomical operations. The author knows very well how much perfection is to be expected of the instruments, and therefore how much deception lies in certain arithmetical computations carried out with the greatest exactness; and so he gives the celestial motions in the tables only to minutes or to seconds, as was necessary for the observers of that time, and does not fall into Ptolemy's ridiculous error of giving the motions of the planets even in sixths. That he not rarely carried out the computations more precisely and afterwards rounded the numbers is plain from an inspection of the tables and from the example given on p. 234, n. *l*.
