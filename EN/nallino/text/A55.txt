@p 0402b006..0402b015
The Arabs call *pegs*, as I have said more fully on p. 248, the four points of the ecliptic that are determined by the intersection of the horizon and the meridian: namely the ascendant, the midheaven, the descendant or setting, and the lower midheaven or peg of the earth. The arc of the equator, counted from the beginning of Aries, that passes through the meridian together with a given star is the star's right ascension; the arc of the equator that rises or sets together with a given star is the star's oblique ascension or descension, or its ascension or descension in the clime. The author has already dealt with these arcs in the preceding chapters. It remains for us to know the arcs of the equator that rise together with a given star at any other place of the sphere, that is, the arc of the equator, or the hour angle, that a planet or a given degree of the ecliptic must run through to pass the circle of position[^402.1] of another planet or degree[^402.2].

@p 0402b019..0402b029
That the reason for this computation may be better understood, I shall set out some definitions. Comparing the life of men and of nations to a horse let out from the gate of the starting-pen towards the turning-post of the circus, the ancient astrologers said that life is let go (ἀφίεσθαι), more or less strongly, from some point of the zodiac, which was called τόπος ἀφετικός, «the place letting go», the *aphetic*, *prorogative* or *hylegiacal place*; that the aphetic places are the midheaven, the ascendant, the beginning of the eleventh house (ἀγαϑοδαίμων), the beginning of the seventh, or descendant, and the beginning of the ninth (ϑεός). Life let go from the aphetic place perishes, or at least is in danger, when it has come on the ecliptic to some obstacle, which was called τόπος ἀναιρετικός, «the killing, destroying place»; the degrees of the equator that answer to the interval between the aphetic place and the anaeretic place are to be converted, according to different methods, into years, which count the years of life from the birth to the unlucky event or to death.
@p 0402b030..0402b033 + 0403b001..0403b010
The star which, holding the aphetic place at the time of the birth, must be taken as the ruler and governor of life, is called by the Greeks ἀφέτης, «the one letting go», or κύριος τῆς ζωῆς, «lord of life»; by the Arabs *dalīl* (plural *dalāʾil*, *adillāʾ*, *adilla*), «the one pointing out, indicating», or, with a Persian word, *haylāj* (plural *hayālij*); by the Latins of the Middle Ages and later times *apheta, significator, prorogator, vitae gubernator, liberator, dispositor, hileg, hylech*. The aphetae are five: the sun, the moon, the ascendant, the planet that has the greatest power (οἰκοδεσποτεία) over the degree of the Part of Fortune[^403.1], and the planet that has the greatest power over the degree of the last conjunction or opposition of the sun and the moon. An apheta has no force by itself unless it is found in an aphetic place. Which of the aphetae is to be taken in the different cases, according to Ptolemy's doctrine, may be read in V. Nabod, *Enarratio elementorum astrologiae*, Cologne 1560, pp. 383–84, 388–90, and in Bouché-Leclercq, pp. 415–19. — Lastly, the star whose body or hostile rays, meeting the significator in the profections, cut off life, or at least foretell that great dangers threaten it, was called by the Greeks ἀναιρέτης, «the killer», or μοῖρα ἀναιρετική, «the killing, destroying degree», and by the Latins of the fifteenth, sixteenth and seventeenth centuries *promissor*.
@f 403.1 0403nl001..0403nl004
Ὁ κλῆρος τῆς τύχης, in Arabic *sahm al-saʿāda*, is the place of the ecliptic that is as far from the ascendant, in the succession of the signs, as the interval of the zodiac between the sun and the moon.
@p 0403b011..0403b021
*Direction*, or *prorogation*, in Greek ἄφεσις, in Arabic *tasyīr* (whence *alazir, alaçir, alhacir* in our writers of the Middle Ages), is the arc of the equator that rises or sets together with the arc of the ecliptic lying between the significator and the promissor, with respect to the circle of position on which one of them lies. The astrologers distinguish two directions; the one is called *direct direction*, or in the succession of the signs (ἄφεσις εἰς τὰ ἑπόμενα τῶν ζωδίων, from west to east), in which the promissor (ἀναιρέτης), or «following place» (ἑπόμενος τόπος), is thought to be carried to the «preceding place» (προηγούμενος τόπος), that is, to the circle of position of the significator or apheta. The other is called *converse direction*, or against the succession of the signs (ἄφεσις εἰς τὰ προηγούμενα τῶν ζωδίων), in which the significator is thought to be carried to the circle of the promissor. In both, when the nativities of men are concerned, each degree of the equator signifies a year; in the revolutions of the years of the world and of the nativities a degree signifies a day, and so on.
@p 0403b022..0403b029
*Profection* is the uniform motion of the significator in the succession of the signs. In the *annual* profection of a nativity one sign of the zodiac is given to each solar year; so if the given nativity or geniture has the sign of Aries in the ascendant, the second year will have the sign of Taurus, the third the sign of Gemini, and so on. In the profection that they call *monthly* the solar year is divided into 13 equal parts, or profectional months, to each of which one sign is given; so the sign of the annual profection is the sign of the first month of the same year. In the *daily* profection, again, the profectional month is subdivided into 13 equal parts, to each of which one sign is given.
@p 0403b030..0403b035
The doctrine of direction, as far as its astrological use is concerned, is extremely obscure and intricate; besides, different astrologers rely on different principles in drawing forecasts, whence arises the greatest confusion, which Cl. Salmasius himself, *De annis climactericis et antiqua astrologia diatribae*, Leiden 1648, pp. 264–398, 407–439, could not avoid. Bouché-Leclercq, pp. 411–419[^403.2], may be consulted, who, however, as always, says nothing or little of value on the mathematical computations.

@p 0403b037..0403b038 + 0404b001..0404b003
*The computation of the ascensions of the signs outside the four pegs* is the computation of the *direction of the significator* to the promissor and of the promissor to the significator. — So Henry Bate (see p. 308, note 5), who had read al-Battānī's work attentively, rightly says, f. d2r: «Now the science of the ascensions of the signs between the quarters of the sky is the same as the science of the directions: so that, when the one is known, the other is known too».
@p 0404b004..0404b013
Al-Battānī treated these ascensions in the spaces between the pegs in a special little book, as I have said sufficiently in the preface. The reason for publishing it was doubtless the ignorance of the astrologers, who, little versed in mathematical matters, erred badly in the computations of the directions. For some perhaps thought that the direction is the difference of the right ascensions of the significator and the promissor, which is true only when the degree to which the direction is made occupies the upper meridian (the peg of the midheaven) or the lower (the peg of the earth)[^404.1]; some took the difference of the oblique ascensions or descensions, which, however, is not true unless the degree to which the direction is made stands in the ascendant or the descendant (setting)[^404.2]; others, finally, like Ptolemy himself, though they mixed right and oblique ascensions well, neglected the latitude of the planets.
@f 404.1 0404nl001..0404nl002
For then the circle of position of the degree to which the direction is made is the meridian itself.
@f 404.2 0404nl003..0404nl004
For the circle of position to which the planet is directed is then the horizon itself.
@p 0404b014..0404b017 + 0404b018..0404b021
Let A be the «earlier place», namely the significator planet or apheta, to which, in the direct direction, the «following place» B (namely the promissor or anaereta) must be directed in the succession of the signs. Let both stand outside the pegs; for if B holds one of the pegs, the problem is reduced to the difference of right or oblique ascensions, as I have just said. — Let NAS be the circle of position drawn through A, P the pole of the equator; we must find how many degrees the arc *bc* of the equator contains, which B runs through to reach C.

Al-Battānī indicates two methods of computing *bc*, of which I shall give the proof here.
@p 0404b024..0404b032
I. — Let EE′, FF′ be the arcs of the parallels that B and A run through in the daily motion of the sphere from the horizon to the meridian; let circles of declination be drawn through E and F as far as the equator, produced; the arc Dβ of the equator will be the semidiurnal arc of the planet B, the arc Dα the semidiurnal arc of the planet A. Since P*b*, P*c*, P*a* are circles of declination, the arc of the direction sought, namely *bc*, will be: \[ bc = (\text{\MoonFont ♈}a - \text{\MoonFont ♈}b) - ca = (\text{RA } A - \text{RA } B) - ca. \]
@p 0404b033..0404b039 + 0405b001..0405b014 + 0405b015..0405b017
If A were on the meridian, the arc *ca* would be reduced to nothing; for the circle of position would become the meridian itself, on which A and C would fall. If on the contrary A occupied the horizon at F, the circle of position would become the horizon itself, on which C too would fall; so the arc *ca* would reach its greatest amount, namely it would become βα, which is the difference between the semidiurnal arc of the planet A and the semidiurnal arc of B. Since the motion of the sphere is supposed uniform, the author thinks that the amount of the arc *ca* grows the more, the farther A is from the meridian; and this is what al-Battānī says on p. 132, ll. 10–36. So, *in his view*, this proportion arises[^404.3]: \[ ca : (\text{semid. arc } A - \text{semid. arc } B) = \text{dist. of } A \text{ from the meridian} : \text{semid. arc } A, \] whence \[ ca = (\text{semid. arc } A - \text{semid. arc } B)\times\frac{\text{distance of } A \text{ from the meridian}}{\text{semidiurnal arc } A}. \] If we express the distance in temporal hours[^405.1], of which the semidiurnal arc contains six, plainly \[ ca = (\text{semid. arc } A - \text{semid. arc } B)\times\frac{\text{hours of distance of } A}{6}. \] From the precepts of chapter XIX it follows that \[ \text{semid. arc } A = 90^\circ + \text{difference between RA } A \text{ and obl. asc. } A, \] \[ \text{semid. arc } B = 90^\circ + \text{difference between RA } B \text{ and obl. asc. } B, \] so[^n] \[ (\text{semid. arc } A - \text{semid. arc } B) = (\text{obl. asc. } A - \text{obl. asc. } B) - (\text{RA } A - \text{RA } B). \] If we denote by the letter α the difference of the right ascensions and by the letter β the difference of the oblique ones, al-Battānī's rule is[^405.2]: \[ [[(α)]] \text{direction} = \alpha \pm (\beta - \alpha)\times\frac{\text{temporal hours of distance of } A}{6}\,, \] where (α − β) is put for (β − α) if α > β; the second term of the second member has the sign − when α > β, the sign + when α < β.
@n The semidiurnal arc is 90° plus the ascensional difference, which in the eastern hemisphere is the right ascension less the oblique ascension; so semid. arc A − semid. arc B = (RA A − RA B) − (obl. asc. A − obl. asc. B) = α − β, and the right side of this identity is printed with the opposite sign. The rule (α) agrees with α − β: it gives α when A is on the meridian, and β when A is on the horizon (six temporal hours), as it must.

If A and B are found in two different hemispheres, one must proceed as al-Battānī teaches, p. 134, ll. 16–20: direct B to the upper or the lower meridian (according as the one or the other lies between B and A), which direction is the difference between the right ascension of B and the right ascension of the midheaven or of the peg of the earth; to this difference add the amount D*c*, namely the temporal hours of distance of A from the same meridian *multiplied by the hour-times of B*[^405.3]; the direction sought will come out.

@p 0406b003..0406b005
II. — Al-Battānī, p. 134, l. 21 ff., indicates *another rule*; that is, if A and B stand in the eastern hemisphere[^n]: \[ [[(β)]] \text{direction} = (\text{RA } B - \text{asc. of the midheaven}) - \text{hours of dist. } A\times\text{hour-times } B. \]
@n This is the rule in al-Battānī's words (p. 134, ll. 24–26 of the translation, where the product is taken from the right ascensions between the midheaven and the following degree); it gives Db − Dc, whereas the proof that follows, made on the figure of p. 315, in which b lies between D and c, gives Dc − Db. Both give the size of the arc bc.
@p 0406b006..0406b015
This can be proved thus: from the former figure it follows *approximately* (see p. 315, note 3) that \[ E'C : F'A = E'E : F'F \qquad\text{or}\qquad Dc : Da = D\beta : D\alpha, \] that is \[ Dc : (\text{RA } A - \text{RA of the midheaven}) = \text{hour-times } B : \text{hour-times } A, \] whence \[ Dc = \frac{\text{RA } A - \text{RA of the midheaven}}{\text{hour-times } A}\times\text{hour-times } B, \] \[ Dc = \text{temporal hours of dist. of } A \text{ from the midheaven}\times\text{hour-times } B. \] But also \[ Dc = bc + Db = \text{direction} + (\text{RA } B - \text{asc. of the midheaven}); \] so, as al-Battānī has it: \[ \text{direction} = \text{hours of dist. } A\times\text{hour-times } B - (\text{RA } B - \text{asc. of the midheaven}). \]

@p 0406b018..0406b034
At the end of the chapter[^406.1] he says that this is Ptolemy's computation. Rightly indeed, for in the *Tetrabiblos* III, 10 (= III, 15 of the Frankfurt version) al-Battānī's *second* method is set out and illustrated by a numerical example. Why one must proceed in this way, Ptolemy explains thus: «But if the preceding place is not on these cardinal points, but falls in the intermediate spaces, it is not the times corresponding to the arcs rising or setting or culminating that will bring the following place to the preceding one, but different ones. For that place is like and the same which has a like position, and in the same degrees, both with respect to the horizon and to the meridian. This happens *approximately* (ἔγγιστα) to the places that fall on one and the same of the semicircles drawn through the common intersections of the horizon and the meridian; each of these semicircles, standing at approximately equal distances [from one another], completes one [temporal] hour. Now the equator is always inclined in the same way to any one of those semicircles, and to the horizon and to the meridian, just as if it turned about those intersections; the degrees of the ecliptic, on the contrary, change their relation to each semicircle, and pass in unequal times on either side. And in the same way, in whatever direction from the cardinal points [i.e. the pegs] the significator is distant, it passes through those semicircles in unequal time. We shall, however, lay down one way by which … the number of equinoctial times that brings the preceding place to the following can be found by proportion».

But Ptolemy (like Alcabitius and others) always considers points of the ecliptic, as if the planets to be directed never had latitude. Al-Battānī, on the contrary, rightly bids us, when the planet deviates from the zodiac, take, instead of the degree of the ecliptic that numbers its longitude, the degree culminating with the planet in the computation of the right ascensions, and the degree rising with the planet in the computation of the oblique ascensions. — Like al-Battānī, so Johannes de Saxonia in the commentary on the seventh chapter of the fourth difference of Alcabitius[^406.2]; who says of his computation for planets having latitude: «and I have not seen [this] way set out in any book» (f. mm1v = 58v), as he had already asserted of the radiations computed in the manner of the direction (see what I set out on p. 311, note 2).
