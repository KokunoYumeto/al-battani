All the propositions on *chords* that the author gives are proved in the *Almagest* I, 9 (ed. Halma, vol. I, pp. 26–37). If we denote the radius by the letter R, they are expressed in our algebraic notation as follows:
@m 0242b007..0242b014
[[(α)]] \text{chord}\,\frac{360^\circ}{6} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{chord}\,60^\circ = R
[[(β)]] \text{chord}\,\frac{360^\circ}{3} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{chord}\,120^\circ = \sqrt{4R^2 - \text{chord}^2\,\frac{360^\circ}{6}}
[[(γ)]] \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - a) = \sqrt{4R^2 - \text{chord}^2\,a}
[[(δ)]] \text{chord}\,\frac{360^\circ}{4} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{chord}\,90^\circ = \sqrt{2R^2}
[[(ε)[^n]]] \text{chord}\,\frac{360^\circ}{10} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{chord}\,36^\circ = \sqrt{R^2 + \Bigl(\frac{2R}{4}\Bigr)^2} - \frac{2R}{4}
[[(ζ)]] \text{chord}\,\frac{360^\circ}{5} \quad\text{or}\quad \text{chord}\,72^\circ = \sqrt{\text{chord}^2\,\frac{360^\circ}{10} + R^2}
[[(η)]] \text{chord}\,(a - b) = \frac{\text{chord}\,a\ \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - b) - \text{chord}\,b\ \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - a)}{2R}
[[(ϑ)]] \text{chord}\,\frac{a}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{2R - \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - a)}{2} \times 2R}
@n In (ε) the print has 2R/4 under the root without the square; the side of the decagon, chord 36° = √(R² + (R/2)²) − R/2, requires the square, which is set here.
@p 0242b015..0242b017 + 0243b001..0243b002
To find the chord of the sum of two arcs, Ptolemy (ed. Halma, vol. I, pp. 32–33), whom our author follows, has nothing like the rule (η), but goes by way of the chord of the supplement[^242.1]; hence
\[ [[(ι)[^n]]] \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - [a + b]) = \frac{\text{chord}\,(180^\circ - a)\ \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - b) - \text{chord}\,a\ \text{chord}\,b}{2R} \]
\[ [[(κ)]] \text{chord}\,(a + b) = \sqrt{4R^2 - \text{chord}^2\,(180^\circ - [a + b])} \]
@n In (ι) the print has the two products of the numerator in the opposite order, which gives the negative of the chord; they are set here in the order that gives the chord.

Almost all the historians of mathematics describe the way by which Ptolemy came to compute the chord of 1°; of the most recent, one may consult M. Cantor, *Vorlesungen über Geschichte der Mathematik*, Leipzig 1880 ff., vol. I, pp. 353–355 (or 390–392 of the second edition, of 1894); R. Wolf, *Handbuch der Astronomie, ihrer Geschichte und Litteratur*, Zürich 1890–93, vol. I, pp. 164–165, § 61*b*; A. von Braunmühl, *Vorlesungen über Geschichte der Trigonometrie*, Leipzig 1900 ff., vol. I, p. 21. The error, which F. Woepcke determined with Lagrange's interpolation formula (*Discussion de deux méthodes arabes pour déterminer une valeur rapprochée de sin 1°*, in the Journal de Mathématiques pures et appliquées published by J. Liouville, vol. XIX, 1854, p. 165), is 0.00000009, that is, less than 1/10,000,000; it therefore becomes noticeable only in the thirds, which Ptolemy leaves out of his table of chords. — A value of the sine of 1° nearer the truth was found by Abū l-Wafāʾ (940–998), whose error shows only in the fifths, that is, in the ninth decimal place (sin 30′ = $0^{p}\,31'\,24''\,55'''\,54^{\mathrm{IV}}\,55^{\mathrm{V}}$ instead of $54^{\mathrm{V}}\,47^{\mathrm{VI}}\,16^{\mathrm{VII}}$); see Woepcke, *Recherches sur l’histoire des sciences mathématiques chez les Orientaux*, in the Journal Asiatique, 5th series, vol. XV, 1860, pp. 286–303; Cantor, I, 641–642 (= 703 of the second edition); von Braunmühl, I, 56–57. — According to what Mīram Čelebī (d. 931 H = 1524–25) has handed down to us in his commentary on Ulugh Beg (in Sédillot's French translation, pp. 69–77, note), other, early Arabs, whose names are not known, carried Ptolemy's method through and found a value of the sine of 1° that exceeds the truth by less than 1/100,000,000; for the error is 37/416 (or about 1/11) of Ptolemy's error, since they make sin 1° = $1^{p}\,2'\,49''\,43'''\,13^{\mathrm{IV}}\,5^{\mathrm{V}}\,30^{\mathrm{VI}}$ instead of $11^{\mathrm{IV}}\,14^{\mathrm{V}}\,44^{\mathrm{VI}}$. But had they not deliberately neglected certain small fractions in their computations, they could have found a far better number, whose error would have been 1/64 of Ptolemy's. See Woepcke, *Discussion de deux méthodes* etc., pp. 155–157, 159–167. — Lastly, Ghiyāth al-Dīn[^243.1] Jamshīd al-Kāshī, in whom other Arabs had probably gone before him, computed sin 1° = $1^{p}\,2'\,49''\,43'''\,11^{\mathrm{IV}}$ correctly and most elegantly, by solving the equation of the trisection of the angle approximately, that is, by expanding the equation of the third degree in a series; his method, handed down to us by Mīram Čelebī, loc. cit., pp. 77–83, was explained by Woepcke, *Discussion*, pp. 167–176, 301–303. See also H. Hankel, *Zur Geschichte der Mathematik im Alterthum und Mittelalter*, Leipzig 1874, pp. 290–293; Cantor, I, 671–672 (= 737 of the second edition); von Braunmühl, I, 73–75.

@p 0243b033..0243b036 + 0244b001..0244b016
The technical word *jayb*, by which the Arabs denote the sine, al-Battānī never mentions, although it was already in common use at the beginning of the ninth century, as appears from the unpublished Latin translation of the astronomical work of Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, made by Adelard of Bath in the twelfth century. For there the Arabic name *elgeib* is kept (with the Arabic article *al* or *el* put before it), and «*elgeib elmustewi* or plane» [*al-jayb al-mustawī*], that is, the right sine, and «*elgeib elmacus* or diminished»[^244.1], that is, the versed sine, are named. Likewise Ḥabash, a contemporary of al-Battānī, always uses the word *jayb*. But our author would not take up the word, which is of foreign origin and wholly alien to Greek usage. Not a few historians of mathematics, then, and among the best, are wrong when they ascribe the use of the word *jayb* to our author and think that Plato of Tivoli brought the word *sinus* to Europe with his translations; of the more recent I shall cite only Hankel, p. 280; Wolf, *Handb. der Astron.*, I, 166, § 62*a*; Cantor, I, 632 (= 693 of the second edition), II, 101 and 249. The old Kaestner, however, had already corrected the error, *Geschichte der Mathematik*, Göttingen 1796, vol. I, p. 522; see now M. Koppe, *Die Behandlung der Logarithmen und der Sinus*, Programm des Andreas-Realgymnasium, Berlin 1893, p. 32; von Braunmühl, I, 50. — The word *sinus* occurs only once in Plato of Tivoli's translation: «si autem per has cordas [*sic*] versas arcus scire desideras, si chorda quam habueris minus 60 fuerit, eam de 60 minue, et residui arcum scito, quem de 90 minues, et quod remanserit, erit arcus *sinus* versi» («but if you wish to know the arcs from these versed chords: if the chord you have is less than 60, take it from 60 and find the arc of the remainder; take that from 90, and what remains will be the arc of the versed *sinus*»; fol. 7v of the Nuremberg edition; the passage corresponds to p. 11, ll. 28–30 of my translation). But, as von Braunmühl, I, 50, note 3, rightly judges, the word must be taken for a gloss of Regiomontanus that has crept into the text by mistake.

@p 0244b017..0244b029
Since chords are hardly ever used in trigonometric computations, it is no wonder that al-Battānī called the halves of the chords of the double arcs by the name of chords; a similar usage is found among the Indians as early as the fourth century, of whom one reads the following in the notes of Burgess and Whitney on the book entitled *Sūrya-siddhānta*: «In this passage, the sine is called *jyārdha*, ‘half-chord’; hereafter, however, that term does not once occur, but *jyā* ‘chord’ (literally ‘bowstring’) is itself employed, as are also its synonyms *jīvā*, *māurvikā*, to denote the sine. The usage of Albategnius is the same» (*Translation of the Sūrya-Siddhānta, a text-book of Hindu astronomy, by* E. Burgess, note on ch. II, verse 28, in the Journal of the American Oriental Society, vol. VI, New Haven 1860, p. 201). — Nor should it be passed over that among the Arabs the name of the whole *chord* too comes from the usage of the Indians, as the passage just quoted shows; among the Greeks the chord was called ὑποτείνουσα, «subtending», ἡ ἐν τῷ κύκλῳ εὐθεῖα, «the straight line in the circle», εὐθεῖα ἐν κύκλῳ μὴ διὰ τοῦ κέντρου οὖσα, «a straight line in the circle not passing through the centre», εὐθεῖα ἐλάττων τοῦ διαμέτρου, «a straight line smaller than the diameter».
@p 0244b030..0244b042 + 0245b001..0245b011
The origin of the technical word *jayb* was explained satisfactorily by Munk, whom F. Woepcke, *Essai sur la propagation des chiffres indiens*, in the Journal Asiatique, 6th series, vol. I, 1863, p. 478, note, and almost all later writers have followed. From the Indian word *jīvā*, chord and sine, which I mentioned a little above, the Arabs, who have no letter *v*, made *jīb* جيب; for the Sanskrit *j* is pronounced like the Arabic *j*. But since *jīb* has no meaning in Arabic, while the letters جيب can also be read *jayb* without any change to the writing (for in ordinary writing the small signs that show the true pronunciation of the vowels are almost always left out), the Arabs preferred this form of the name, which in their language means not only «the round opening of a garment through which a man's neck and head pass», but also «the fold or pocket made in a garment for carrying small things»; occasionally, but rarely, it also means a man's bosom itself. We have already seen that Adelard of Bath kept the Arabic name *elgeib* in his translations; but his contemporary Gerard of Cremona (1114–1187), in translating the astronomy written by Jābir ibn Aflaḥ, used the word *sinus*, which came into our languages by this route, as Koppe, loc. cit., rightly observed[^245.1]. J. Ruska, *Zur Geschichte des «Sinus»* (in the Zeitschrift für Mathematik und Physik, vol. XL, Leipzig 1895, historisch-literarische Abtheilung, p. 128), cites two Syriac writers of the thirteenth century[^245.2] in whom the technical word *jayb* is always rendered by the Syriac *ʿūbā* ܥܘܒ݁ܐ, which means a man's bosom; but he is wrong when he writes: «Die Uebersetzung durch *sinus* ist demnach als richtig durch die syrische Belege gesichert. Die verständigere Uebersetzung aber, welche einen Mann voraussetzt, dem der mathematische Inhalt und dessen klarer Ausdruck wichtiger ist als pedantische Wörtlichkeit, lautet natürlich *chorda* . . . . . Beide Uebersetzungen finden sich vor: die zweite, wie Koppe nachgewiesen hat, bei Plato von Tivoli» («The translation by *sinus* is thus confirmed as correct by the Syriac evidence. The more sensible translation, however, which presupposes a man to whom the mathematical content and its clear expression matter more than pedantic literalness, is of course *chorda* . . . . . Both translations occur: the second, as Koppe has shown, in Plato of Tivoli»). For Plato of Tivoli writes *chorda* not from any striving after a more rational translation (how foreign that was to him and to the other translators of his age, their translations show plainly enough everywhere), but because al-Battānī uses this very word.

@p 0245b012..0245b023
That the Arab mathematicians learned from the Indians the terms unknown to the Greeks — the chord, the sine, the right sine (Sanskrit *kramajyā*), the versed sine (Sanskrit *utkramajyā*), the sagitta (Sanskrit *śara* or *ishu*) — can hardly be doubted. To this one may add what the Arab writers relate of the Indian book entitled *al-Sindhind* (that is, the Sanskrit *Brahma-sphuṭa-siddhānta*), which was brought to the caliph at Baghdad in the year 156 H, 773. When all this is weighed, the history of the trigonometric functions can be told as follows. The Indians, moved above all by their use of orthogonal projections (von Braunmühl, I, 31–32), used right and versed sines instead of the chords of the Greeks; they computed them for the twenty-fourth parts of the quadrant, that is, for every 3° 45′, taking a radius of 3438 minutes. Once they had received the first notion of sines from the Indians, and had come to know Ptolemy's books at about the same time, the Arabs rightly understood that it was more convenient and more exact to construct new sines by halving Ptolemy's chords (which are computed for every half degree and a radius of sixty parts). This had already been done before al-Battānī.

@p 0245b025..0245b033
The *interpolation* for finding the sines of arcs that fall between two consecutive arcs of the table is the same as the one found in Ptolemy for computing chords: the change of the sines and of the chords is taken to be proportional to the change of the arcs within the interval of the table, half a degree. What Cantor, I, 355 (= 392 of the second edition), writes of Ptolemy, «ein Beweis für diese angenommene Proportionalität in engem Bereiche ist dagegen nicht vorhanden» («a proof of this proportionality, assumed within a narrow range, is however not given»), may rightly be said also of al-Battānī, of Ḥabash (fol. 18v) and of the other Arabs. More exact interpolations they used only in constructing tables. — Tables of sines for every minute of the quadrant, computed by Ibn Yūnus, survive in the Arabic codex 5752 of the Royal Library of Berlin; Ulugh Beg also computed others.
@p 0245b034..0245b040 + 0246b001..0246b007
*Versed sines*, whose use the Indians knew before the Arabs, the author defines:
\[ \text{versed sine}\ a = R - \cos a. \]
He thought it superfluous to construct a table of them, which on the other hand we find in Ḥabash. At the foot of the page two formulas are given; one is
\[ [[(α)]] \cos a = \sqrt{R^2 - \sin^2 a}, \]
which follows from the theorem of Pythagoras and, with $R = 1$, corresponds to our familiar rule
\[ \cos a = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 a}. \]
The other,
\[ [[(β)]] \sin \frac{90^\circ - a}{2} = \sqrt{30^{p}\,(R - \sin a)}, \]
comes from the rule of al-Battānī and of Ptolemy mentioned in note *a* (p. 153),
\[ \text{chord}\,\frac{a}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{2R - \text{chord}\,(180^\circ - a)}{2} \times 2R}, \]
which, when sines are put for chords and $R = 60^{p}$ is taken, becomes
\[ \sin \frac{a}{2} = \sqrt{30^{p}\,(R - \cos a)}. \]
Why al-Battānī took the complement of *a* in place of *a*, I do not know.
