@p 0696b016..0696b018
Ptolemy sets out the doctrine of the latitudes of the planets, which is extremely involved, very obscurely in XIII, 1–6 (ed. Halma, vol. II, pp. 367–416); I shall now try to explain it briefly, so that the construction of the tables may be clear.
@p 0696b019..0696b031
Let us begin with the *three superior planets*, whose latitude consists of two parts: the inclination of the plane of the deferent to the plane of the ecliptic, and the inclination of the plane of the epicycle to the plane of the deferent[^696.2]. The first is unchanging; the second variable, for the true apogee (and perigee) of the epicycle describes a certain small circle (κυκλίσκος), perpendicular to the plane of the deferent and having its centre on the circumference of the deferent, under this law: it completes half the circumference of the small circle while the centre of the epicycle passes from one of the nodes (that is, from one of the places where the deferent and the plane of the ecliptic intersect) to the other. Hence it follows that the true apogee (and perigee) travels round the small circle with an unequal motion, and completes its revolutions with uniform motion not about the small circle's own centre but about another point, which lies off the centre of the small circle in the same proportion as the centre of the deferent lies off the centre of the earth[^696.3]. When the centre of the epicycle stands at the nodes, the line of the apogee and perigee of the epicycle coincides with the plane of both the deferent and the ecliptic; outside the nodes the apogees of the epicycle always lie between the plane of the deferent and the plane of the ecliptic, the perigees beyond the plane of the deferent. Hence it is clear that the greatest latitude befalls the superior planets at the true perigee of the epicycle.

The *northern limit*, τὸ βόρειον πέρας, is the point of the deferent that inclines most to the north, and so lies 90 degrees from each node towards the north; the *southern limit*, τὸ νότιον πέρας, is the point of the deferent opposite to it. In Mars the northern limit is also the apogee of the deferent, and the southern limit the perigee; but not so in Saturn and Jupiter. The northern limit of Saturn lies 50° from the apogee of the deferent towards the west, that of Jupiter 20° from the apogee towards the east, as we already saw on p. 239, ll. 8–10.

@p 0698b012..0698b013
Let us now go on to the *inferior planets* (Venus and Mercury). They have three inequalities in latitude, which I shall take one by one:
@p 0698b014..0698b021
The 1st inequality is the inclination of the deferent to the plane of the ecliptic (ἔγκλισις τοῦ ἐκκέντρου, called *deviatio* by some of our writers); and it is not fixed, as in the superior planets, but varies with the true course of the centre of the epicycle[^698.2]. For it is nil when the centre of the epicycle occupies either node (that is, the common section of the planes of the deferent and of the ecliptic), and then grows gradually as the epicycle recedes from the nodes, always carrying Venus to the north and Mercury to the south; it reaches its greatest amount (10′ to the north in Venus, 45′ to the south in Mercury) at the apogee or the perigee of the deferent. Since the epicycle completes the circumference of the deferent in a sidereal year, it is clear from what has been said that the apogee of the deferent is northern for half a year and southern for half a year.
@f 698.2 0698nr002..0698nr005
*Almag.* XIII, 1 (ed. Halma, vol. II, p. 370): Ὥστε καὶ ἐκ τούτου συνάγεσϑαι διότι αἱ μὲν τῶν ἐκκέντρων ἐγκλίσεις κινούμεναι καὶ αὐταὶ συναποκαϑίστανται ταῖς περιόδοις τῶν ἐπικύκλων («so that from this too it is gathered that the inclinations of the eccentrics, which move as well, return together with the periods of the epicycles»).
@p 0698b022..0698b030 + 0699b001..0699b003
The 2nd inequality (ἔγκλισις τοῦ ἐπικύκλου, «inclination of the epicycle», Arabic *mayl falak al-tadwīr*, our writers' *inclinatio* or *declinatio*) is the inclination to the plane of the deferent of the diameter of the epicycle that joins the true apogee and perigee of the epicycle. The apogee and the perigee of the epicycle describe, indeed, a small circle perpendicular to the plane of the deferent, as we saw for the superior planets; but with this difference, that for Venus and Mercury the inclination is greatest at the nodes and nil at the apogee or the perigee of the deferent, for at the apogee and the perigee of the deferent that diameter of the epicycle lies in the plane of the deferent. At the apogee of the deferent the true apogee of the epicycle of Venus begins to incline to the north of the deferent and the perigee to the south; the contrary happens to Mercury. At the perigee of the deferent the true apogee of the epicycle of Venus begins to incline to the south of the deferent and the perigee to the north; the contrary happens to Mercury. — The position of the ascending node of Venus is not the same as that of Mercury; for Venus, as it recedes from the ascending node, is on the side of the apogee of the deferent, Mercury on the side of the perigee[^699.1].

II, p. 373) means: καὶ ἔτι καὶ ἐπὶ τούτων ἡ τῆς ὁμοίας ἀφέσεως ἀρχή τε καὶ ἀποκατάστασις, ἐπὶ μὲν τοῦ τῆς Ἀφροδίτης ἀπὸ τοῦ κατὰ τὸ προσϑετικὸν ἡμικύκλιον συνδέσμου συνίσταται, ἐπὶ δὲ τοῦ τοῦ Ἑρμοῦ ἀπὸ τοῦ κατὰ τὸ ἀφαιρετικόν, «moreover, the beginning and the return of such a course take place for Venus from the node of the additive semicircle, for Mercury from the node of the subtractive one».
@p 0699b004..0699b013
The 3rd inequality (λόξωσις, «slant», Arabic *inḥirāf*, *warāb*, *iltiwāʾ*, our writers' *obliquatio* or *reflexio*) is the variable inclination of the diameter of the epicycle that joins the points of the mean distances, that is, of the diameter that cuts at right angles the diameter drawn through the true apogee and perigee of the epicycle. The ends of this diameter (90°–270°) also describe small circles, but perpendicular to the plane of the ecliptic and having their centres on diameters parallel to the plane of the ecliptic. These centres move with the same velocity as the rest (ἰσοταχῶς τοῖς ἄλλοις). This slant, like the second inclination of the superior planets, is greatest at the apogee or the perigee of the deferent and nil at the nodes, where that diameter coincides with the plane of both the deferent and the ecliptic. At the apogee of the deferent the eastern end of this diameter of the epicycle of Venus is to the north, at the perigee to the south; the contrary happens to Mercury.
@p 0699b014..0699b015
From what has just been set out it follows clearly enough that everything that happens to Venus inclining to the north happens likewise, and by the same laws, to Mercury declining to the south.
@p 0699b016..0699b022
In the superior planets we saw that the latitude consists partly of the inclination of the deferent and partly of that of the epicycle, and that the two are so mixed that they never exist separately. The three greatest inequalities of Venus and Mercury, on the contrary, can easily be caught singly by observation and computed in numbers; for the inferior planets occupy the perigee or the apogee of the epicycle when the centre of the epicycle falls in the apogee or the perigee of the deferent[^699.2], so that at both places they appear to decline equally from the ecliptic to the north or to the south; moreover, when the inclination of the epicycle is greatest, the slant of the epicycle is nil.

@p 0701b001..0701b011
Let us now go on to the *greatest slants* of the diameter of the mean distances, which occur at the apogee and at the perigee of the deferent. By observations and computations Ptolemy[^701.1] found the greatest slant of Venus to be 2° 27′ at the apogee of the deferent and 2° 34′ at the perigee, and the greatest of Mercury 2° 17′ at the apogee and 2° 46′ at the perigee[^701.2]. Hence it follows that at the mean geocentric distance of the epicycle a slant of very nearly 2½° befalls both planets; for the greatest departures from this mean amount are −3′ and +4′ for Venus, −13′ and +16′ for Mercury. Such variations of Venus, so small that they cannot be caught with instruments, can be neglected altogether; so a table made for the mean slant of the epicycle, 2½°, will suffice for all the geocentric distances of the epicycle of Venus. But the variation that befalls Mercury is about ¼ of a degree, or 1/10 of those 2½°; so the table made for the mean geocentric distance will need some correction for the other distances of the epicycle of Mercury.

Having set out the method by which Ptolemy computed the two columns of the *superior* and of the *inferior* planets, let us go on to explain the column of the *proportional parts*, which is common to all the planets and is found in vol. II, p. 141. The preceding columns were made for the centre of the epicycle placed either at the points of the greatest latitudes or at the nodes of the deferent. Those columns must also be fitted to the intermediate points of the deferent. A direct computation is too long a labour and requires very many tables; so to fit the preceding columns to any other position of the centre of the epicycle Ptolemy used proportional parts. He did not, however, compute them directly; but, considering, XIII, 4 (ed. Halma, II, p. 411), that the inclinations and slants grow or decrease very nearly in the same proportion as the latitudes of the moon, he took these lunar latitudes, already computed exactly, as they stood in the tables, and multiplied them by 12 so that they became sixtieths; for the greatest latitude of the moon is 5°, whence 5° × 12 = 60°. In this way the last column came out very easily.

Alwin Haebler, *Die Lehren des Claudius Ptolemaeus von den Bewegungen der Planeten*, in Zeitschr. für Mathematik und Physik, 45. Bd., 1900, hist.-litter. Abteilung, pp. 171, 192, 198[^703.1], thinks that another doctrine of the latitudes, far simpler and common to all the planets, occurs in the *Hypotheses*. But wrongly. For in the *Hypotheses* (ed. Halma, pp. 48–56), which are later than the Almagest, Ptolemy reviews summarily, for each planet, the spheres by which all their motions are represented. He describes the parecliptic circle (ὁ ὁμόκεντρος τῷ ζωδιακῷ κύκλος), revolving in the plane of the zodiac from west to east with the same motion as the fixed stars, the oblique circle (ὁ ἐγκεκλιμένος κύκλος, ὁ λοξός), the deferent (ὁ ἔκκεντρος, movable for Mercury, fixed for the others) and the centre of the equant (περὶ ὃ κινεῖται ἰσοταχῶς), as I have set out on pp. 238–39 and 243, except for two small discrepancies in Mercury, which I mentioned on p. 243, notes 1 and 2. The peculiarities of the epicycles, however, he describes for each planet as follows. Let a *small circle* (κυκλίσκος) be supposed in the sphere of the epicycle, concentric with it and lying in the plane of the oblique circle; let the straight line produced from the centre of the equant through the centre of the small circle always pass through two points of the small circle, which we shall call the apogee and the perigee [of the epicycle]; the size of this small circle is not determined. Let there be a *second small circle*, concentric with the first and in the same plane of the oblique circle, revolving uniformly about its centre in such a way that, beginning from the apogee [of the epicycle], it moves from east to west (ἐπὶ τὰ αὐτὰ τῇ τοῦ κόσμου περιστροφῇ, «in the same direction as the revolution of the world») with the same motion with which the centre of the epicycle goes round [the circumference of the equant]; the size of this small circle too is not stated. Finally, let a *third small circle* be supposed, concentric with the second and the first but inclined to their plane, whose circumference the planet goes round uniformly [with its anomalistic motion] from west to east (ἐπὶ τὰ ἐναντία τῇ τοῦ κόσμου περιστροφῇ); the inclination and the radius of this third small circle are given for each planet. — It is clear that this third small circle is the true epicycle, and that the first two, like the parecliptic circle, are useless for the mathematical theory and serve only the philosophical theory of the celestial spheres. It is also clear that this whole exposition of the *Hypotheses* contains only a general outline of the motions of the planets, but *nothing* that would serve for computing the *latitudes*.
