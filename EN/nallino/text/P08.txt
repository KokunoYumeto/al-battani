VI. In the Laurentian Library in Florence a parchment codex is kept, marked *Conventi soppressi 414*, of 63 leaves, 194 mm high, 140 mm wide, which contains on f. 1r–45r the work of an unknown author, to which, however, a later hand added this inscription: «*Here begins the book* of Albategni *that is called the little Almagest*». After a brief introduction, whose opening is «Omnium recte philosophantium non solum visibilibus et credibilibus argumentis sed etiam firmis rationibus deprehensum est formam celi spericam esse motumque ipsius orbicularem circa terram.....» (by all who philosophize rightly it has been established, not only by visible and credible arguments but also by firm reasons, that the form of the heaven is spherical and its motion circular about the earth.....), it continues thus: «Que cum ita sint superest ut proposita aggrediamur. — Data circuli diametro. latera decagoni. pentagoni. exagoni. tetragoni atque trianguli omnium ab eodem circulo circumscriptorum reperire» (this being so, it remains for us to take up what is proposed. — Given the diameter of a circle, to find the sides of the decagon, the pentagon, the hexagon, the square and the triangle, all circumscribed by the same circle). It comprises six books, whose subjects agree with those of the first six of the Almagest: in the *first*, f. 1r–3v, there are propositions on chords, on the measurement of the obliquity of the ecliptic, on the declinations of the Sun and on the right ascensions; in the *second*, f. 4r–8v, on the oblique ascensions, the semidiurnal arcs, the temporal and the equinoctial hours, the length of the day, the angles contained by the ecliptic and the horizon, the altitudes, etc.; in the *third*, f. 9v–14v, the whole doctrine of the motion of the Sun; in the *fourth*, f. 14v–20r, on the mean motions of the Moon and its first inequality; in the *fifth*, f. 20v–33v, on the inequalities of the lunar motions, the distances of the Moon from the Earth, the parallaxes and the sizes of the luminaries; in the *sixth*, lastly, f. 34r–45r, on the conjunctions, the oppositions and the eclipses of the Sun and the Moon. The last chapter of the sixth book is: «Flexus tenebrarum sive in solis sive in lune defectu patentes assignare» (to assign the inclinations of the darkness that appear in an eclipse of the Sun or of the Moon).
@p 0036b011..0036b015
In each book the necessary definitions are set out first; then the propositions, always with geometrical demonstrations added, not mixed with numerical ones, as is done in the Almagest. How astronomical observations are to be made is taught from time to time; thus f. 3r: «maximam declinationem per instrumenti artificium et considerationem reperire» (to find the greatest declination by the skill of an instrument and by observation), and f. 9v: «Anni quantitatem per considerationes instrumentis deprehendere» (to determine the length of the year by observations with instruments).
@p 0036b016..0036b024
The book does not seem at all to have been translated from the Arabic; it has no smell of the Arabic language. The unknown author always adduces al-Battānī's astronomical elements and methods where they differ from the Ptolemaic ones; and they agree very well with Plato's translation[^36.1]. Sometimes he also mentions the *Toledan Tables* of Arzachel (al-Zarqālī). There is no doubt that this book is the same that Albertus Magnus, loc. cit., after mentioning Ptolemy and al-Battānī, describes thus: «From these two books too a certain man compiled a book in the style of Euclid, whose commentary contains the view of both, namely of Ptolemy and of Albategni, which begins thus: *Omnium recte philosophantium* etc.». — I find the same «*Almagesti minus*» quoted by John of Capua at the end of his *Canones eclipsium* (of the year 1332), which I shall report in § 4, no. II.

When this is weighed, the time at which the author flourished can be determined roughly; for he must be later than the year 1175, in which Gerard of Cremona translated the Almagest into Latin, and earlier than Albertus Magnus, who died in 1280.
