@p 0366b003
The computation of the *apparent conjunction of the sun and the moon.*
@p 0366b004..0366b009
[The relation between the apparent conjunction and the true conjunction in eclipses of the sun gives rise to two different problems. When the time of the apparent conjunction is known from direct observation of the eclipse, the epoch of the true conjunction is very easily derived. It is enough to compute the effect of the parallax at the time of the true conjunction; then the difference of the true longitudes of the sun and the moon will be found, and from this difference, the hourly synodic or relative motion being known, the time of the true conjunction will easily be deduced.
@p 0366b010..0366b013
But when the time of the true conjunction is given, to deduce the apparent conjunction one must know the parallax at the time of the apparent conjunction, which time is itself unknown. So the problem cannot be solved directly, but only by approximations. Al-Battānī uses several successive approximate corrections, and proceeds in this way.
@fig AB01-PDF0366-F01 AB01-PDF0366-body-F01
@p 0366b014..0366b029
Let AB be the ecliptic in the region of the celestial sphere in which the sun and the moon stand at the time of the eclipse; at the time of the true conjunction (such, that is, as it would be seen from the centre of the earth, and as it is deduced from the astronomical tables) let L′ be the true place of the moon, S′ the true place of the sun, L′S′ perpendicular to AB. Let L′Z′ be the direction of the vertical circle of the moon at the same moment; because of the parallax in altitude the moon will appear to the observer not at L′ but at λ′. If the perpendicular λ′σ′ is drawn from λ′, the arc of the ecliptic S′σ′ will plainly be what al-Battānī calls *the first parallax* [in longitude], namely the difference of longitude that is actually observed between the sun S′ and the moon λ′ at the time of the true conjunction; we shall call this moment of time ϑ.
@p 0366b030..0366b035
In the case that the figure represents, the moon is to the west of the nonagesimal (or of the degree that is 90° from the ascendant); and since the moon on the ecliptic is behind the sun (that is, σ′ is behind S′), it will need some time to reach the longitude of the sun. This space of time is what we must find.
@p 0366b036..0366b044
If the effect of the parallax in longitude always remained one and the same, it would be enough to divide the arc S′σ′ by the relative hourly motion of the two luminaries; the time sought would come out. But the parallax changes quickly; and since the interval between the true and the apparent conjunction can sometimes reach nearly two hours, we cannot even suppose that it changes uniformly. So if, as al-Battānī bids, we take the parallax in longitude S′σ′ and divide it by the relative hourly motion[^n], we do not find the time by which the apparent conjunction is retarded with respect to the true, but only a quantity near that time. Let ε′ be this time close to the true one, namely, as al-Battānī says, *the hours of the first parallax*. Let it be added to the time ϑ; there will come out the first approximation to the problem and to the time sought, namely ϑ + ε′.
@n Al-Battānī divides the first parallax by the unequal hourly motion of the moon itself (p. 104, ll. 21–22; p. 110, ll. 11–12), and only the second by the gain of the moon on the sun (p. 104, ll. 29–30); adding the minutes of the first parallax to the place of the moon then gives the place of the moon at ϑ + ε′. In either way ε′ is only the first approximation, which the second corrects.
@p 0367b001..0367b009
Then let the arc S′σ′ be added to the longitude of the moon, to the anomaly and to the argument of latitude; that is, let the moon be supposed to have advanced in its celestial orbit from L′ to L², running the arc L′L² = S′σ′. For the corrected time ϑ + ε′ let the ascendant be determined again; and with the new place L² of the moon let the parallax in altitude L²λ² be computed anew; λ² will be the place where the moon is seen at the time ϑ + ε′. The parallax in longitude (al-Battānī's *second parallax*) will be Σσ², determined by the perpendiculars drawn from L² and λ² to the ecliptic. Let S² be the place of the sun at the time ϑ + ε′. If S² and σ² coincided, the problem would be solved. Otherwise a new correction must be applied to the time ϑ + ε′ to give the time of the apparent conjunction; likewise the *second parallax* must be corrected again, although it is always much nearer the truth than the first parallax.
@p 0367b010..0367b017
So, al-Battānī says, if we divide the *second parallax* by the relative hourly motion of the two luminaries, we shall find a correction ε² of the time better than the correction ε′. He calls this ε² *the hours of the second parallax*. For the new, more exact time ϑ + ε² let the ascendant, the middle of the heaven, the place of the moon and the anomaly of the moon be found again; from them let a new parallax (*the third parallax*) be deduced. If this is equal to the second, the problem will be solved, and the time ϑ + ε² will give the time of the apparent conjunction; for then we shall know most certainly that the second and the third parallax are exactly equal to the difference of the true longitudes of the sun and the moon, so that the apparent longitudes of the luminaries are equal to each other.
@p 0367b018..0367b025
But from this al-Battānī concludes certain *false* things, printed in italics on p. 104, ll. 41–42, p. 105, ll. 1–3, 24–25, 34, p. 110, ll. 25–26. For he says: If the third parallax is equal to the second, the apparent longitudes of the two luminaries are also equal. So if the third parallax differs from the second by some quantity, the corresponding apparent longitudes of the sun and the moon differ from each other by the same quantity; that is, by that quantity the third parallax must exceed the difference of the true longitudes of the moon and the sun. — This is not true[^n]; for from the fact that two differences are equal to each other when they are nothing, it by no means follows that they are also equal when they are not nothing.
@n Al-Battānī's statement holds. The time ε² is the second parallax divided by the relative hourly motion, so that at ϑ + ε² the true longitudes of the moon and the sun differ by the second parallax, as Schiaparelli says just above; the apparent longitudes then differ by the difference of the second and the third parallax, whether this is nothing or not. That difference is therefore the Δ that remains, and al-Battānī's use of it below is right. The translation of al-Battānī's chapter has the same note.
@p 0367b026..0367b032
Let Δ be the difference of the apparent longitudes, which remains to be found by applying the third parallax to the true longitude of the moon: we must reduce this residual difference to nothing by correcting the time ϑ + ε² somewhat again. It is enough to compute the speed of the apparent relative motion of the sun and the moon in longitude; let Δ be divided by this speed, and the time of the apparent conjunction will come out. — For the quantity Δ al-Battānī wrongly uses *the difference between the second and the third parallax*. With this *error* excepted, al-Battānī's computation is true, and is explained in this way:
@p 0367b033..0367b046
The apparent relative hourly motion of the moon and the sun is deduced from the true relative hourly motion that the tables give (*the gain of the moon on the sun*, as the Arabs say) by subtracting from it (and never *adding* to it[^n]) the hourly variation of the parallax, taken absolutely, without distinction of sign. If this were uniform, that is, proportional to the variation of the hour angle, the variation of the parallax answering to a minute of time would very easily be found by dividing the difference between two parallaxes found by the minutes of time elapsed from the one to the other. But the variation changes so rapidly that it cannot be considered uniform even within the space of one hour; so the computation cannot be made for so long an interval of time. One must use at least one further approximation, namely determine at least the second parallax and the time ϑ + ε′ that answers to it. The error of this time will then be a small fraction of an hour, in which the variation of the parallax may be thought uniform, and so too the apparent relative motion in longitude. Better still if we compute the third parallax and the time ϑ + ε² that answers to it. — The problem now is to find the hourly speed of the apparent relative motion in the short interval of time needed to reduce the residual difference Δ of the apparent longitudes to nothing.
@n Al-Battānī's text subtracts the sixfold difference of the parallaxes from the gain of the moon when the third parallax exceeds the second, and adds it when the third is the smaller (p. 105, ll. 23–24 and 33–34).
@p 0368b001..0368b004
Al-Battānī, p. 105, ll. 6–24, lays down that the short interval of time in which the variation of the parallax may be considered uniform cannot exceed 1/6 of an hour, or 10ᵐ of hour angle in time. Of the two parallaxes that must be used, one is already known, and answers to the hour angle ϑ + ε², as we have just seen. The author distinguishes two cases:
@p 0368b005..0368b012
1. If the hour angle, or time, ϑ + ε² differs from the nearest whole hour by more than 1/6 of an hour, we must compute a new parallax directly for the time ϑ + ε² + 1/6 or ϑ + ε² − 1/6. The difference of the two parallaxes will be the parallactic variation in 1/6 of an hour; multiplying it by 6 we shall find the hourly parallactic motion, which must be subtracted from the «gain of the moon» (the relative hourly motion in true longitude) to give al-Battānī's «*corrected motion*», that is, the apparent relative hourly motion of the two luminaries in longitude. — We cannot use Theon's tables, because the nearest datum in these tables answers to an interval greater than 1/6 of an hour from the hour angle ϑ + ε².
@p 0368b013..0368b019
2. But if the fraction of an hour in the time ϑ + ε² is less than 1/6 or greater than 5/6 (for example 1/8, 1/10 … or 7/8, 9/10 …), we can make the computation shorter by using the nearest whole hour, that is, taking 1/8, 1/10 … as the interval. The limits of the interval will then be the hour angle ϑ + ε², whose parallax is already known, and the hour angle of whole hours, whose parallax is given in Theon's tables. In this way some labour is avoided. — Plainly such an advantage cannot be had unless an interval of 1/8, 1/10 … of an hour is taken; the interval of 1/6 must be avoided, for it leads beyond the whole hour. — The «*corrected motion*» will be computed as before.
@p 0368b020..0368b027
Al-Battānī then rightly observes that the parallax in longitude decreases with the time and the hour angle if the moon is between the ascendant and the nonagesimal, and increases if the moon is found between the nonagesimal and the descendant. In the first case he teaches (ll. 27–33) to *subtract* 1/6 of an hour from ϑ + ε², to make the interval between the hour angles ϑ + ε² and ϑ + ε² − 1/6; in the second case to *add* 1/6 of an hour, so that the interval between the hour angles ϑ + ε² and ϑ + ε² + 1/6 results. This precept ensures that the new auxiliary parallax is always greater than the parallax of the angle ϑ + ε². A thing *useful* for avoiding errors of sign, but not *necessary*; above all since the precept concerns case 1 only, not case 2.
@p 0368b028..0368b031
Lastly, let the residual difference Δ of the apparent longitudes of the sun and the moon be divided by the *corrected motion*; there will come out a small time τ, which must be added to or subtracted from the approximate time ϑ + ε², according as the moon at that time ϑ + ε² followed or preceded the sun in longitude. There will result *the required time of the apparent conjunction*.
@p 0368b032..0368b042
All the author's precepts can be set out briefly in this way. For the given time ϑ of the true conjunction let the first parallax be computed and divided by the «gain of the moon»; there will come out the time ε′, which will give the first approximation ϑ + ε′. For this new time ϑ + ε′ let the longitudes of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the ascendant be computed again; from them let a new parallax be deduced, which, divided by the gain of the moon, will give the time ε²; hence the second approximation ϑ + ε². For this third time let the longitudes of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the ascendant be computed anew; from them let the third parallax be found. Let it be added to the true longitude of the moon; the apparent longitude of the moon will come out. If this is equal to the apparent longitude of the sun, the problem will be solved. If a difference ± Δ remains, let the apparent relative hourly motion of the sun and the moon (the «corrected motion») be computed as we taught above; let Δ be divided by it; let the time that comes out of the division be applied, according to its sign, to ϑ + ε²; the time of the apparent conjunction will come out. — Schiaparelli.]
@p 0368b043..0368b045
The way in which al-Battānī works differs somewhat from that of older writers. It is worth reporting what al-Khāzinī relates in *al-Zīj al-Sanjarī*, ff. 80r–81r (treatise VIII, part II, ch. II, «on correcting the hours of the middle of the eclipse»).
@p 0368b046 + 0369b001..0369b022
«*The first method*, according to Ptolemy. — He says: Let us find the distance of the hours of the true conjunction from noon, the altitude of the moon, and the parallax of the moon in the circle of altitude. From this parallax let us subtract the parallax of the sun; there remains the apparent parallax. With these true hours let us find the angle of longitude, and from it the parallax in longitude, which we shall call *the first parallax*. Let us divide it by the true gain of the moon: there will come out *the hours of the first parallax*, which we shall subtract from the hours of the true conjunction if the moon is less than 90° from the ascendant, and otherwise add. There will result *the hours of the second conjunction*. Again with these hours let us find the altitude of the moon, the apparent parallax, the angle of longitude and the parallax in longitude, which we shall call *the second parallax*. If it is equal to the first parallax, the hours of the second conjunction will be the very hours of the middle of the eclipse; if different, we shall need to correct it. The correction is this: let us find the difference between those two parallaxes; then, by solving the proportion
\[ \text{unknown quantity} : \text{difference} = \text{difference} : \text{smaller parallax}, \]
that is, by dividing the square of the difference by the smaller parallax, we shall find the unknown quantity (*al-juzʾ al-majhūl*). Let us add this to the difference if the second parallax is greater than the first, or subtract it from the difference if the first is the greater; there will come out the corrected difference, which, added to the first parallax in longitude, will make *the corrected parallax*. Let us divide this by the true gain of the moon; there will come out *the hours of the apparent parallax*. Let us subtract them from the hours of the true conjunction if the moon is less than 90° from the ascendant, otherwise add them; there will result *the corrected, exact (murattaba) hours of the middle of the eclipse*. — For these let us determine the true places of both luminaries and of the node, the anomaly of the moon, the altitude of the moon, the lunar parallax in latitude and its side; from the apparent place of the moon and the node we shall find the true latitude of the moon, and shall obtain its corrected latitude.
@p 0369b023..0369b041
«*The second method*, received from tradition (*ʿamaluhu mursalan*). — Thābit[^369.1], the authors of the *Tested Tables*[^369.2] and the Indians say: Let us find the distance of the hours of the true conjunction from noon, and the parallax of the moon in longitude; let us divide the parallax by the true gain of the moon, and *the hours of the first parallax* will come out. If the moon is less than 90° from the ascendant, let us subtract them from the hours of the true conjunction to give *the hours of the second conjunction*; likewise let us subtract the first parallax in longitude from the degree of the conjunction, and *the apparent place of the moon* will result. If, on the other hand, the moon is more than 90° from the ascendant, we shall add the hours of the first parallax to the hours of the true conjunction to give *the hours of the second conjunction*, and add the first parallax to the degree of the conjunction to give *the apparent place of the moon*. — Then, using these hours, let us compute the parallax in longitude again; if it is equal to the first parallax, the hours of the second conjunction will be the very hours of the middle of the eclipse, and the apparent moon will be the moon of the middle of the eclipse. But if those two parallaxes are different, let us divide the second by the true gain of the moon; *the hours of the second parallax* will come out. Let us add them to the hours of the true conjunction, and the parallax to the degree of the conjunction, or subtract, as we said above. *The hours of the third conjunction* will result. If this is equal to the second, it will be what is sought; if different, we shall compute the fourth and fifth hours of the conjunction, and shall not cease to repeat the computations in this way many times until we have found a number equal to the preceding number. The hours that come out last will be *the hours of the middle of the eclipse*, and the apparent moon will be the one that was sought.

Let us divide it by the true gain of the moon; *the hours of the first parallax* will come out. If the moon is less than 90° from the ascendant, we shall subtract them from the hours of the true conjunction, and the first parallax from the degree [of the same conjunction]; if it is more than 90° away, we shall add both; *the hours of the second conjunction* and *the apparent place of the moon* will come out. If the second parallax itself is equal to the first, and *the hours of the third conjunction* equal to the hours of the second, these hours will be those of the middle of the eclipse, and the apparent moon will be the one that was sought. But if the second parallax is different from the first, let us divide the difference between them[^370.1] by the gain *apparent at that hour*[^370.2], *and not by the true gain* (وقسمناه على السبق المرئي لتلك الساعة خاصة دون سبقه الحقيقي); what comes out will be a fraction of an hour. If the second parallax is greater than the first, we shall add the fraction to the hours of the second parallax so that they become *corrected*; but if the second is less than the first parallax, we shall subtract the fraction, and shall find *the corrected hours*. Then, if the degree of the conjunction is less than 90° from the ascendant, we shall subtract these corrected hours from the hours of the true conjunction; if the distance is greater than 90°, we shall add the corrected hours. What results from the subtraction or addition will be *the hours of the middle of the eclipse*. — Then let us multiply these corrected hours into the motions of the sun, the moon, the node and the anomaly of the moon; let us add the products to the corresponding places at the time of the conjunction if the corrected hours were additive, or subtract them if the hours were subtractive, except only for the node, with which one must work the other way round[^370.3]. *The exact places* (*al-murattaba*) will come out».
