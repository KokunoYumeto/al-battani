@p 0387b009..0387b024
I. — «*Ptolemy relates in his book …*». Not Ptolemy, but Theon of Alexandria (fourth century), in the *Handy Tables*, ed. Halma vol. I, p. 53, chapter on the *turning* (περὶ τροπῆς): «Since according to certain opinions (κατά τινας δόξας) the ancients among the astrologers (οἱ παλαιοὶ τῶν ἀποτελεσματικῶν) hold that the tropic points, after a certain epoch, are carried 8 degrees in the order of the signs [i.e. eastward], and afterwards go back as many, — which Ptolemy indeed does not admit, because without such a hypothesis the equinoctial computations taken from the tables agree with those deduced by instruments[^387.1]; so we too advise (ὅθεν καὶ ἡμεῖς παραινοῦμεν) that such an opinion be not received; — we shall set out the method of computation that they used. — For, taking as the epoch at which the greatest digression of 8° eastward had been made the 128 years that preceded the reign of Augustus, and adding the 313 years that elapsed from the reign of Augustus to the reign of Diocletian, and the years that have passed since Diocletian [down to the given time], with this sum they find the place [of the tropic points], taking the motion as 1 degree in 80 years; then, taking the quotient of this division from 8 degrees, they add the remainder (which shows the degree that the tropic points have reached at that time) to the degrees of the places of the sun, the moon and the five planets taken by these computations».

@p 0389b003..0389b009
The doctrine of trepidation, which is also mentioned by Proclus[^389.1] in the fifth century, arose, both think[^389.2], from the wish to reconcile with each other the Chaldaeo-Egyptian hypothesis of immovable equinoctial points and the theory of precession that Hipparchus had deduced from observations made over less than two centuries. Those eight degrees, they think, come from a change, wrongly interpreted, that Hipparchus brought into the division of the ecliptic when, following the custom of the more ancient astronomers, he restored the beginnings of Aries, Cancer, Libra and Capricorn to the equinoctial and solstitial points; these beginnings Meton, Eudoxus and others had put 8° to the west of the equinoxes and solstices.

@p 0389b010..0389b021 + 0390b001..0390b002
What Letronne and Martin say of Eudoxus (in the second half of the fourth century BC) and of Meton (in the first half of the fifth)[^n] must not be thought true; for it is reported by Columella[^389.3], of little authority in these matters. Hipparchus[^389.4], on the contrary, expressly states that Eudoxus put the equinoctial and solstitial points in the *centre*, i.e. at 15°, of the signs of Aries, Cancer, Libra and Capricorn; and those who have written on Greek astronomy in our time do not doubt it[^389.5]. Nor does Schiaparelli doubt it, who explains Columella's error to me in this way: in Eudoxus' time the Greeks began the year in summer, whence came the custom of beginning the zodiac with Cancer, which appears from the passage of Hipparchus just cited and from the *Parapegma*, or calendar, placed at the end of Geminus' *Elements of astronomy*. The constellation of Cancer occupies about half a sign, i.e. about 15 degrees. Some grammarian, having heard that Eudoxus put the summer solstice in the middle of Cancer, confused the *dodecatemories* with the *constellations* and thought that Eudoxus put the summer solstice at 7° 1/2 of Cancer, that is, in the *eighth* degree. That grammarian or astrologer probably lived after the time when the Greek astronomers came to know the use of degrees, that is, after the middle of the second century BC.
@n Meton observed the summer solstice at Athens in 432 BC (Ptolemy, *Almagest* III, 1), in the second half of the fifth century.

Lastly I shall give the words of the Hispano-Jewish Abraham bar Ḥiyya[^391.2] that concern this chapter of al-Battānī. He says that «the sages of India, all the inhabitants of the land of the Romans and the ancients among the sages of the Chaldaeans held only this opinion of the fixed stars: that the stars do not run through the whole heaven, but move only eight degrees of the zodiac forward and back, to the east and to the west. The cause of this motion is the pole of the ecliptic, which revolves to the east and the west on a small circle [whose diameter is] 8° of the zodiac. That pole completes the [whole] circle in 1600 years; because of [this] going round of the pole on the circle some learned men imagined that the fixed stars go round the whole heaven, since that secret was not manifest to them and they did not know the revolution of the pole of the ecliptic, by which the fixed stars move from west to east for 800 years, and afterwards are brought back again westward to their former place after 1600 complete years. … These are the things that the Indians tell in defence of their opinion. — Ptolemy mentioned this motion [of trepidation], but would neither reject it nor approve it. — The sages of Ishmael [i.e. the Arabs] who lived in the time of [the caliph] al-Maʾmūn gathered their strength to overturn the arguments of the Indians. Among them al-Battānī, to refute the Indian opinion, said that the ancients who lived before Ptolemy observed the positions of those stars and set them down in their books; that 420 years later Ptolemy came, determined their positions and found between his observations and those of the ancients a difference of 4° 1/5; whence he deduced that their motion completes 1° in 100 years. About 720 years after Ptolemy, al-Battānī came; he gave himself to these matters, and between his own investigation and Ptolemy's he found a difference of about 11°, from which he deduced that their advance had been 1° 1/2 in 100 years. So it has been found that from the time of the ancients to al-Battānī's age, in 1140 years, the fixed stars advanced 15° 1/5; whereas according to the pronouncements of the Indians it should have been only 11° 36′, if we take the beginning of the computation from the advance, or 3° 36′, if from the return or from the middle point of the direct motion. Similarly, between Ptolemy and al-Battānī only 7° 1/5 eastward should have been found to add to Ptolemy's place, if we begin from the return. And we may begin indifferently from the middle point of the advance or from the middle of the return. — A great argument against them is that they said that the fixed stars advance and return 8 degrees, whereas according to al-Battānī's observation they have advanced 15° from the place that they held at the time of the observation of the ancients. The opinions of the Indians, then, are overturned and demolished, if what al-Battānī relates is true».

III. — Al-Battānī names only *ancient* astrologers, although, as has already appeared from what we have said, there was no lack among the Arabs of men who professed that doctrine. They had mostly received it from the Indians[^392.1]: so Ḥabash in his first book (not in the later ones), in which, as Ibn al-Qifṭī attests, he followed the computations of the Indian book *al-Sindhind* (i.e. *Siddhānta*); so Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Tanūkhī[^392.2], who, to acquire perfect knowledge, «travelled through various regions and visited India, whence he brought foreign notions of astronomy, such as the motion of advance and return [i.e. trepidation] and others»[^392.3]; so finally Ibn al-Adamī[^392.4], who, in a book published after his death by a pupil in the year 308 H (beginning 22 May 920), «computed the motions of the stars by the method of [the book] *al-Sindhind*, and told some things about the motion of advance and return of the heavenly sphere that no one had set out before him. Before the appearance of this book things were heard about that motion that could neither be understood nor reduced to a fixed rule; but when this book came out, the form of that strange motion could be understood, and this was the reason why [many] considered that doctrine attentively. Ṣāʿid ibn al-Ḥasan al-Andalusī[^392.5], judge of Toledo, says: After reading this book, things about that motion became clear to me that I think have not yet become clear to anyone, and I perceived in it causes that I have set out in my book entitled *The correction of the motions of the stars*»[^392.6].

Some Arabs also came to the hypothesis of trepidation by the Greek road; among them Thābit ibn Qurra, who, as we saw on pp. 298 and 301–02, built a new theory[^392.7], chiefly on the words of Theon and the uncertainty of the old observations of the motion, so as to explain in some way the variations of the obliquity of the ecliptic, the various amounts of the length of the year and the discrepancies in the motion of the fixed stars. Alpetragius (see above, p. 299) cites the authority of the fictitious and most ancient philosopher Hermes, whose apocryphal or pseudepigraphic works the Arabs had translated from the Greek; finally Augustinus Ricci says that he learned from his teacher Abraham Zacut[^392.8] that the Arabic doctrine of trepidation rests on two observations of the stars, which Hermes had made 1985 years before the epoch of Ptolemy's catalogue and by which the longitudes of the stars had been found 7 degrees greater than Ptolemy's longitudes[^392.9].

In the fourth century H (the tenth AD) Abū Jaʿfar al-Khāzin[^393.1] and Ibrāhīm ibn Sinān, as we saw on p. 301, followed the doctrine of trepidation. But after this age the astronomers who flourished in the eastern part of the caliphate, from Egypt to farthest India, rejected trepidation[^393.2]. Not so, however, in western Africa and in Spain, where Arzachel (al-Zarqālī), Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī, Alpetragius and Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan (Aboul Hhassan) tried to explain by the doctrine of trepidation the discrepancies between the most ancient observations and the Arabic ones. From their writings the Latin *Alfonsine Tables* (of the year 1251) and many European astronomers down to the middle of the sixteenth century received it.
