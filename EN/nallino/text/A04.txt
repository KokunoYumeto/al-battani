@p 0248b018..0248b024
It should be noted that an error of two minutes in fixing the obliquity is of little consequence in the computations derived from it; for in the right ascensions it can produce a discrepancy only in the seconds of arc[^248.10]; in the oblique ascensions the greatest difference never reaches three minutes of arc (especially since the latitudes that the Arabs had to use were always smaller than 45°); and lastly, in computing the greatest half arc of the day of the sun in the lands under Arab rule, the difference can never exceed ten seconds of an hour. Such differences could be neglected in those times.

@p 0250b004..0250b030 + 0251b001..0251b012
To the later Arabs, who could compare many good observations with one another, the hypothesis of a continuous decrease was not unknown, although the small discrepancies could have been put down to errors of the observers and of the instruments. Indeed, we read the following in the astronomical book entitled the Sanjarī (*al-zīj al-sanjarī*), which Abū Manṣūr ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Khāzinī wrote about the year 520 H (1126)[^250.1]: «In [fixing] the obliquity of the ecliptic there are many discrepancies, about whose cause opinions differ. The most likely of all is that they arise only from the refraction of the rays (وغالب الظنّ ان سببه انعطاف الشعاع دون سايره), so that, when the latitude of two observers is one and the same, the same obliquity appears to both. The greatest declination is 24° 0′ with the Indians, 23° 51′ with Ptolemy and Hipparchus, 23° 35′ with the authors of the Tested [Tables] and al-Battānī, 23° 33′ with some of the moderns (بعض المحدثين). Because of these discrepancies, and because of the irregularity in the motion of the fixed stars, some thought fit (ظهر لبعضهم) to posit a motion in the space between the two spheres, that of the universe and that of the fixed stars, which would complete the whole circle every 1285 (١٢٨٥) solar years, and whose poles, like the poles of the fixed stars too, would not lie on the axis of the universe; so that the poles of the signs of the zodiac would approach the equators or recede from them, and the motion of the fixed stars would be quickened or slowed, in a way that it would take too long to set out now. But since the obliquity is 23° 35′ with most [authors], we shall use this value in our reckonings, as one rightly accepted (استعملناه في الاعتبار مُسلا)». — Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī, in his Memorandum of what is chiefly to be remembered in astronomy[^250.2], relates the following: «The total declination found by the ancient and by the more recent observations is not one and the same; the value the ancients found is greater than the one the more recent have measured. What a more recent observer found is sometimes taken to be less than what an earlier one found, the greatest value found not reaching 24°[^250.3], while the least does not go below 23° 1/2 + 1/(2 × 10) [= 23° 33′][^250.4]. Most (الجمهور) set it at 23° 1/4 + 1/3 [= 23° 35′]. Because of these discrepancies some have thought that the ecliptic moves in latitude and approaches the equator; if this were true, another sphere would have to be posited that carries the zodiac with such a motion. If the ecliptic moved, it could complete the circle; or it could not complete it, but reach a certain limit from which it would afterwards turn back. Any such limit could lie after the two falls of the ecliptic onto the equator, or at the time of the second fall, or between the two falls; and this last [in three ways, namely] after half the circle has been completed, or exactly at the half circle, or before the semicircle is completed. Lastly, the ecliptic could also not reach the space between the two falls, if it is thought to turn back either at the time of its first fall onto the equator or before it. There are, then, eight hypotheses. By the first five, the two halves of the zodiac, the northern and the southern, would exchange places, together with their properties; and by three of these [five], each half of the zodiac would [at times] fall onto each half of the equator. By the first seven hypotheses, a half of the ecliptic would coincide with the half of the equator nearest to it; whenever this happened, days and nights would be equal in all places of the earth, and the different seasons of the year would fail. By the last [that is, the eighth] hypothesis[^251.1], however, there would follow only an increase or a decrease in the altitudes [of the sun] and in the lengths of the days and the nights».

The rule for finding the **declination** of the *sun*, or of any degree of the ecliptic, follows from Ptolemy's construction, *Almag.* I, 12 (ed. Halma, vol. I, pp. 56–58). Let ε be the obliquity of the ecliptic, δ the declination of the given degree, L the longitude of the sun or of the given degree; by the well-known theorem of Menelaus Ptolemy proves
\[ \frac{\text{chord}\,180^\circ}{\text{chord}\,2\varepsilon} = \frac{\text{chord}\,180^\circ}{\text{chord}\,2\delta} \times \frac{\text{chord}\,2L}{\text{chord}\,180^\circ}. \]
Since chord 180° is the diameter (2R), if sines are used instead of chords, there results
\[ \frac{2R}{2\sin\varepsilon} = \frac{2R}{2\sin\delta} \times \frac{2\sin L}{2R}, \]
whence al-Battānī's formula[^251.2]
\[ \sin\delta = \frac{\sin L\,\sin\varepsilon}{R}. \]

With Ptolemy and al-Battānī the radius is of sixty parts; in our trigonometric formulas, on the other hand, $R = 1$ is taken, so that in our usage the author's rule becomes $\sin\delta = \sin L \sin\varepsilon$.

Likewise in the tables of the declinations of the sun, under the columns of the degrees of the ecliptic, Theon, I, 144, writes:
