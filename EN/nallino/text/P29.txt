216: In the first half of the second century AD, Theon of Smyrna (by no means to be confused with Theon of Alexandria) puts the sidereal year, which he does not distinguish from the tropical, at $365\tfrac{1}{4}$ days, and the anomalistic year, in which the Sun returns to the apogee, at $365\tfrac{1}{2}$. Denying, then, the motion of precession, he plainly thinks that the solar apogee runs through the whole zodiac in 1461 years, that is, that it moves $27'\,6\tfrac{1}{2}''$ a year[^n]! Where he took these absurdities from we do not know; an error in the Greek text cannot be supposed, for, as Martin observes, p. 110, the author himself adds that the Sun returns to the same point of height, that is, to the same distance from the Earth, at the same hour of the day every *two years*[^83.1]. Martin, loc. cit., therefore judges rightly: «But see, Theon of Smyrna had already asserted a motion of the solar apogee in the zodiac with respect to the equinoxes and the stars, and had indeed made it so much faster than it should be that it would have been better for him to ignore it or deny it than to believe such a thing».
@n The whole zodiac in 1461 years is 360°/1461 = 14′ 47″ a year, which is also the Sun's motion in the quarter of a day by which Theon's anomalistic year exceeds his sidereal year; at 27′ 6½″ a year the apogee would go round in about 797 years.

242, n. 2: I had explained the origin of the name *Dhū l-Qarnayn* on the authority of several very learned men, especially since Clement of Alexandria complains bitterly that Alexander had himself portrayed in the image of a god, furnished with horns. The true origin of the name is now made clear by Fr. Kampers, *Alexander der Grosse und die Idee des Weltimperiums in Prophetie und Sage*, Freiburg i. Br. 1901, pp. 71–85 (= Studien und Darstellungen aus dem Gebiete der Geschichte hrsg. von H. Grauert, fasc. II–III). In the fabulous stories about Alexander that circulated among the Orientals, especially the Syrians, in the first centuries of the Christian era, Alexander is presented as the restorer of a new age and the saviour of the world, like the Messiah of the Hebrews; and so two horns were attributed to him, which in the Jewish writings are called the badge of the Messiah. Everything flows from very ancient Babylonian beliefs, which may be read, briefly set out, in the book of E. Schrader, *Die Keilinschriften und das Alte Testament, 3. Auflage neu bearbeitet von* H. Zimmern *und* H. Winckler, Berlin 1902, pp. 380–82; for the same reason, 25 centuries before Alexander, Naram-Sin, king of Babylonia, had been portrayed furnished with two horns.

266, n. 1: A very fine example of an uncertain case is in Aḥmad ibn Khālid al-Nāṣirī al-Salāwī, *Al-istiqṣāʾ li-akhbār duwal al-Maghrib al-aqṣā*, Cairo 1312 H, vol. IV, p. 245. In the night between the 28th and the 29th day of the month of Ramaḍān 1292 (28–29 Oct. 1875), twelve men in the city of Rabāṭ in the empire of Morocco say that they have seen the new Moon after sunset with complete certainty. The qāḍī of the city accepts their testimony and at once writes to the sultan of Morocco, who was then staying near the city. At the beginning of the 29th day everyone stops fasting. The astronomers (*falakiyyūn*) of the court, however, assert that that day can in no way be a feast; and so doubt arises among the people. In fact, after sunset on the 29th day everyone looks for the new Moon in the sky in vain, though the air is very clear; so they fast again until the night of the 30th day (by our way of reckoning), when the new Moon is at last seen. The fraud of those witnesses becomes clear from this, and they are thrown into prison.[^n]
