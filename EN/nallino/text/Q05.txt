According to Assemani's *Catalogue*, no. 292, another copy of Naṣīr al-Dīn's recension is kept in the Laurentian Library. This is false; for that codex, now «Orient. no. 94», which lacks its beginning and consists of 184 leaves written in the Maghribī script, is a very full *commentary* on the *Centiloquium* (Καρπός). But the commentator did not keep the original order: he arranged the sentences of the Centiloquium, distributed over eight treatises (*maqāla*), so that those belonging to the same part of astrology stand together. Each sentence always has its ordinal number written above it, preceded by the letter آ (standing, it seems, for في الاصل, «in the original»). The commentator's words are always introduced by the phrase قال المفسّر, «the commentator says». Only the last six treatises survive complete in the codex; the third begins thus, f. 24r: {ar:المقالة الثالثة في جمل من القول على المسائل وهي يه كلمة} {ar:قال بطلميوس}[^430.1] {ar:موضع اقوى الادلة في المسئلة يدل على ما في ضمير السائل} {ar:قال المفسر من هنا اخذ بطلميوس في ذكر المسائل وهي من الامور التي كثر الاختلاف فيما بين القدماء ...}[^n]. — Treatise IV (f. 46r) is {ar:في تحويل سني العالم وكيفية تغيير الاهوية بالانواء والامطار وهي و كلمات}; treatise V (f. 56v) {ar:في تاثير الكسوف ودلائل الكواكب ذوات الذوائب والنيازك وهي د كلمات}; treatise VIII (f. 173r) {ar:في البحارين والمرضى والادوية المسهلة وهي ثماني كلمات}[^n]. Who the author of this commentary is I do not know; besides Ptolemy himself he seems to mention no other writer. The text of the Centiloquium agrees very well with the one that Ibn al-Dāya had before his eyes in writing his commentary.

Pseudo-Abū Maʿshar = *Kitāb al-muḥaqqiq al-mudaqqiq al-yūnānī al-faylasūf al-shahīr bi-Abī Maʿshar al-falakī al-kabīr*, Cairo (al-Ḥamīdiyya press), without date (about 1320 H), 121 + 2 pp., in duodecimo.
@p 0430b019..0430b027
A pseudepigraphic book, whose last redaction is not earlier than the sixteenth century AD, since in some passages (pp. 63, 81, 92, etc.) women are mentioned who like to drink the drink of Arabian coffee (شرب القهوة) in gardens. The redactor seems to be an Egyptian, for he uses only the Coptic months, and (pp. 2–4) sets out prognostications about the flooding of the Nile. — After a few preliminaries on the prophecies to be taken from the first day of the year, on the natures of the planets, the days and the hours, and on the computation of the victor and the vanquished, it lists the prognostications to be taken from the ascendant of a nativity according to the decan (*wajh*) that it occupies in a given sign of the zodiac: first for men (pp. 10–61), then for women (pp. 62–118). There follow twelve amulets (*ḥijāb*, pl. *ḥujub*) of Solomon, handed down by the same Abū Maʿshar. Pictures of the signs of the zodiac are not lacking.
@p 0430b028..0430b034
Although it has the same opening[^430.2][^n], it differs from the book *Bughyat al-ṭālib fī maʿrifat al-ḍamīr li-l-maṭlūb wa-l-ṭālib*, which is attributed to the same Abū Maʿshar and likewise teaches the prognostications for men and women according to the sign (not the decan) occupied by the ascendant of the nativity. This little book, the *Bughyat al-ṭālib*, furnished with pictures of the signs like those of the other book, has been printed several times by lithography (Cairo 1280 H, 1287, 1316). Similar little books, falsely attributed to the same Abū Maʿshar, are in Ahlwardt, V, 278–79, nos. 5881 and 5882.

@p 0430b036..0430b040 + 0431b001..0431b002
*Tāj al-azyāj wa-ghunyat al-muḥtāj*, Escorial codex no. 932 (in Casiri, vol. I, pp. 368–69, no. 927), in which the author is called Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn Abī l-Shukr al-Maghrabī. It is written in the Maghribī script, 22 cm high and 14 cm wide, and has 119 leaves of eighteen lines each. F. 2v–59r give the text, divided into 100 chapters (*bāb*); f. 60v–119v the tables. F. 66v–70v are the tables of the longitudes and latitudes of 146 cities, in increasing order of their distances from the meridian of the Fortunate Isles (*al-jazāʾir al-khālidāt*); for each city the longitude, the latitude, the region (*al-amākin*) and the climate are given.
@p 0431b003..0431b026
The title of the book[^431.1] and the author are not known from elsewhere. Suter, p. 156, therefore wonders whether part of the name has dropped out through the copyist's carelessness, and whether the true author was Yaḥyā ibn Muḥ. ibn Abī l-Shukr al-Maghrabī Muḥyī al-Dīn, the eminent astronomer and astrologer attached to the observatory of the city of Marāgha[^431.2] in the time of Naṣīr al-Dīn and after his death. The thing is indeed easy; but the book itself and the elements of its tables would have to be weighed most carefully. I shall make some observations, relying on the geographical part of the book, which alone I had the opportunity to examine. The longitudes and latitudes of those 146 cities very often disagree with those that Naṣīr al-Dīn took into the *Īlkhānī Tables*, which list 253 places[^431.3]; they agree far better, especially as regards Persia, with the numbers excerpted by Abū l-Fidāʾ from the great work of al-Bīrūnī. Of the cities mentioned in the *Tāj*, 61, almost half, are missing in Naṣīr al-Dīn, who on the other hand has 168 places left out in the *Tāj*. Of the places of western Africa and Spain Naṣīr al-Dīn gives nine, namely Sūs, Upper Tāhart, Lower Tāhart, Sijilmāsa, Fās, al-Qayrawān, al-Mahdiyya, Tripoli and Córdoba; of these the *Tāj* leaves out the first four, while from those regions it mentions Silves, Santarem, Tangier, Fās, Ghāna, Sabta (Ceuta), Seville, Málaga, Córdoba, Almería, Granada, Toledo, Murcia, Zaragoza, Valencia, Bijāya (Bougie), Tunis, al-Qayrawān, al-Mahdiyya, Kūkawa (كوكو), Qābes and Tripoli. From the Persian province of Ādharbayjān Naṣīr al-Dīn adduces ten cities, namely Salamās, Marand, Siyāh-kūh, Awjān, Khuwayy, Nakhčewān, Tebrīz, Urmiya, Marāgha and Ardebīl; the *Tāj* none at all, not even Marāgha! — Moreover the numbers in the *Tāj* disagree entirely with the numbers established by Aboul Hhassan[^431.4] and by Ibn Saʿīd al-Maghrabī[^431.5]; the author used a source older than these two writers. — It is therefore more prudent not to correct the name written in the codex, and to attribute the book to the *father* of that well-known Yaḥyā. But if Suter's conjecture were true, Yaḥyā would have composed the book before he moved from Spain to Persia.
