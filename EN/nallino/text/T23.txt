@p 0684b015

@p 0684b016..0684b022
*The parallaxes of the sun.* As the author himself points out, they are taken from the *Almagest*, V, 18 (ed. Halma, vol. I, p. 359)[^684.2]; but, because he accepted a different distance of the sun, al-Battānī bids us always add to the numbers of the table an eighteenth part of themselves; see vol. I, pp. 257–58, and p. XXXVIII, note 3. The argument is the true (or geocentric) zenith distance *z*; the parallaxes are computed by the formula \[ \tan \text{parall. in altitude} = \frac{\sin\pi\,\sin z}{1 - \sin\pi\,\cos z}\,, \qquad \text{where} \quad \sin\pi = \frac{\text{radius of the earth}}{\text{distance from the earth}}\,. \] Ptolemy made the computations as if the *sine* of the parallax were found instead of the *tangent*, which in such small angles produces no sensible error. Moreover, the variations of the distance of the sun are neglected.

*The parallaxes of the moon.* They are taken from the *Almagest*, loc. cit.; but in the third and the fourth term al-Battānī corrected Ptolemy's rougher numbers somewhat. The argument of the four columns that give the terms is the *true zenith distance of the moon*.
@p 0684b026..0684b035
The variations of the distance from the earth, of small importance for the sun, cannot be neglected for the moon in computing the parallax in altitude. Ptolemy[^684.3] chose four principal distances, or four terms, as follows. He put the apogee distance of the centre of the epicycle, or of the moon, at 59ᵖ, the radius of the earth being taken as 1 part, and the perigee distance at 38ᵖ 43′; and since on this reckoning the radius of the epicycle is reckoned at 5ᵖ 10′, he made the first term 59ᵖ + 5ᵖ 10′ = 64ᵖ 10′ (i.e. with the moon in the apogee of the eccentric and of the epicycle at once), the second term 59ᵖ − 5ᵖ 10′ = 53ᵖ 50′ (i.e. with the moon in the apogee of the eccentric but in the perigee of the epicycle), the third term 38ᵖ 43′ + 5ᵖ 10′ = 43ᵖ 53′ (with the moon in the perigee of the eccentric but at the apogee of the epicycle), and the fourth term 38ᵖ 43′ − 5ᵖ 10′ = 33ᵖ 33′ (with the moon placed in the perigee of the eccentric and of the epicycle at once). At these four distances from the earth the parallaxes (for 90° of true zenith distance) will be[^684.4]:

From these horizontal parallaxes π, the parallaxes in altitude answering to the true zenith distance *z* are derived for each term by the formula mentioned above for the sun. The table gives the *parallaxes* of the first term (col. III) and of the third (col. V), and also the *difference* between the second and the first (col. IV) and between the fourth and the third (col. VI), for every second degree of the true zenith distance of the moon.
@p 0685b010..0685b017 + 0685b018..0685b031
The three remaining columns serve to compute the parallaxes at the intermediate distances from the earth, and this by proportional sixtieths. — Ptolemy assumes that the variations of the parallaxes are very nearly proportional to the variations of the distance from the earth; and he considers that these distances vary because of the anomalistic motion of the moon on the circumference of the epicycle and because of the motion of the centre of the epicycle on the circumference of the eccentric. When the centre of the epicycle occupies the apogee A of the eccentric, let the true anomaly, or the elongation of the moon from the true apogee H of the epicycle, be HG = 60°; then[^685.1], by the formula with which the distance of the moon from the earth in the syzygies is computed (see vol. I, pp. 253–54), EG is found to be 62ᵖ 48′ of the parts of which EH (the first term) is 65ᵖ 15′, EK (the second term) 54ᵖ 45′, and KH (the difference between the second and the first term) 10ᵖ 30′. So the distance EG differs from the first term by −2ᵖ 27′; and if the whole difference KH is taken as unity (1ᵖ = 60′), those 2ᵖ 27′ become $60' \times 2^{p}\,27' : 10^{p}\,30' =$ 14′. Hence it is clear that, at the apogee distance of the centre of the epicycle and with the moon at the true anomaly 60°, 14 sixtieths of the difference between the first and the second term for the same zenith distance must be added to the first term (answering to the given zenith distance). — One must proceed in a similar way when the centre of the epicycle holds the perigee of the eccentric[^685.2], that is, when the distance of the moon from the earth lies between the third and the fourth term. — All this, computed for every second degree of the *true anomaly*, Ptolemy wrote in col. VII (with the centre of the epicycle in the apogee of the eccentric) and in col. VIII (with the centre of the epicycle in the perigee of the eccentric). But the argument of both columns is *half* the true anomaly, not the whole anomaly; so those 14′ will be found under 30°, not under 60°. Ptolemy did this because he had also halved the argument of col. IX, so that one would have to enter the table not with the double elongation but with the simple one.

These corrections suffice when the centre of the epicycle holds the apogee or the perigee of the eccentric. At the other points a new correction must be applied, because of the different distance of the centre of the epicycle from the earth. Let the mean double elongation of the moon from the sun be 60 degrees, that is, let the centre of the epicycle be taken to have completed 30° of the eccentric from the apogee of the eccentric[^n]; from the formula with which the geocentric distances outside the syzygies are computed (vol. I, pp. 255–56) one gathers that the distance of the moon from the centre of the earth is then 54ᵖ 3′ of the parts of which the apogee distance is 60ᵖ and the perigee distance 39ᵖ 22′, their difference being 20ᵖ 38′. So the apogee distance will exceed the given distance by 5ᵖ 57′, which, if the whole difference 20ᵖ 38′ is taken as unity (1ᵖ = 60′), become $60' \times 5^{p}\,57' : 20^{p}\,38' =$ 17′ 18″. These are found written in col. IX under 30°, and they serve to correct the parallax found by the earlier columns. So the argument of col. IX is the *simple mean elongation* of the moon from the sun.

Hence the use of the tables is clear. Enter the table with the true zenith distance of the moon, and take the parallax of the first and of the third term (cols. III and V) with the adjacent differences (cols. IV and VI). Find the corrected anomaly of the moon or, if it exceeds 180°, its complement to 360°; entering the table with half of it, take the sixtieths of cols. VII and VIII. Multiply the difference taken from col. IV by the sixtieths of col. VII, and the difference of col. VI by the sixtieths of col. VIII; add these proportional parts to the parallaxes of cols. III and V. Two parallaxes will result; compute their difference. Then find the mean elongation of the moon from the sun or from the point diametrically opposite, and entering the table with it take the sixtieths of col. IX, by which multiply the difference of the parallaxes found before; add the product to the smaller of the two parallaxes; the sum will be the parallax sought.
