The *era of Alexander Dhū l-Qarnayn*[^331.2] is the *Seleucid era*, whose epoch is commonly Monday 1 October 312 BC; but in al-Battānī, as I noted sufficiently on p. 67, note 1, it is *Saturday 1 September 312*. — See also Barhebraeus, *Syriac Astronomy*, p. 191 of the text: «The Greeks, in the time of paganism, began the year from the first Teshrī [October]; after they had accepted Christianity, from the month Ēlūl (= September)».
@f 331.2 0331nl004..0331nr003
That is, the Two-Horned. The name is in the *Qurʾān* XVIII, 82 ff.; it arose from the images in which Alexander the Great, as son of Jupiter Ammon, was portrayed with two horns.
@n XVIII, 82 is Flügel's numbering of the verse, 83 in the Cairo numbering now usual. Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part I, p. LXXIV) add that he had given this explanation after several scholars, chiefly because Clement of Alexandria complains that Alexander had himself portrayed in the likeness of a god, with horns; the true origin of the name is shown by Fr. Kampers, *Alexander der Grosse und die Idee des Weltimperiums in Prophetie und Sage*, Freiburg i. Br. 1901, pp. 71–85: in the legends of Alexander current in the East, above all among the Syrians, in the first centuries of the Christian era, he appears as the restorer of a new age and the saviour of the world, like the Messiah of the Hebrews, and is therefore given the two horns that Jewish writings call the emblem of the Messiah. All this flows from very ancient Babylonian beliefs (E. Schrader, *Die Keilinschriften und das Alte Testament*, 3rd ed. by H. Zimmern and H. Winckler, Berlin 1902, pp. 380–82), for which Naram-Sin, king of Babylonia, was already portrayed with two horns 25 centuries before Alexander. The Addenda are translated with Part I's front matter.
@p 0331b034..0331b036 + 0332b001..0332b014
The *era of Alexander of Macedon*, which al-Battānī mostly calls the *era from the death of Alexander*, is the one on which the *Handy Tables* (κανόνες πρόχειροι) of Ptolemy and Theon were built, which count the mean celestial motions «from the first year of Philip, son of Alexander[^n], the founder of the city [of Alexandria]»[^332.1]. It is the *era of Philip Aridaeus* (or Arrhidaeus), the successor, as they say, of Alexander the Great, whose brother by the same mother he was[^n]; and so the Arabs mostly call it the *era of Philip*. — Years of this era are sometimes mentioned in the *Almagest*, where the observations of Hipparchus are treated; they are *vague* years there (that is, of 365 days, without the Julian intercalation), counted «from the death of Alexander», ἀπὸ τῆς Ἀλεξάνδρου τελευτῆς, and their epoch is Sunday 1 Thoth 425 of Nabonassar, that is, 12 November 324 BC. The same epoch, and with *vague* years too (whether Persian or Egyptian), is kept by al-Farghānī, Ḥabash, al-Masʿūdī, al-Bīrūnī, Ibn Yūnus, Kūshyār al-Jīlī and the rest; and so they put the interval between the Seleucid era and the era of Philip at 4341 days, that is, 11 vague years, 10 months and 26 days[^332.2]. — But in Theon's *Handy Tables*, following an Alexandrian usage that arose in the first century AD, the years are *Julian*, and the *epoch of the era is 29 August 324* BC; al-Battānī (see p. 69, ll. 20–22) follows Theon, and so he puts the interval between the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn (counted from 1 September, not from 1 October) and the era of Alexander of Macedon at 12 Julian years less 3 days.
@n The Greek of note 1, Φιλίππου τοῦ μετ’ Ἀλέξανδρον τὸν κτίστην, says «of Philip, who came after Alexander the founder»; Philip was not Alexander's son.
@n Philip Arrhidaeus was the son of Philip II by Philinna of Larissa, Alexander's half-brother by the same father; «uterinus», by the same mother, does not fit.

@p 0333b002..0333b007
*The era of the Copts.* — Among the other Arabic writers known to me three eras are designated by this name: 1) the era of Nabonassar, 2) the era of Philip Aridaeus, 3) the era of Diocletian or of the Martyrs[^333.1]. But al-Battānī means none of these. From what he relates later in this chapter and in ch. XLII it is plain that it is an era with *Julian* years, whose epoch is Friday 29 August 25 BC, for it is 287 Julian years less 3 days distant from the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn.
@f 333.1 0333nl001..0333nl010
The era of Nabonassar and the era of Philip are in al-Farghānī, ed. Golius, ch. I, p. 6; that of Diocletian in Ḥabash f. 4v, Ibn Yūnus (Delambre 96) and al-Maqrīzī (*al-Mawāʿiẓ wa-l-iʿtibār fī dhikr al-khiṭaṭ wa-l-āthār*, Būlāq 1270 H, vol. I, pp. 261–262); the era of Nabonassar in al-Masʿūdī, *Prairies* vol. III, p. 400; those of Nabonassar, Philip and Diocletian in al-Masʿūdī, *Tanbīh* p. 198. — Abū l-Faẓl ʿAllāmī says only that it is ancient (vol. I, p. 274 = transl. II, 23).
@p 0333b008..0333b012
It is, then, a form of the *era of Augustus* or *of Actium*. Ptolemy mentions this era incidentally, *Almag.* III, 6 (ed. Halma vol. I, p. 204), from which passage Ideler, *Handbuch der mathematischen und technischen Chronologie*, Berlin 1825–26, vol. I, p. 116, deduced that its epoch was 1 Thoth 719 of Nabonassar, that is, Sunday 31 August 30 BC, and that its years were vague Egyptian years[^333.2].
@f 333.2 0333nl011..0333nr005
Petavius and the book *L’art de vérifier les dates* have 29 Aug. 30 BC, because they wrongly think that the years of the era were Julian years from the beginning. — To the first year of Augustus Ptolemy refers the mean places of the sun, the moon and the planets in the *Canobic Inscription*, which follows the Hypotheses (*Hypothèses de C. Ptolémée, publiées et traduites par Halma*, Paris 1820, p. 59).
@p 0333b013..0333b019
The difference that al-Battānī has in the epoch of the era must, I think, be explained in this way. Theon relates in the *Handy Tables* (ed. Halma, vol. I, pp. 30–32; see Ideler I, 158 ff.) that the first day of the month Thoth of the vague Egyptian year coincides again with the first day of Thoth of the fixed Alexandrian (or Julian) year after a period of 1460 fixed years, or 1461 vague years; and that this happened «*in the fifth year of Augustus*» (so on p. 30), or «*five years after the beginning of the reign of Augustus*» (so on p. 32). For this reason, Theon adds, one starts from this fifth year when days of the vague year have to be turned into days of the fixed Alexandrian year.
@p 0333b020..0333b026
Theon's passage gives two pieces of information that disagree with each other. For if that period was completed in the fifth year of Augustus, the epoch of Theon's era becomes *Friday 30 August 26* BC[^n]; and so Mommsen, Lepsius and Th. H. Martin[^333.3] think it was, relying on many historical arguments. But if the period was completed 5 years after the beginning of the reign of Augustus, the epoch of the era becomes *Friday 29 Aug. 25* BC; and in this way, but not well, Halma, Biot and Letronne among the moderns, and al-Battānī among the ancients, understood it. The years of this era are Julian.
@n 30 August 26 BC was a Thursday; 29 August 25 BC, 365 days later, was a Friday.
@f 333.3 0333nr006..0333nr013
Who treated the question at length in his *Sur la date historique d’un renouvellement de la période sothiaque*, pp. 239–244 and 293–294 (in Mémoires présentés par divers savants à l’Académie des Inscriptions, 1st series, vol. VIII, part 1, 1869). R. Schram follows this opinion implicitly, *Hilfstafeln für Chronologie* (in Denkschriften der kais. Akad. der Wissenschaften zu Wien, mathem. Cl., vol. XLV, 1892).
@p 0333b027..0333b029
So the Copts who used this era (which should be called *Alexandrian* rather than *of Augustus*), beginning the year on 29 August, preceded by three days, as al-Battānī says, the Greek-Egyptians, whose first day of the year was 1 September.
@p 0333b030..0333b032 + 0334b001..0334b007
Al-Battānī adds that in every fourth year the difference is not of *three* but only of *two* days: «they [i.e. the Greek-Egyptians] in every fourth year run ahead of them [i.e. the Copts] by one day». I find the explanation of this in a chronological fragment of the emperor Heraclius that Halma edited (*Commentaires de Théon sur les tables manuelles*, vol. III, p. 106). For it relates that «the Alexandrians put the intercalary day at the end of the year that precedes the Roman leap year; then, therefore, they begin their year not on the third but only on the second day before September». Al-Battānī plainly means this usage, which is set out more fully by al-Bīrūnī, *Chron.*, pp. 141 and 196 of the text (137 and 176 of the translation), and is still in force among the Copts today; so, after the Coptic intercalary year, up to the end of February of the Gregorian leap year, the correspondence of the months changes by one day[^334.1]:

Martin, loc. cit., pp. 282–283, explains the origin of the Alexandrian usage in this way: it is probable that the Alexandrian tetraëteris, or four-year cycle, imitated a Sothiac four-year cycle that perhaps had its fourth year as the leap year; and that at the time when the fixed Alexandrian year was established, the Sothiac leap year was the third year of the Julian four-year cycle.
@p 0334b019..0334b031
The era of Augustus of whatever kind, the Ptolemaic as well as the Theonian, was in force in Egypt itself for no long time. So it is rarely mentioned by Oriental writers, and not always rightly, it seems. From what Ḥabash, ff. 3r and 5r, and al-Bīrūnī, *Chron.*, pp. 29, 137, 141 and 196 of the text (33, 133, 137, 176 of the translation), say about the era of Augustus (*taʾrīkh Oghusṭos*), it is plain that they interpreted Theon's passage in the same way as al-Battānī, and put the epoch of the era on 29 Aug. 25 BC. But Kūshyār al-Jīlī[^334.2], Ibn Yūnus[^334.3], al-Khāzinī (*al-Zīj al-Sanjarī*, book I, section I, ch. II, f. 22v) and al-Kharaqī (treatise III, ch. VI, Paris codex f. 118v) reckon the era of Augustus in such a way that its epoch is deduced to be, beyond doubt, Thursday 13 November 30 BC; so it does not even agree with Ptolemy's Sunday 31 Aug. 30 BC. The cause of this difference I do not know. Abū l-Faẓl ʿAllāmī (*Āyīn-i Akbarī*, vol. I, p. 275 = II, 25 of the translation) says only that the era of Augustus begins at the beginning of the reign (that is, in 30 BC), and that its years are Julian, but its months Coptic.

@p 0334b033..0334b037
*To compute on what day of the Roman year of the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn (or of the Seleucids) the first day of a given Persian year* (according to the era of Yazdegerd) *falls.* — From 366, says al-Battānī, let there be taken 77 days + as many days as there are Roman years in a quarter of the years of the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn elapsed since the epoch of Yazdegerd; let what remains from the subtraction be counted, beginning from the first day of Aylūl (September); the first day of the Persian year will come out.
@p 0335b001..0335b004
The rule will serve until the remainder is found to be 0, that is, until the equation $366 = 77 + \frac{x}{4}$ arises, from which is deduced $\frac{x}{4} = 366 - 77 = 289$, and $x = 289 \times 4 = 1156$ Julian years. So the rule can be used up to the first day of the Julian year 1156 of Yazdegerd = 1788, which was amply sufficient for al-Battānī's readers.
@p 0335b005..0335b007
Once 1156 Roman years have elapsed after Yazdegerd, that is, when the remainder $366 - \left(77 + \frac{x}{4}\right)$ is negative, one must not go forward from 1 Aylūl (September) with this remainder, but back.
