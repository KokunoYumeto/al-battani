On pp. 84–87: the mean syzygies for the Roman years and months
@p 0676b011
The tables are mentioned in vol. I, p. 92, ch. XLII.
@p 0676b012..0676b016
Earlier, on pp. 29–32, he had given the mean syzygies for the wandering, or Egyptian, years; now he sets them out for the Roman, or Julian, years of the era of Dhū l-qarnayn. But one must not forget what I said above, on pp. 222–23, and in vol. I, p. 246: that in his tables al-Battānī uses a *fictitious* Roman *year*, which begins on 1 Ādhār (March) of the *chronological year already begun*; so the months from September to the end of February belong in the tables to the earlier year, as this survey will show:

Likewise the numbers of the *other columns* for the collected years differ from those derived from the tables of the mean motions. For[^678.1]
@f 678.1 0678nl001..0678nr003
The tables of the mean motions give not the motion of the moon in latitude but the retrograde motion of the node; if the motion in longitude is added to it, the motion in latitude (ἡ κατὰ πλάτος κίνησις) results. And in the conjunctions, plainly, the longitude of the moon does not differ from the longitude of the sun.

@p 0679b001..0679b006
**Pp. 84–85**, for the *collected years*. Given the first line of numbers, the other numbers are found by always adding 4ᵈ 35′ 11″ in the column of the days, and in the other columns the mean motions answering to this interval of time; but if the sum of the days exceeds a whole lunation (29ᵈ 31′ 50″), the new moon found will be the second new moon of the month Ādhār or the first of the following month, so that a whole lunation must be taken from the sum, and from the motions of the other columns the motions answering to a lunation.
@p 0679b007..0679b010
**P. 86**, for the *single years*. Let A be the moment at which the first new moon of Ādhār occurred in any year; in the following year the new moon of Ādhār will be A + 18ᵈ 53′ 52″ if the following year is common, or A − 1ᵈ + 18ᵈ 53′ 52″ if the following year is a leap year; as always, when the sum exceeds the length of a lunation (29ᵈ 31′ 50″), the lunation and the motions answering to it must be taken away. —
@p 0679b011..0679b030
**[**For year 11 the codex gives 28ᵈ 9′ 39″, 358° 32′ 6″, 271° 4′ 19″, 211° 11′ 43″; for year 22 the codex has 26ᵈ 19′ 18″, 357° 4′ 18″, 182° 8′ 39″, 62° 23′ 27″. The inept interpolator took these numbers from the table on p. 32, not understanding the difference between the table for the wandering years and the table for the Julian years. For, because of the leap years, the whole days of the second column in the table of the Roman years lose a day every fourth year with respect to the table of the Egyptian years; that is, the beginning of the month, which in the first year was one and the same in both tables, shows a difference of one day every fourth year, of two days after eight years, of three after twelve years, and so on. So it can happen that the new moon of Thōth of the Egyptian table does not fall in the month Ādhār of the Roman table but in the month Subāṭ (February). This happens in the eleventh year of the tables. In the Egyptian tables, adding 18ᵈ 53′ 52″ to 11ᵈ 47′ 37″, one gets 30ᵈ 41′ 29″ for the new moon of Thōth, plainly exceeding the days of Thōth; so, a lunation being taken away, the new moon of Thōth 1ᵈ 9′ 39″ remains (the 29″ is a misprint of the compositor). But in the Roman calendar this new moon belongs to Subāṭ (February); for to the 9ᵈ 47′ 37″ of the Roman table must be added, because of the following leap year, 17ᵈ 53′ 52″, whence Ādhār 28ᵈ 41′ 29″ results[^n], from which nothing is to be taken away. — The case is similar in year 22. These differences would become ever more frequent if the tables were carried beyond 24 years; after 120 years Thōth would leave the month Ādhār, or March, altogether (with which at the beginning it had the first day and the new moon in common), so that the moons of Ādhār would all become different from the moons of Thōth. Not noticing this, the interpolator had wrongly thought that the Roman table had to be corrected after the Egyptian one and Ptolemy[^679.1], although he had kept the whole days of the second column as they arose from the leap years. — Schiaparelli.**]**
