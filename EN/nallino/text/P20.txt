Up to here everything agrees with the *Arabic* text and my version (as far as p. 139, l. 37); but the Marcian and Vatican codices *go on thus*: «Ac illa eius puncta 2. circuli signorum poli dicuntur. Quapropter signorum circulus a circulo directo secundum quantitatem inter 2. polos positam declinabit, eruntque in spera 4. poli, quorum bini et bini super spere dyametrum in opposito continentur. Erunt etiam in ea 2. circuli qui speram in 2. equalia et se se super 2. punctos sibimet oppositos secabunt; qui circuli sunt primus et secundus circulorum» (and those two points of it are called the poles of the circle of the signs. Therefore the circle of the signs will decline from the straight circle by the amount set between the two poles, and there will be four poles on the sphere, held two by two opposite each other on a diameter of the sphere. There will also be on it two circles that will cut the sphere into two equal parts and each other at two points opposite each other; these circles are the first and the second of the circles). In this way their discrepancy from the Arabic text of al-Battānī, which they far exceed in length, keeps growing. When the description of the *globe* is finished (cf. p. 140, l. 7 of my version), those codices (M f. 83va, towards the end) have[^62.1]: «Hijs omnibus itaque peractis, eneum circulum plane superficiei, cuius latitudo sit unius digiti, grossitudo vero sit mediocris, ita tamen quod nusquam curvari aut alterari possit constituas; intra quem supradictam speram, ita quod spere superficies interiori circuli superficiei quasi adhereat et sine difficultate circumvolvatur eumque in omni parte contingat, adaptabis. Idem vero circulus ab exteriori parte cum circino rotundetur; cuius superficiem in 4. equales partes, et unamquamque quartarum in 90., partiaris, numerumque partium in tertia circuli parte interiori scribas; et intervallorum divisiones ad exteriora protrahens, in unaquaque quarta 18. intervalla reperies. Postea ex 4. punctis quartarum 2. sibimet opposita puncta polos constituas; a quibus numerare incipiens, numerorum litteras que sunt 5., 10., etc. in singulis intervallis inscribas...» (when all these things have been done, then, set up a bronze circle of flat surface, whose breadth is one finger and whose thickness is moderate, yet such that it can nowhere be bent or altered; inside it you will fit the sphere described above, so that the surface of the sphere almost touches the inner surface of the circle, turns round without difficulty and touches it on every side. Let the same circle be rounded off on the outside with the compasses; divide its surface into four equal parts, and each of the quarters into 90, and write the number of the parts on the inner third of the circle; and drawing the divisions of the intervals out to the outside, you will find 18 intervals in each quarter. Then of the four points of the quarters make two points opposite each other the poles; beginning to count from them, write the letters of the numbers, which are 5, 10, etc., in the single intervals...). This first ring is to be joined to the globe by two nails to be fixed in the poles of the ecliptic, so that it can turn lightly about those nails; it is plainly the *ring of the solstitial colure*, on which the latitudes of the fixed stars are counted.

Then, observing the latitude of the star carefully, count according to the amount of its latitude on the part of the bronze circle from the place of the star towards the side of the latitude, and where the counting ends you will mark a point on the sphere, for it will be the centre of that star. About it you will describe a small circle with the compasses; this you are to set out among the stars of the six magnitudes, so that the circles of the stars of the same magnitude are equal; for some of the stars are greater than certain other stars. It is clear, therefore, that the instrument is not al-Battānī's *egg* but Ptolemy's *solid sphere* (στερεὰ σφαῖρα) (cf. p. 320, n. 2). The codices then go on thus (M f. 84va): «Hanc autem eandem speram si aliter operari volueris ut per eam altitudines et horas aliaque quam plurima cognoscere valeas, super dictum circulum eneum quem per 2. polos transire diximus facies 2. foramina 2. polorum.....» (but if you wish to make this same sphere otherwise, so that by it you may know the altitudes, the hours and very many other things, you will make on the said bronze circle, which we said passes through the two poles, two holes for the two poles.....). With a very great circuit of words they teach how *other rings* are constructed and fitted to the globe; from which arises something like Ptolemy's *spherical astrolabe* (ὄργανον ἀστρολάβον), on which p. 320, n. 1. The description seems to have been put together from *different sources*, for where the suspension of the instrument is dealt with there is this (M f. 85ra; V f. 144ra): «in huius autem una foraminis extremitate ex utraque parte quoddam signum imprimas. *In alio libro* inveni: quandam abscisionem facias in qua quemdam perpendicularem anulum anulo abstrolapsus [l. astrolabii] similem adaptabis, cui alterum iterum anulum aliquantulum maiorem, ad eius similem a quo pendes abstrolapsus, concatenabis; perpendicularis autem anuli summitatem polique dimidium perforando cum quodam rotundo clavo, ipsum anulum eidem polo similiter alligabis, ut cum speram manu volveris ad similitudinem abstrolapsus ab ipso pendeat.....» (and at one end of its hole impress a mark on both sides. *In another book* I have found: make a cut in which you will fit a perpendicular ring like the ring of the astrolabe [read astrolabii], to which you will link again another ring somewhat larger, like the one by which you hang the astrolabe; and piercing the top of the perpendicular ring and half of the pole with a round nail, you will likewise bind the ring itself to the same pole, so that when you turn the sphere with your hand it hangs from it like an astrolabe.....). They also show how the rising, setting and culminating degrees of the zodiac are found, and how the elapsed hours of the day, and the azimuth («cenit»), rising and setting of the Sun. This part, on the use of the instrument, ends: «Istorum circulorum grossitudines eiusdem quantitatis esse necesse est, ut unus alium non excedat; spera quoque tam caute stabiliri debet ut, cum per suspendiculares anulos suspensa fuerit, nusquam declinet, sed perpendiculariter pendere dicatur» (the thicknesses of these circles must be of the same amount, so that one does not exceed another; the sphere too must be set up so carefully that, when it has been hung by the suspending rings, it nowhere leans but may be said to hang perpendicularly). There follows a short description of the figure of the instrument: «Sit igitur ad spere similitudinem circulus *m n*, cuius polus in plano centrum ipsius est punctus *a*.....» (let there be, then, in the likeness of the sphere, the circle *m n*, whose pole in the plane, its centre, is the point *a*.....); the end is this: «Rotundus clavus qui perfecte [V: profunde] ingrediatur ut polus a suo loco non recedat infigatur. Et si volueris quoddam punctum alio in loco sub orizonte secundum libitum facias, hoc autem manifestum est quod perpendicularis loco [V: locus] cenit capitum designat, a quo circulus equinoctialis secundum poli septentrionalis elevationem ab orizonte versus meridiem elongabitur. et hec est figura subscripta» (let a round nail be fixed that goes in perfectly [V: deeply], so that the pole does not leave its place. When the codices and the editions are compared with one another and with the Arabic text, the following is gathered. Plato of Tivoli *translated all the chapters*[^64.1], *but probably left out the tables and the appendices*, which perhaps were mixed with the tables (cf. the notes, p. 322). The first part of the last chapter, in which the general considerations are set out, was translated by Plato and stands in its place in the Ambrosian codex; but in the Marcian and Vatican codices it is moved after the description of the quadrant and the triquetrum, and in the editions and other codices it is left out. The description of the armillary sphere, however, hard to understand and perhaps already much corrupted in Plato's Arabic copy, was restored most unhappily with the help of other books[^64.2], and does not answer to al-Battānī's meaning; it is left out in some codices and in the editions, and moved to the end of the book in the Ambrosian, Marcian and Vatican codices. — For the rest, the codices and the editions of Plato of Tivoli agree very well with one another, apart from small but not infrequent errors of the copyists and the compositors. The apogees of the planets, however, which, as I said on p. 114, n. 1, are set out in the editions rather inopportunely at the end of chapter XLVII, rightly end chapter XLV, as in the Arabic text, in the Marcian and Vatican codices (and perhaps in others).
