**§ 1. The life of al-Battānī.**
@p 0016b012..0016b016
I. *The Arabic witnesses to al-Battānī's life.* — Whoever would tell the life of al-Battānī has, even today, hardly any other Arabic sources to help him than those that D. Chwolsohn[^16.1] and M. Steinschneider[^16.2] used; but with new aids gathered, some of what the Arabs have handed down can be understood, or even emended, more correctly than those two most distinguished men managed to do. And al-Battānī's writings are now known to us far better and more surely.
@p 0017b001..0017b002
Two Arab writers above all have handed down information about al-Battānī to us, namely al-Nadīm[^17.1] and Ibn al-Qifṭī[^17.2]. They relate this[^17.3]:
@p 0017b003..0017b010
«Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān <al-Raqqī> al-Ḥarrānī, known [by the surname] al-Battānī, *one of the illustrious [men] in observing the stars, and of the leaders in the science of geometry and of astronomy, in the knowledge of the stars, in the art of astrology. He has a famous astronomical work*[^17.4], *in which are contained the observations of the Sun and the Moon made by him and the correction of their motions as laid down in the book of Ptolemy entitled the Almagest. In this [work] he sets out the motions of the five wandering stars, according to the corrections he was able to make, together with the other computations of the celestial sphere that are needed.*
@p 0017b011..0017b018 + 0018b001..0018b004
«*Part of his observations, which he mentions in the astronomical work, were in the year 267 of the hijra*[^17.5], *part in the year 287*[^17.6]. *No one is known in Islam who equalled him in observing the stars exactly and in investigating their motions.* A Sabian by origin, from [the city of] Ḥarrān. He began to observe, according to what Jaʿfar ibn al-Muktafī relates[^17.7], *[having it] from [al-Battānī] himself*[^17.8], in the year 264[^17.9], [continuing] until the year 306[^17.10]; he set the fixed stars in his astronomical work for the year 299[^17.11]. He came to Baghdād with [the people called] Banū l-Zayyāt, of the inhabitants of [the city of] al-Raqqa, to seek the return of the taxes exacted from them[^17.12] unjustly[^17.13]. Returning from there, he died on the way at Qaṣr al-Jiṣṣ, in the year 317[^17.14]. His works are these: The astronomical work, in two editions, <the first and the second; the second better than the first>. — The book <of knowing> the ascensions of the signs <in the spaces between the quarters of the celestial sphere>. — *The book*[^18.1] <of the verification> of the amounts of the applications; he composed it for Abū l-Ḥasan Ibn al-Furāt[^18.2]. — *The book of the commentary on the Tetrabiblos of Ptolemy*»[^18.3].
@p 0018b005..0018b008
What Ibn al-Qifṭī alone has comes, I think, from the universal history entitled *al-Taʿrīf bi-ṭabaqāt al-umam*, whose author is Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī[^18.4]; for Ḥājjī Khalīfa quotes from that work of Ṣāʿid the words from «a famous astronomical work» down to «computations of the celestial sphere»[^18.5].
@p 0018b009..0018b021 + 0019b001..0019b006
From the book of that al-Nadīm derives the life of al-Battānī that Ibn Khallikān took into his biographical lexicon[^18.6]: «Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān, of Ḥarrānian origin, *an inhabitant of al-Raqqa*[^18.7], al-Battānī, the famous calculator and astronomer, author of the astronomical work [called] the Sabian. He has admirable operations, and observations carried out most accurately. He began to observe in the year 264, [continuing] until the year 306; he set the fixed stars in his astronomical work for the year 299. He was unique in his age in his art; his operations show his great excellence and his vast learning. He died in the year 317, returning from Baghdād, at a place called Qaṣr al-Ḥaḍr[^18.8]. I have not found that he embraced the Islamic faith; but his name [Muḥammad] shows that he became a Muslim. His writings are these: The astronomical work, in two editions, the first and the second; the second better. — The book of knowing the ascensions of the signs in the spaces between the quarters of the celestial sphere. — The epistle on *the amount of the applications. — The book in which he explained*[^18.9] *the four quarters of the celestial sphere. — The epistle on*[^18.10] the verification of the amounts of the applications. — The commentary on the Tetrabiblos of Ptolemy. — And other [writings]. [The name] al-Battānī has the vowel *a* after the letter *B*; [but] Abū Muḥammad Hibat Allāh Ibn al-Akfānī[^19.1] asserts that the vowel is *i*. It is a name derived from B..ttān, a rural district (ناحية) of the territory (من أعمال) of Ḥarrān[^19.2] ..... In another history I have read that he entered Baghdād, then set out from it, and died on the way, in the year mentioned above, at Qaṣr al-Jiṣṣ[^19.3]. Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī says in his book entitled *al-Mushtarik*: Qaṣr al-Jiṣṣ, near Sāmarrā, one of the palaces built by al-Muʿtaṣim. — God most high knows [all things] better».
@p 0019b007..0019b017
Abū l-Fidāʾ, the famous writer of history and geography, who met his last day on 23 al-Muḥarram 732 (26 Oct. 1331), follows Ibn Khallikān in the *Annals*, under the year 317, but he seems to have had the astronomical work in his own hands[^19.4]: «In this year [317] died Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān, of Ḥarrānian origin, al-Battānī, the famous calculator and astronomer, author of the astronomical work [called] the Sabian. *His name shows that he became a Muslim; so does his preface to the astronomical work. Ibn Khallikān says: I have not found that he embraced the Islamic faith.* He has observations carried out most accurately. He began to observe in the year 264, [continuing] until the year 306; he set the fixed stars in his astronomical work for the year 299. His astronomical work is in two editions, the first and the second; the second better. Al-Battānī, with *a* after *B* (or with *i*, as others say), is a name derived from B..ttān, which is a rural district of the territory of Ḥarrān».
@p 0019b018..0019b022
Of no importance, and drawn from Ibn al-Qifṭī, is what Abū l-Faraj (Barhebraeus) relates in the *History of the Dynasties*[^19.5]: «In the year 317 died Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān Abū ʿAbd Allāh al-Battānī, one of the famous men in the observation of the stars. No one is known in Islam who attained his excellence in observing the stars exactly and in investigating their motions. A Sabian by origin, from [the city of] Ḥarrān».
@p 0019b023..0019b025
Lastly al-ʿAynī, who died at Cairo on 4 Dhū l-Ḥijja 855 (28 Dec. 1451), copied almost all Ibn Khallikān's words into his unpublished chronicle *ʿIqd al-jumān*, according to Chwolsohn I, 615.
@p 0019b026..0019b027 + 0020b001..0020b008
All the authors mentioned so far seem to flow from a single old source. Two others, who however say almost nothing new, perhaps have a different origin: namely Ẓahīr al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Abī l-Qāsim al-Bayhaqī, in the *Taʾrīkh ḥukamāʾ al-islām*, «history of the sages of Islam»[^20.1], written a little after 553 H (beginning 1 Febr. 1158), and, in the seventh century of the hijra (the thirteenth AD), Muḥammad ibn Maḥmūd al-Shahrazūrī in the *Nuzhat al-arwāḥ wa rawḍat al-ifrāḥ*, «the delight of the souls and the gardens of joy»[^20.2]. Both are unpublished; the passages on al-Battānī, which I had found to be in them from Ahlwardt's great catalogue of the Arabic codices of Berlin, Dr. Eugen Mittwoch very kindly transcribed for my use. What ʿAlī al-Bayhaqī alone has I enclose in the text in angle brackets < > and print in italics in the translation; the vowels come from me, not from the codices:

محمد بن جابر البتاني <هو محمد بن جابر بن سنان بن ثابت بن قرة الحراني> صاحب الرصد المشهور بعد أيام المأمون وكان <حكيماً> عارفاً بتفاصيل أجزاء علوم الحكمة وقد انفق أموالاً في الرصد وبتّان قرية <في حدود > حرّان واليها يُنْسَب <محمد بن جابر وما نُقِل عنه كُدُورة العمر في جار السّوء والولد العاقّ والمرأة السّيّئة الأخلاق وقال أشياء لا يُسْتَقَلّ قليلها الدَّيْن والعداوة والمرض>
@p 0020b011..0020b018
«Muḥammad Ibn Jābir al-Battānī, *that is, Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān ibn Thābit ibn Qurra al-Ḥarrānī*[^20.6], master of the most famous observatory[^20.7], after the age of al-Maʾmūn. He was *a sage*, versed in distinguishing finely the parts[^20.3] of the disciplines of philosophy[^20.8]. He spent riches on the observatory. Battān is a village *in the territory*[^20.4][^20.9] of Ḥarrān; from it *Muḥammad ibn Jābir*[^20.5][^20.10] took his relative name. *Among the things that are reported [as said] by him: The affliction of life lies in a disobedient son, a bad neighbour and a woman of evil character. He said [also]: There are things whose small quantity is never reckoned little: debt, enmity, disease*».
@p 0020b019..0020b020
Whether these sayings come from al-Battānī, or whether ʿAlī al-Bayhaqī rather, as at the beginning of the life, confused our author with another man, I do not venture to judge.
