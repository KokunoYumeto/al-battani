@p 0110nl001..0110nr005
p. 21, n. 1 (*longissimus quantitate data diem medium superat*): Plato adds this: «Si autem haec per aliquam stellarum fixarum circa polum positarum, et ipsae sunt, quae in ipsa regione nunquam occultantur, inspicere volueris, ipsius altitudines cum altius et inferius fuerit, quod contingit cum per circulum medii caeli semel supra polum et semel subtus polum transierit accipe, scilicet altitudines, aut eas in unum collige, et collecti dimidium accipe, quodque fuerit, erit altitudo poli in regione illa» (but if you wish to examine this by one of the fixed stars placed about the pole, which are those that never set in that region, take its altitudes when it is highest and when lowest, which happens when it passes the circle of the midheaven once above the pole and once below the pole — take, that is, the altitudes — or gather them into one and take half of the sum, and what it comes to will be the altitude of the pole in that region). — But this does not fit the title of the chapter at all, and has already been explained in chapter VI (p. 15).

@p 0117nl004..0117nr005
p. 28, n. 3 (*arcuatio et conversio ad gradus aequalitatis*): If a circle of declination cuts the ecliptic and the equator, the degrees of the ecliptic from the equinox to that circle are called the *degrees of equality* with respect to the arc of the equator between the beginning of Aries and the circle of declination, that is, with respect to the right ascension. See al-Jaghmīnī, p. 238; Aboul Hhassan, ch. XXXII, p. 218. In the Alfonsine Tables they are called *gradus aequales* (equal degrees).
