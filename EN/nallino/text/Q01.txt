@p 0422b004..0422b009
**§ 1.** — I have noted now and then in my notes that the numbers of the Escorial codex teem with errors, so that no table in the codex is free from them. Of the 180 sines, to give an example, 74 are faulty in the minutes or the seconds. I was unwilling to bring out the tables corrupted as they stood in the codex, and it would have been tedious to list all the false readings, which were of no use; so I decided to publish the tables corrected as far as I could, indicating only those readings of the codex whose correction was less certain or clear.
@p 0422b010..0422b014
The astronomers of the Arabs write the numbers of the tables not with the figures that we now call Arabic, but with the letters of the alphabet, to which they assign numerical values. From the confusion of similar letters, written hastily or carelessly by the copyists, corruptions easily arise. The letters most often confused with one another in this codex of ours, which is written in the western Arabic, or Maghribī, characters, are these:

Errors arise more rarely from the fact that some letters have one numerical value in the Maghribī script of the codex and another in the eastern Arabic, or *naskhī*, script, which al-Battānī undoubtedly used. The writer of the Maghribī archetype, from which the Escorial codex and the codices used by Plato of Tivoli and by the Spanish translator derive, sometimes (rarely, however) corrupted the numbers by neglecting the appropriate changes. For al-Battānī's {ar:س} 60, {ar:ص} 90, {ar:ف} 80, {ar:ض} 800, {ar:ظ} 900 mean 300, 60, 100, 90 and 800 in the Escorial codex. I mentioned an example of the confusion in vol. III, p. 230, n. 8.
@p 0423b001..0423b005
Still more rarely errors seem to have been taken over from an eastern Arabic copy, such as the confusions of {ar:ب} 2 with {ar:ج} 3, or of {ar:ج} 3 with {ar:د} 4; for in mathematical books the eastern Arabs write the number 3 without the point and the tail[^423.1], that is {ar:ح‍} (but 8 always {ar:ح}). Our scholars, who did not notice this, have very often erred in editing or translating books of the eastern Arabs, and have put 8 for 3.
@p 0423b006..0423b012
Seven times we find written for {0}, that is 0, the Arabic negative {ar:لا} *lā*, which by the value of its letters means 31: on p. 53 (in the minutes of the latitude of the city of al-Sirajān), 57 (in the seconds of the declination for 1°), 84 (in the seconds of the motion in latitude, last line), 93 (in the seconds of the third term for 20° and 32°), 160 (in the degrees of the latitude of the eighth star of Virgo), and 185 (in the degrees with which no. 63 rises). The same thing sometimes occurred in the copy that the Spanish translator used, as I noted in vol. II, p. 215, n. 66, p. 217, n. 261, and p. 271. Cf. vol. II, p. 266, ll. 5–9, where I gave an example of such an error from the *Alphonsine Tables*.
@p 0423b013..0423b024
As regards the healing of the corruptions, the tables are of three kinds. The *first* is that of the tables whose numbers are derived from one another by adding or subtracting a given quantity; then an error affecting a number in the codex, if it is not carried on to the following numbers, is plainly to be attributed to the copyists and is very easily corrected. The *second* kind occurs when there is some relation between the numbers of the table, yet not so certain that discrepancies of one or two seconds (if seconds are given), or of one or two minutes (if only these occur with the degrees), can be restored with full safety to the author's intention; or also when the numbers have no evident relation to one another and must be derived one by one by trigonometric computation, in which discrepancies of one or two units in the seconds or the minutes may come from the author's own computations. The *third* kind occurs when the numbers have no relation to one another and cannot be derived by computation (as in the geographical tables and the catalogue of the fixed stars), so that the tables can be corrected only approximately, by collating the sources from which they derive.
@p 0423b025..0423b032
The errors of the *first* kind I have corrected tacitly. Those of the *second* I have healed with complete certainty whenever a comparison with Ptolemy or Theon helped; where these aids failed, as in the declinations of the Sun and the ascensions of the signs, I had to compute the numbers with logarithms, on whose authority I could correct discrepancies greater than one or two or even three seconds. The false numbers of the tables of the *third* kind I have taken care to restore to the author's intention as far as I could, whenever the restorations were obtained by small and very easy changes of letters; but I have always set out the numbers of the codex in the notes. — I have said more about the corrections made in the commentary on the single tables.
@p 0423b033..0423b037
In this most tedious labour of checking so many thousands of numbers, Giovanni Schiaparelli, with the great kindness that is his, was my companion; he examined once more, most carefully, the tables first corrected by me. Schiaparelli alone corrected the tables that give the declinations of the Sun (pp. 57–58), the lengths of the days (p. 59), the positions of the fixed stars in the year 1211 of Dhū l-Qarnayn (pp. 178–186), and the altitudes and the directions of the hours (p. 188).
