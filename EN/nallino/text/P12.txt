Nor was its fame content with the bounds of the East alone; for, after the translations of Robert of Chester (or of Retines) and of Plato of Tivoli, the name of al-Battānī travelled through the lands of Europe too. That Robert had already composed «*Canones in motibus caelestium corporum ad meridiem urbis Londoniarum*» (canons on the motions of the celestial bodies for the meridian of the city of London), divided into two parts, of which the first is computed for the year 1169 on the authority of the Toledan Tables of Arzachel, the second for the year 1150 «*iuxta Albaten* (var. *Albategnium*) *Harecensis*» (according to Albaten of Harec), i.e. al-Battānī al-Raqqī[^44.1]. That the Arab astronomer conveniently reduced the doctrines of the Almagest to a compendium is asserted by Rudolf of Bruges, writing at Toulouse in 1143 or 1144, as I noted above (p. xxv); and the unknown man who at the end of the twelfth century or the beginning of the thirteenth composed his *Almagestum parvum*, of which I have treated sufficiently in § 3, no. III, pp. xxvi–xxvii, always had him at hand. William of Auvergne, who died in 1249, praises him as a noble philosopher who had only his name (Muḥammad, or Mahometus) in common with the accursed prophet of the Arabs[^44.2]; Henry Bate, in his *Magistralis compositio astrolabii*, written in 1274, plainly shows how much of al-Battānī he had read and how much he valued him[^44.3]. — Gerard of Sabloneta (Sabbioneta), an astronomer of the thirteenth century who is not rarely confused with Gerard of Cremona (twelfth century), knew al-Battānī's work by hearsay, not by sight; for in his *Theorica planetarum*, Bologna 1480 (at the end of the *Spera mundi* of John of Sacrobosco the Englishman), f. 35v, dealing very briefly with the determination of the apogee and the eccentricity of the Sun, he goes on thus: «and in this way Albategni *is said* to have found how much the apogees of the planets move in a year, a month and a day: and he composed tables for this: and he had a great astrolabe [sic!], namely of three cubits or of greater size». Elsewhere, however, he tells things altogether false; f. 34v: «The composers of the tables on Arim, which is a city in India, are said to have been Nembroth[^44.4]: Hermes: Hyconimus[^44.5]: Ptholomaeus: Albategni: Albumazar: Algorismus»; and f. 35r, on the apogees of the planets: «Likewise they are said by Albategni to be moved one degree in 60 years and four months, always to the east»[^45.1][^n]. Albertus Magnus, in the passage cited on pp. xxiv and xxv, commends al-Battānī's work, which he knew from Plato's translation; Alfonso X of Castile collected writings into the *Libros del saber de astronomia*, which not rarely rest on our author's authority, as I have shown in my notes. John of Lignères[^45.2], in chapter XXVIII of the *Canones ad Tabulas Alphonsinas*, mentions our author and the eccentricity of the solar orbit determined by him[^45.3]; perhaps too, if I may propose a conjecture, it was after reading the astronomical work that he first formed the plan of constructing his *table of sines*[^45.4]. In the same age John of Genoa ends his still unpublished *Canones eclipsium* (of the year 1332) with these words: «But see the way of correcting [the times of the eclipses] in Albategni and in the canons of master Io. de Lineriis..... Here end the canons of eclipses that master John of Genoa compiled, extracting them partly from the common canons, partly from Albategni, partly from the lesser Almagest[^45.5], partly from master John of Sicily.....»[^45.6]. The famous Lēwī ben Gersōn, or Leo de Balneolis, the Jew, in the Latin version of his astronomy, cod. Vatic. Lat. 3098, more than once adduces observations of *Albetem*, that is, of al-Battānī. Nor should it be passed over that even in the second half of the fifteenth century Johannes Lucilius Santritter of Heilbronn, *In tabulas Alfonsi canones seu propositiones*, prop. XXXII, sets out al-Battānī's precepts on the limits of the eclipses of the Sun[^45.7] and on computing the apparent conjunction of the luminaries[^45.8].

This Humenus, however, whose tables al-Zarqālī had edited anew, I think is Ammonius of Alexandria, mathematician, rhetorician and philosopher at the end of the fifth century, who, as the philosopher Stephanus (first half of the eighth century) attests in his book περὶ τῆς μαθηματικῆς τέχνης, «*on the mathematical art*», constructed tables for the era of Philip Arrhidaeus: ὁ δέ γε Θέων καὶ Ἡράκλειος καὶ ὁ Ἀμμώνιος τοῖς τοῦ Φιλίππου [ἔτεσιν ἐχρήσατο] καὶ μησὶν Αἰγυπτιακοῖς (the passage was edited by Fr. Cumont in the *Catalogus codicum astrologorum graecorum*, Brussels 1899 ff., vol. II, p. 182). The treatise on the astrolabe that is often attributed to Ammonius in the codices has as its author Nicephorus Gregoras (Νικηφόρος ὁ Γρηγορᾶς), as P. Tannery showed in the *Revue de philologie*, vol.
