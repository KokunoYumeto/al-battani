III. *Sharḥ al-maqālāt al-arbaʿ li-Baṭlamyūs* «Commentary on the Tetrabiblos of Ptolemy».
@p 0029b020..0029b027
That it once existed in the Escorial Library we know from the *Bibliotheca Arabico-Hispana* of Casiri, who described the codex thus, vol. I, p. 399: «Cod. CMLXVI, no. 2: A commentary on the Quadripartitum of Ptolemy, on the judgements of the stars, with tables added. The author is the most distinguished man Mohammad Ben Geber Albategnius». But H. Derenbourg has already noted that the codex is now lost with others, *Les manuscrits arabes de l'Escurial*, Paris 1884, vol. I, p. xxiv; nor could it be found in October 1894, when I was staying at the Escorial. In September 1901, when the Arabic codices were examined again by the librarians, no trace of the book appeared, as Father Pedro Blanco Soto most kindly wrote to me.
@p 0029b028..0029b030
It is likely that in this commentary al-Battānī explained above all the mathematical principles of astrology; for the «*tables added*» could have served only for these, and our author seems to have indulged a little in the ravings of judicial astrology.
@p 0029b031..0029b034 + 0030b001..0030b009
In the Royal Library of Berlin an Arabic codex is kept, comprising 124 pages and mutilated by the loss of its first leaf, which has this subscription at the end[^29.3]: «Here ends the *book of the Tetrabiblos, the commentary* of al-Battānī[^29.4], by the grace of God. Aḥmad ibn Tamīm wrote it[^29.5], giving thanks to God for His benefits and praying for Muḥammad the Prophet», etc. The author of the catalogue judges the book thus: «That this is a commentary on the book of Ptolemy so entitled [i.e. the Tetrabiblos] in no way appears; it seems to be a work standing by itself (*ein selbstständiges Werk*), and is undoubtedly to be regarded only as a new recension (*Bearbeitung*) of the original book, which is difficult to understand. This, as the name itself shows, is divided into four books, of which there is no trace in the codex..... The work comprises many chapters (which, however, lack the name «*chapter*»), marked with no number». So that the book may be judged better, I translate the Arabic headings of the chapters as they are given in Ahlwardt's catalogue, adding the numbers of the corresponding chapters of the *Tetrabiblos* according to the vulgate and according to the Frankfurt edition (cf. p. 305, n. 2):

So, unless some leaves have fallen out or some headings have escaped Ahlwardt, only these chapters of the Tetrabiblos seem to be left out, besides the first seven, which were certainly contained in the lost leaf or leaves: I, 9, 15–22; II, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8; IV, 4. According to Ahlwardt's catalogue, chapter I, 8 begins: «The Moon and the three superior planets receive an increase», which certainly agrees with the Greek text and with both Latin translations from the Arabic. — The first words of the chapter on children (IV, 5) are: «as to children we must inspect the planets that are in the place»; in Ptolemy: «Since, after marriage, children must be dealt with, we must inspect the planets that are in the place». So the codex does not have Ptolemy's first words; but it does not have the preceding chapter (IV, 4) either, which dealt with marriage. — The last words of the codex I do not find in the Tetrabiblos: «So too we must work in the years through the twelve signs of the zodiac. Others say: You will take every day [the interval] from the Sun to the Moon, and cast it off from the ascendant of the native's birth, [which?] you will determine for the native on that day in place of the Part of Fortune». One would have to investigate whether this belongs to some appendix. — Lastly, there is no trace of the *tables* that Casiri had seen in the Escorial codex of al-Battānī's commentary. The word *zawāyā* also gives room for suspicion, since it never occurs in that sense in the astronomical work, as I noted on p. xxi, n. 1.
@p 0032b013..0032b016
Having weighed Ahlwardt's description, then, and all these things, I hold it likely that the Berlin codex contains only very long excerpts from the Tetrabiblos, and that nothing in it belongs to al-Battānī. Perhaps the compiler had taken all of it from a copy of the Tetrabiblos that also contained al-Battānī's commentary; hence the strange subscription of the Berlin codex.
@p 0032b017..0032b023
Al-Battānī's commentary was unknown to ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān (Haly Heben Rodan), who openly testifies that he had never seen any «gloss» on the Tetrabiblos[^32.1]. Not so, however, Abū l-Ḥasan ʿAlī Ibn Abī l-Rijāl; for he relates[^32.2]: «There are also some of the first sages who take all these significations[^32.3] from the eclipses that happen in the years of the conjunctions of the planets, mixing them with the revolutions of the years: Ptolemy is of this opinion, and his greater confidence in all those judgements rests on it; and Mahomet son of Geber Albategni subscribes to it, and all those who follow the way of Ptolemy».

@f 30.4 0030nl009..0030nr027 + 0031nl001..0031nl004
In the Greek περὶ ἰδιοπροσώπων καὶ λαμπηνῶν καὶ θρόνων (var.: τῶν τοιούτων) «on [planets] having their own peculiar face, on royal chariots and thrones (var.: and the like)». According to Ptolemy, a planet has its own peculiar face, that is, is ἰδιοπρόσωπος, when it keeps towards the Sun or the Moon the same configuration (σχηματισμός) that its domicile (οἶκος) has towards their domiciles; as, for example, Venus when it is distant from the luminaries by a sextile aspect, an evening star from the Sun, a morning star from the Moon, for so their domiciles are placed. The Arabs rendered this ἰδιοπροσωπία *al-muwājaha*, «to be face to face with someone», whence among the astrologers of the Middle Ages «*almugea, the seeing of each other face to face, to see face to face*». — The brightness or splendour (*al-ināra*, whence in the Latin translation of the Tetrabiblos from the Arabic «*alchinara*») stands for the Greek λαμπήνη, that is, a chariot or royal carriage. «*And* [the planets] *are said to be in their royal chariots and thrones and the like* [excellences] when they are joined in fellowship, in two or more of the ways set out above, with the places in which they are found. For because then their efficacy is most increased, by the likeness and the help of the homogeneous familiarity of the signs, therefore they are said *to sit on their thrones* and *to shine*» ἐν λαμπήναις δὲ ἰδίαις λέγονται εἶναι καὶ θρόνοις καὶ τοῖς τοιούτοις, ὅταν κατὰ δύο ἢ κατὰ πλείους τῶν προεκτεθειμένων τρόπων συνῳκειωμένοι τυγχάνωσι τοῖς τόποις ἐν οἷς καταλαμβάνονται. τότε γὰρ μάλιστα τῆς δυνάμεως αὐτῶν αὐξανομένης πρὸς ἐνέργειαν διὰ τὸ ὅμοιον καὶ συμπρακτικὸν τῆς τῶν δωδεκατημορίων ὁμοφυοῦς οἰκειώσεως, ἰδιοθρονεῖν καὶ λάμπειν λέγονται. So Ptolemy, with whom Hephaestion of Thebes agrees. In translating the title of the chapter the Arabic translator plainly derived the word λαμπήνη from the verb λάμπειν «to shine», which occurs at the end of the passage quoted; that he interpreted the words ἐν λαμπήναις δὲ ἰδίαις λέγονται εἶναι καὶ θρόνοις otherwise appears from both Latin translations from the Arabic: «in the place *of its joy and honour*». The word seems to have been obscure even to the ancients; a certain scholiast on the Tetrabiblos: λαμπήνη λέγεται ἡ ἕδρα ἡ ἀργυρᾶ ὑψηλή, ἐν ᾗ ὁ ἄρχων ἱδρυνθεὶς λαμπρότερος φαίνεται. θρόνος δὲ ὁ τούτου κατώτερος καὶ ἐκ ξύλων ἀποτελεσθεὶς «λαμπήνη is the name of the high silver seat, sitting in which a ruler appears more splendid; θρόνος that which is lower than it and made of wood» (in H. van Herwerden, *Lexicon Graecum suppletorium et dialecticum*, Leiden 1902, p. 489, a passage that I owe to my very dear friend C. O. Zuretti).
