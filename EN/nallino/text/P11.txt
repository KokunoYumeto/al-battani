II. *What posterity thought of al-Battānī.* — The Arabs extol al-Battānī's work with praise with one voice, not excepting al-Ṣūfī and Ibn Yūnus, who nevertheless blame small errors in it, and not always rightly[^41.3]. From the notes added to this volume and to the next it is gathered well enough how often his observations and computations are both mentioned by later writers and used in constructing tables; and likewise that al-Battānī's elements also occur in books in which there is no mention of our astronomer[^41.4]. To the writers cited by me here and there, such as Thābit ibn Qurra, al-Masʿūdī, Ibn Hibintā, al-Bīrūnī, al-Zarqālī, al-Khāzinī, the Indo-Persian Abū l-Faẓl ʿAllāmī, and those who, as we have seen, wrote al-Battānī's life, others can easily be added. Maslama al-Majrīṭī, who died in the year 398 H (beginning 16 Sept. 1007), reduced to a compendium al-Battānī's tables of the equations of the Sun, the Moon and the five planets[^42.1][^n], perhaps also adapting them to the era of the Arabs, as he had done in the tables of al-Khwārizmī; and Kūshyār ibn Labbān, who flourished about the years 360–420 H (AD 971–1029), took the tables of the equation of the Sun into his *al-Zīj al-jāmiʿ*[^42.2]. In the first half of the fifth century H (the eleventh AD), ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad al-Nasawī gave this judgement, reported to us by Ḥājjī Khalīfa III, 564, no. 6946: «Of the astronomical works that serve for observations the most perfect indeed is (ان اصح الزيجات الرصدية) the *zīj al-Battānī*, because it is the nearest to the truth [of all]; but it is constructed for the eras of the Romans and of the hijra, whose use, because of the leap years and the fractions, makes its adaptation to the era of the Persians difficult[^42.3]». In the same fifth century the *zīj al-Battānī* is mentioned by an anonymous pupil of the famous Aḥmad al-Sijzī in his book *Tasṭīḥ al-ṣuwar wa tabṭīḥ al-kuwar*, on the doctrine of making the astrolabe[^42.4][^n]; and al-Bīrūnī, whom we have seen mentioning al-Battānī with praise in his works, also testifies that he wrote a book entitled *Jalāʾ al-adhhān fī zīj al-Battānī*, «the brightening of the minds on the astronomical work of al-Battānī»[^42.5]. Lastly, in the second half of the fourteenth century AD, the famous historian Ibn Khaldūn[^42.6] counts the works of al-Battānī and of Ibn al-Kammād among the most excellent books of astronomy.

That al-Battānī's work was translated into Hebrew is not established[^42.9]; yet the Jews writing in the Hebrew language in the twelfth century AD and after did not lack knowledge of it. That Abraham bar Ḥiyya had it at hand and quoted it is plain from my notes on pp. 6, 287, 298, 302–03; and in the same age Abraham Ibn ʿEzra mentions al-Battānī and commends his tables[^43.1]. The famous Maimonides[^43.2] tacitly follows our astronomer in the eighth treatise of the work *Mishnēh Tōrāh*, «Repetition of the Law», which is entitled *hilkōt qiddūsh hā ḥōdesh*, «rules on the sanctification of the new moon», ch. XII; for there he puts the mean motion of the Sun at 28° 35′ 1″ in 29 days, or 348° 55′ 15″ in the «ordinary», or lunar, year, which agree exactly with al-Battānī's tables (vol. II, pp. 22 and 20), and the motion of the solar apogee at 54″ a year[^43.3]. In computing the arc of visibility of the new Moon, however, he follows an easier method, but less exact, than al-Battānī's[^43.4]. — That the name of al-Battānī occurs more than once in the *yěsōd ʿōlām*, «Foundation of the World», composed by Isaac Israeli in 1310, is attested by Steinschneider on Baldi, pp. 450 and 455 (extr. pp. 26 and 29). What Lēwī ben Gerson (Leo de Balneolis) took from our astronomer we shall see below. It is worth mentioning, unless there is an error in Steinschneider's notice[^43.5], that in the unpublished *ʿērek hā ḥălōph*, written at Tarascon in 1365, ʿImmānūʾēl ben Yaʿăqōb (Bonfils) constructed astronomical tables according to al-Battānī's. Lastly, Ēliyyāh ben Mosheh Bashyasī, an adherent of the sect of the Karaites, who died in 1490, constructed tables of the lunar and solar motions «for the latitude of Constantinople [41⅓°] according to the doctrine of al-Battānī and the doctrine of the sage ʿImmānūʾēl, author of the book of the [six] wings», *bě ōpheq Qōsṭandūnā lě daʿat al-Battānī wě lě daʿat hā ḥākām ʿImmānūʾēl baʿal hak-kěnāphīm*[^43.6].

He means chapter XXVI, of which I have treated on pp. 200–01 and 203–04; Ibn Yūnus did not understand al-Battānī's rather obscure text. «*From the equator*» I think is an error of the codex for «*from the ecliptic*». Caussin had translated otherwise, connecting «*distance*» with «*from the equator*» and understanding chapter XVIII of al-Battānī: «ce que dit.... Albattani sur la déclinaison d'une étoile qui a une latitude, renferme une erreur évidente» (what Albattani says.... on the declination of a star that has a latitude contains an evident error). Which is certainly not impossible, though it hardly agrees with the Arabic construction. Another quite certain example of the word رسالة in the sense of «*precepts, procedure of computation*» is given by Ibn Yūnus on the same page, l. 11: {ar:وانما ثبت اماكن الغلط لئلا يجد واحد في عمل واحد رسالتين في زيجي هذا وفي غيره من الازياج فلا يعلم اين الصواب فيبقى حائرا فاذا قرأ هذه الرسالة علم ان الخلاف التي (sic) فيه لغيره من الازياج وقع عن علم بالصواب}, where the first *risālatayn* is rightly rendered by Caussin «deux procédés» (two procedures). A third example in Ḥabash, f. 168r: «likewise according to the *precepts* that preceded these ({ar:الرسالة التي قبل هذه})», where two methods of computation set out in the same chapter are dealt with.
