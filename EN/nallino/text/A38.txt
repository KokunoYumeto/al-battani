@p 0339b035..0339b037 + 0340b001..0340b006
The *argument of latitude*, which is the arc of the lunar orbit between a certain point of the orbit and the place of the moon at a given moment of time, Ptolemy and Theon (*Tables manuelles* vol. I, p. 55 and passim) count from the *northern limit* (βόρειον πέρας), that is, from the point of the lunar orbit at which the greatest northern latitude occurs; it is 90° from the ascending (or northern) node. Al-Battānī, on the other hand, and the other Arabs compute the argument from the node itself, following the usage of the Indians; and the Indians in this probably followed the Chaldaeo-Greek astrologers, for whom the nodes of the moon, and especially the northern node, had great authority and power in nativities (or genitures): for if beneficent planets were found in it, even a mediocre and lowly nativity turned out more splendid and exalted. The astrologers themselves often count the nodes of the lunar orbit among the planets[^340.1].

«On est tenté de reporter à des Chaldéens authentiques ou à des rénovateurs de vieilles superstitions orientales l’idée baroque qui consiste à assimiler aux planètes et à noter dans les thèmes de géniture les noeuds ascendant et descendant de l’orbite lunaire, qui font le tour de l’écliptique en dix-huit ans. Ptolémée ne connaît pas ou ne veut pas connaître ces planètes fictives: mais Tertullien, un demi-siècle plus tard, parle déjà de l’influence que leur attribuaient les astrologues. Les Grecs du Bas-Empire et surtout les Arabes firent grand état des noeuds écliptiques, appelés la Tête ☊ et la Queue ☋ du Dragon» («One is tempted to ascribe to genuine Chaldaeans, or to revivers of old Oriental superstitions, the baroque idea of treating as planets, and marking in the charts of nativity, the ascending and descending nodes of the lunar orbit, which go round the ecliptic in eighteen years. Ptolemy does not know, or does not wish to know, these fictitious planets; but Tertullian, half a century later, already speaks of the influence that the astrologers attributed to them. The Greeks of the Later Empire, and above all the Arabs, made much of the ecliptic nodes, called the Head ☊ and the Tail ☋ of the Dragon») (Bouché-Leclercq, loc. cit., pp. 121–22).

Al-Battānī's formula
\[ [[(α)]] \sin\,\text{lat.}\,\MoonSym = \dfrac{\sin\,\text{arg. of lat.} \times \sin\,\text{greatest lat.}\,\MoonSym}{R} \]
is not found in Ptolemy and Theon[^n]; it is very like the rule by which the declination of the sun is derived from the longitude and the obliquity of the ecliptic (pp. 12 and 162). In orthographic projection it is very easily gathered from the similarity of two triangles; nor does it differ from the very well-known theorem of right-angled spherical triangles
\[ \sin\,\text{cathetus} : \sin\,\text{opposite angle} = \sin\,\text{hypotenuse} : R. \]
@n Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part I, p. LXXIV) correct this statement: the rule is in Ptolemy, who says (V, 7; ed. Halma I, 315) that for computing the table of the moon's latitude «we have used the same demonstration (δείξει) by which we have already computed the arcs of the circle through the poles of the equator cut off between the equator and the ecliptic», that is, the declinations of the sun.
