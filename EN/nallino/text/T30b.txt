There was no small difficulty in fixing the *magnitudes* rightly. In Arabic manuscripts confusions between ب 2 and د 4, between ح (or ج) 3 and د 4, and between د 4 and ه 5 are very easy. Moreover, the Greek copies are most uncertain in distinguishing the large and the small stars of the same magnitude. In Grynaeus's Greek edition the words μείζων «greater» and ἐλάσσων «less» are printed in the margin, but placed so carelessly that it often cannot be told to which of two successive stars that distinction belongs. And the discrepancies between the Latin edition published by Liechtenstein[^721.1] and the Latin of George of Trebizond are very frequent. Because of so great an uncertainty in the matter, Halma and Baily left those words out. So only Liechtenstein and al-Ṣūfī gave me help; al-Ṣūfī above all, who, putting general descriptions of the stars before the catalogues of the single constellations, lists with the greatest accuracy the magnitudes indicated in the copies of the Almagest[^721.2]. The *Spanish* version leaves out those «large» and «small»; but Heiberg, who takes the sigla $\overset{\varepsilon}{\mu}$ and ἐλ$^{\varsigma}$ from his codices, was of the greatest help to me in making the supplement of short notes given below.

E. B. Knobel, *On Al-Sūfi's Star Magnitudes* (Monthly Notices of the R. Astron. Society, vol. XLV, 1885), p. 419, points out that these subdivisions of the magnitudes are indicated «in the Liechtenstein Latin *Almagest*, translated from the Arabic by Gerard of Cremona, by «em» (presumably for μείζων) and «el» (ἐλάσσων). In the British Museum Arabic *Almagest* of A.D. 1218, by the Arabic letters mīm م and lām ل. (These two last examples suggest that the original Arabian scribe, who translated from the Greek, had no idea of the meaning or the application of the words μείζων and ἐλάσσων, and therefore appended the initials of the Greek words in Arabic letters). In the Bodleian Library Arabic *Almagest* and the several mss. of Al-Sūfi and Ulugh Beigh, these subdivisions are expressed by the letters Kāf and Sād, being the initials of the words كبير (large) and صغير (small)».

In defining the stars, the Arabic version of the Almagest that al-Battānī used had rendered the Greek ἐν (τῷ) by the Arabic particle في, ἐπὶ (τοῦ, more rarely ὑπὲρ τόν) by the particle على, and ἐπάνω (τοῦ) by the particle فوق; I translated the first two *in* and the last *above*.

In the margin I have put the signs by which, after Johann Bayer's *Uranometria* (1603), astronomers designate the stars; in doing this I had Baily as an excellent guide. It is worth quoting his words (p. 16 of the preface): «I have also, in the last column, referred to the corresponding observations of the same stars, by Flamsteed and Lacaille, as far as I have been able to identify them. This had been previously done by Bode[^721.3] and Delambre[^721.4]; but, on examining their references, I have had frequent occasion to differ from them in opinion. It may perhaps be sometimes difficult to decide as to the precise star that was intended to have been observated. ..... Bayer's characters are such as I have retained in Flamsteed's catalogue, published in my «Account» of his Life[^721.5], and which in some case differ from those adopted by Flamsteed himself. On a revision of the stars in the southern hemisphere, since the printing of *this* catalogue, there are a few on which I have seen reason to alter my opinion as to their identity,.....: but wherever this has occurred I have made the requisite correction in the list of Errata». See my note 1 on p. 175. — Schjellerup, pp. 5–23 of his edition of al-Ṣūfī, also gave the modern names of the stars, saying nothing of the method he followed in doing so. They sometimes differ, above all in the southern constellations, from the signs in Baily, for two reasons: first, because Schjellerup perhaps judged here and there that Bayer's letters should be taken otherwise than in Baily; secondly, because he necessarily fixed the letters according to the meaning of al-Ṣūfī, who, in the general descriptions placed before the catalogues of the single constellations, sought to interpret the Almagest rightly, looking for its stars again in the sky and determining them with the greatest accuracy[^722.1].
