III. *Our own judgement of the astronomical work.* — Having briefly reviewed the judgements of others, let us now see what al-Battānī's work looks like from the complete Arabic text.
@p 0050b005..0050b016
The author modestly admits that he follows in Ptolemy's footsteps, though he has changed some of his elements on the authority of new observations (p. 5, ll. 23–34), and that he wished to make the way plain for those who had given themselves to astronomical studies (ibid., ll. 36–38). He knew the *Almagest* only from Arabic translations[^50.2] (p. 66, ll. 27–29, and cf. p. 282, n. *a*), but he had carefully collated copies that sometimes differed in the numbers (p. 126, l. 7, and the note, pp. 297–98). The passages of the Almagest that he adduces here and there agree very well with the Greek text, though it appears from the catalogue of the fixed stars (vol. II, pp. 153, 154, 156) that the translators did not always understand the Greek rightly; moreover on pp. 40 (l. 29 ff.), 96 (l. 3) and 126 (l. 35) al-Battānī attributes some things to Ptolemy not altogether correctly, as I have noted in my notes[^50.3]. The *Tetrabiblos*, whose doctrines he tacitly sets out on pp. 129–30, he mentions on p. 134, ll. 40–41, after describing its method of directing the aphetae; the passage on p. 149, however, in which the same book is quoted, seems to me spurious. Whether he had the *Geography* in his hands is uncertain; cf. p. 20, l. 11, and my observations on the geographical tables, vol. II.
@p 0050b017..0050b020
The *Canones*, or *Handy Tables*, of Theon of Alexandria he mentions on pp. 69, 82, 84 and 105, and from them he took the tables of the parallaxes for all the climates, vol. II, pp. 95–101; from Theon too undoubtedly come the things related at the beginning of chapter LII (up to p. 127, l. 10), and perhaps the «orders of the declination», p. 13, ll. 22–26.
@p 0050b021..0050b034 + 0051b001..0051b002
On pp. 19–20, and in the heading of the geographical tables, vol. II, pp. 33–54, there occurs the *kitāb ṣūrat al-arḍ*, «book of the figure of the Earth», of an unknown author, of which I shall speak in the observations in vol. II. Besides this book, Ptolemy and Theon, he mentions no author's name. Yet it is certain that al-Battānī drew from other sources too. For on pp. 17–19 he relates geographical matters on the authority of «*certain ancients*», who, as I have shown in the notes, pp. 165–77, were Syrians following the Ptolemaic geography; nor does he correct their serious error on p. 17. Likewise on pp. 120–23 he sets out the distances and the sizes of the heavenly bodies as they were found «*by the more recent sages after Ptolemy*»; on p. 40 he relates new things about the sidereal year of the ancient Egyptians and Babylonians, and about the astrologers who professed the trepidation of the fixed stars (p. 127); he indicates, not without errors, the lunar mansions that the *Arabs* (who are not, however, mentioned in that place) distinguished in the heavens (p. 125 and the note, pp. 295–97); he asserts on p. 5 that he has seen astronomical books that disagreed with one another; he mentions unskilled men of his age who held altogether false opinions about the new moon, and the opinions of the ancients (Hebrews and Indians?) (pp. 86, 88); he divides the gnomon into 12 parts, following the example «*of the calculators*», p. 22; and about the arc of visibility of the fixed stars he reports what some «say» (p. 120, ll. 2–7). The information on calendars comes from a source other than Ptolemy and Theon, as do some other things that will be mentioned below.
@p 0051b003..0051b009
The corrections that he makes to Ptolemy he sometimes mentions, but without any boasting. So on p. 12 on the obliquity of the ecliptic; 41–47 on the length of the four seasons of the year, on the motions of the Sun and on the eccentricity of its orbit; 55–59 and 60–61 on the computation of the angular diameters of the Sun, the Moon and the shadow, by which he found that the diameter of the Sun varies and gives occasion to annular eclipses (p. 58); 54 on the tables of the Moon; 66 on the motions and the apogees of the planets; 10–11 on the sines taken in place of Ptolemy's chords. On pp. 127–28 he excuses Ptolemy, who, because of the fewer observations of the more ancient astronomers, had not determined the motion of the precession well.
@p 0051b010..0051b022
But in other matters, and not small ones, in which he departs from Ptolemy, he buries the disagreement in silence, as though fearing to be reproached for little reverence towards the supreme Greek astronomer. So in the different doctrine of the equation of time, p. 49; in the parallax of the inferior planets, p. 77; in the new and most elegant computation of the magnitudes of the lunar eclipses, pp. 99–100; in the more exact computation of the parallax in the conjunctions of the Sun and the Moon, pp. 104–06; in the correction of the zenith distance of the Moon and of the angles by which the parallaxes are computed, p. 82; in the opportune choice of the nonagesimal for making certain observations that Ptolemy made in the meridian, p. 85; in the mathematical doctrine of the astrological projection of the rays, p. 131; in counting the arcs of visibility of the planets on the equator rather than on the ecliptic, p. 117; in the last part of the computation of the directions (cf. the note, p. 317); in the construction of a new and most convenient astronomical instrument, pp. 138–43; in the more suitable arrangement of the triquetrum, p. 144. He tacitly corrects Ptolemy's very serious error of the immobile solar apogee (cf. the notes, 216–18), and he never mentions that he computed some tables of the Almagest better, vol. II, pp. 93–94 and 138–39.
@p 0051b023..0051b036 + 0052b001..0052b007
It is strange, on the other hand, that al-Battānī says nothing at all of the *Arab astronomers*, although he has some things in common with the observers of the caliph al-Maʾmūn and with the Banū Mūsā ibn Shākir: the obliquity of the ecliptic, the amount of the precession of the equinoxes, the apogee of the Sun affected by the motion of the precession, the apogee and the equation of the centre of Venus equal to those of the Sun (note, p. 242), the motion of the latitude of the Moon computed from the northern node (notes, pp. 250–52). He takes the length of the degree of the meridian from the Greek-Syrian tradition, not from the very famous measurement of the caliph al-Maʾmūn, p. 19; he counts the terrestrial longitudes from the Fortunate Isles, in the Ptolemaic manner, on pp. 19 and 20 and in the geographical tables (cf. the notes on vol. II), not from the meridian of the western coast of Africa that the astronomers and geographers of the school of Baghdād used; the book *of the figure of the Earth*, from which he takes the geographical coordinates, though teaching that they are not certain, is not the one that Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī had written by order of al-Maʾmūn, but a compendium or recension of Ptolemy's geography, translated into Arabic from the Syriac language and adapted in part, and not always happily, to the new knowledge of the Arabs. — Technical words of Persian and Indian origin, already adopted by the astronomers of Baghdād, such as *awj* «the apogee of the eccentric»[^51.1], *jayb* «the sine»[^52.1], *buht* «the unequal motion of the seven planets in a day»[^52.2], al-Battānī rejected altogether, not indeed from zeal for a purer Arabic speech, but because they were not found in the translations of the Almagest[^52.3]. On the other hand, twice he took from certain copies of the Almagest those foolish Greek words *afījiyūn* and *ferījiyūn*, of which I have treated on p. 282 (cf. p. xxxvii, n.), and the Greek names of the winds, vol. II, p. 92. The well-known Persian word *jawzahar* occurs on p. 75, in the title of chapter XXXVII, where, however, it seems interpolated (it is missing in the list of chapters, p. 3, and in Plato's translation); and both the passages in which *haylāj* is mentioned are spurious[^52.4].

@f 51.1 0051nl001..0051nr006 + 0052nl001..0052nl010
Al-Battānī and the old Arabic translations of the Almagest call both the apogee of the *eccentric* and that of the *epicycle* «*the greatest distance*». Not so, however, the other Arabs, whose usage our people of the Middle Ages too followed. Guido Bonatti, the famous Italian astrologer who lived in the second half of the thirteenth century, says: «Some have thought that the aux [*awj*] was the remoter place of the epicycle, laying no stress on the aux, but perhaps speaking of it equivocally, in a broad sense. But the true aux is only the remoter place of the eccentric and not of the epicycle. In the epicycle only the longer distance and the nearer distance are considered..... Moreover, that the aux is not considered in the epicycle is plain from this, that the epicycle goes round the whole eccentric. Hence it is sometimes in the aux, namely in the remoter part of the eccentric, sometimes opposite the aux, namely in the nearer part of the eccentric, sometimes between these places» (Guidonis Bonati Foroliviensis *de astronomia tractatus X, universum quod ad iudiciariam rationem nativitatum, aëris, tempestatum attinet, comprehendentes*, Basel 1550, tract. III, part II, ch. I, cols. 127–28).

A word not unknown to our people: *buth planetarum* in the translation of the Tables of Arzachel (Bullett. di bibl. delle sc. matem., XX, 1887, 591); in proposition XXIV of Johannes Santritter on the Alphonsine Tables (cf. above, p. xxxvi, n. 7): «To find by the table made for this the *Buth* of the Sun and the Moon and of any of the planets, that is, its unequal motion, equated, in one day». The Arabic plural is *abhāt*, which occurs in the astronomical tables of an unknown author kept at Leiden (*Catal. codd. oriental.*, vol. III, p. 139, no. 1156).
