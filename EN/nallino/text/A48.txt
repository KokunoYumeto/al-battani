@p 0372b020
*The «equatorial» arcs of the appearances* of the planets.
@p 0372b021..0372b024
That according to al-Battānī these are truly arcs of the *equator* is plain also from the following precepts, where the arcs are said to be compared with the arc of right ascension («the times of the settings or of the ascensions») that answers to the difference of longitude between the sun and the given planet. The same appears from the end of chapter XLIX, p. 120.
@p 0372b025..0372b032
Ptolemy, on the other hand, chose arcs of the *ecliptic*, that is, the difference of longitude itself[^372.2]: «From this consideration of the observations of the risings of the planets we have found that near (περί) the beginning of Cancer, in general (ὡς ἐπίπαν), Saturn appears (ἀνατέλλει) when it is 14° from the true sun, Jupiter 12 1/2° + 1/4°, Mars 14 1/2°, Venus in the evening 5 2/3°, Mercury in the evening 11 1/2°» (*Almag.* XIII, 7, ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 418–19). But since Ptolemy had determined these limits of the appearances near the beginning of Cancer, where the equator and the ecliptic are nearly parallel and the right ascensions differ little from the corresponding arcs of the zodiac, al-Battānī could transfer Ptolemy's limiting numbers to the equator without perceptible error.
@f 372.2 0372nl005..0372nr007
In *Almag.* XIII, 7 (ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 420–2) he also computed the least depression of the sun below the horizon needed for these heliacal risings and settings of the planets to be possible; he found 11° for Saturn, 10° for Jupiter, 11 1/2° for Mars, 5° for Venus and 10° for Mercury. See R. Wolf, *Handbuch der Astronomie*, Zürich 1890–93, vol. I, pp. 423–24, § 191, *c*, who strangely confesses that he did not find these numbers in the Almagest; what is missing in the Almagest is the amounts of the arcs of the fixed stars (see note *b* on ch. XLIX).
@p 0373b001..0373b003
Al-Battānī rightly preferred to compute the elements of the computation in arcs of the equator; for if they were arcs of the ecliptic, they would change not a little in the same clime in different parts of the year, because of the varying inclination of the ecliptic to the horizon[^373.1].
@f 373.1 0373nl001..0373nl003
[It must be noted, however, that those arcs, of the equator as well as of the ecliptic, are different for different latitudes of the earth. — Schiaparelli.]
@p 0373b004..0373b007
Only on p. 118, ll. 14–28, does al-Battānī count the arc of seeing on the *ecliptic*; for there he explains the use of the tables of the appearances and disappearances of the planets (vol. II, pp. 142–43), which he constructed for the latitude 36° 0′ of the city of al-Raqqa, after the model of Ptolemy's tables, for the use of those who wish to avoid direct computation (which is, however, better).
@p 0373b008..0373b013
Ḥabash, ff. 67v–70v (the tables ff. 73r–76v), and Ulugh Beg, p. 142 of the text, 192 of the translation, always consider arcs of the ecliptic. — Barhebraeus, pp. 79–80 of the text, speaks after Ptolemy of the depression of the sun below the horizon (which is counted on the vertical circle of the sun). The Indians, like al-Battānī, fixed the limits of the visibility of a planet on the equator; but their numbers differ from the Greek and the Arabic ones; see the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*, transl. Burgess–Whitney, IX, 4–9, pp. 365–67, and al-Bīrūnī, *India*, ch. LVII, pp. 246–47 of the text, vol. II, pp. 90–92 of the translation.
