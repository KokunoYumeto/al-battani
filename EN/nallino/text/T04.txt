The column of the leap years gives sixtieths of a day. Since the Roman year is of 365 1/4 days, or 365 15/60, the sixtieths, added to themselves, make up a whole day to be intercalated every fourth year.
@p 0650b010..0650b022
I think that the Latin names of the months were added by a *Western Arab interpolator*. They are never mentioned by al-Battānī in the chapters on chronology; they also differ from the names that occur in the *Eastern Arab* writers[^650.1], namely *yānuwāriyūs, fībruwāriyūs, marṭiyūs, abrīliyūs, māyūs, yūniyūs, yūliyūs, aghūsṭūs, sābṭānbriyūs, oqṭūmbriyūs, nuwāmbriyūs, dīqembriyūs*, which come from the Greek: Ἰανουάριος, Φεβρουάριος, Μάρτιος, Ἀπρίλλιος, Μάϊος, Ἰούνιος, Ἰούλιος, Αὔγουστος, Σεπτέμβριος, Ὀκτώβριος, Νοέμβριος, Δεκέμβριος. Among the *Eastern Arabs* names taken directly from *Latin* or from the *Romance* languages became known only in the twelfth century, through the wars of the Crusades and through the treaties concluded with the rulers of Egypt, above all by the Italian republics[^650.2]; whence it has come about that in our own time the months of the Julian calendar are named thus by the Arabs of *Egypt*: *yanāyir, fabrāyir, mārs, abrīl, māyū, yūniya, yūliya, oghusṭus, sebtembar, oktūbar, nōfembar, dīsembar*. — Strangely, *mārt, māyus* and *oghostūs* (اغستوس) alone have been received into the Julian year that the *Turks* have used since 1675 in matters concerning the treasury, while the other months are called by Syriac names.

These *Western Arab* forms were derived from Latin not by learned men but by popular tradition; for among the peasants of north-western Africa and of the Sudan the Julian year and the Latin names of the months have outlived the Roman dominion and are still in use, as J. Lippert noted in the Mittheilungen des Seminars für orientalische Sprachen zu Berlin, vol. II, part 2 (Westasiatische Studien), 1899, p. 253[^651.2]. So they are always mentioned in the little books on astronomy written by Algerian and Moroccan Arabs for the use of schools: so Saḥnūn al-Wānsharīshī (on whom see vol.
