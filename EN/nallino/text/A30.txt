[*The lunar tables.* — When al-Battānī's tables are compared with Ptolemy's, what the author relates appears true. To the motion of the moon in longitude he added the correction that he had already applied before (ch. XXVII, p. 42) to the motion of the sun, so that the mean elongation (☾ − ⊙) remains as in Ptolemy. The anomalistic motion too is unchanged.
@p 0314b012..0314b021
Ptolemy's motion of the node, or of the latitude[^314.1], he says he found diminished by 27′ in the interval of about 750 years between him and Ptolemy. Comparing the tables of the two, I find al-Battānī's motion in latitude smaller than Ptolemy's indeed, but not by exactly that amount; it is quite certainly gathered that al-Battānī diminished the motion in latitude by exactly three minutes in a century, which in 750 years makes 22 1/2′ instead of 27′. — The number 27′ of the codex and of Plato is, however, certain, for it is confirmed by what is related in ll. 28–30. If a correction of 27′ appeared in 750 years, in the roughly 1200 years that had elapsed since the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn the correction will be $27' \times \frac{1200}{750} = 43.2'$. These are the 3/4 of a degree by which, as al-Battānī says, the motion in latitude had to be diminished from the epoch of the era to «*this our time*», before the epochs of that motion, noted in the last column of vol. II, p. 72, were computed.
@f 314.1 0314nl001..0314nl002
The motion in latitude in al-Battānī is the sum of the motion in longitude and the motion of the node.
@p 0314b022..0314b031
It may seem strange to some that al-Battānī found a correction of the motion in latitude (or of the motion of the node) of only 27 minutes (22 1/2′ in the tables) in 750 years, when in this space of time the correction of the motion in longitude had come to no less than 4° 31′. The matter is explained thus. The ancient astronomers referred the motion of the moon in longitude to the motion of the sun, using the epochs of observed eclipses; so whatever error affected the motion of the sun affected the motion of the moon in the same interval of time. But the motion of the sun (or the length of the year) depended on observations of the equinoxes, each of which could give an error *of several hours*, indeed *of days*. — The motion of the nodes of the moon, on the other hand, was found from the position of the moon with respect to the nodes in the same eclipses; and this position could easily be determined within *a few minutes of arc* from the magnitude of the eclipses observed.

@p 0321b035..0321b040 + 0322b001..0322b006
The matter stands otherwise if we compare the four eclipses with the more recent tables of the moon. The theory of the moon, although cultivated with the greatest labours by the astronomers of our age, has not yet reached such certainty that the ancient observations of the Babylonians, Greeks and Arabs may safely be judged by it. On the contrary, a few years ago a man above all praise, Theodor Oppolzer, strove hard, in his tables of the syzygies and above all in the great *Canon of Eclipses*, to make those observations in some way help the theory. F. K. Ginzel afterwards took the same road: using the ancient observations of total eclipses of the sun, he very diligently sought to work out new corrections of Oppolzer's Canon[^322.1], which R. Schram finally expressed in small tables admirably suited to the use of astronomers[^322.2]. Thus, through the joint labours of three astronomers, tables have come into being, resting partly on a theoretical and partly on an empirical foundation, which must be considered to represent the course of the moon for ancient times more accurately than any others.

It is gathered at once that in all the eclipses the observation puts the syzygy about a quarter of an hour later than the tables do. Some part of this difference must, I have no doubt, be ascribed to the observations themselves. For the beginning of the obscuration cannot be perceived by the naked eye until some minutes of time have elapsed after the geometrical contact; and this must have happened above all to the ancient astronomers, who could not predict the beginning of an eclipse accurately enough from tables. This uncertainty had no place in noting the end of the obscuration; so in the estimate of the middle of the eclipse there must necessarily have been some retardation.
