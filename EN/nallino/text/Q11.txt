222, 30–37: The fifth method is the one that the astronomers of the caliph al-Maʾmūn used in the *Tested Tables*, as appears from a passage that Steinschneider, *Schriften der Araber in hebräischen Handschriften* (ZDMG, XLVII, 1893), pp. 355–56, adduces by chance from chapter V of the book *Maʿārij al-fikr al-wahīj*, whose author is Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr al-Fārisī, writing in the second half of the seventh century of the hijra (the thirteenth AD)[^441.2]. There are, he says, five methods of constructing the tables of the equation of time, of which the first is Ptolemy's, while the fifth was found by Yaḥyā ibn Abī Manṣūr. «The beginning that he set as the root of the equation of time he took from the beginning of Aries; for when he had found by observation, in the year 199 of Yazdegerd [beginning 28 Apr. 830], the apogee of the Sun in 82° 39′, and had laid it down in his astronomical work, he brought the observed apogee back[^441.3] to the first day of the reign of Yazdegerd; for that day the longitude of the apogee came out about 79° 38′. By means of this apogee, taking the greatest equation of the Sun as 1° 59′, he derived the mean longitude of the Sun for the time when it enters Aries[^441.4], and found the mean longitude of the Sun 358° 4′, the true at the beginning of Aries, and the right ascension of that true longitude[^441.5] 360°. The difference of the ascension [from the mean longitude], then, was 1° 56′; and since the difference concerned the ascension, he had, in order to find the true [time], to subtract 1° 56′ from the ascensions of the true Sun, and then to divide[^442.1] by 15 the difference found between the diminished ascensions and the mean longitude of the Sun; the quotient of the division was the equation of time. — An example of the matter is this: that[^442.2] for the year 634 of Yazdegerd [beginning 9 January 1265] we derive the mean longitude of the Sun so that the difference concerns the mean longitude, taking the apogee of the Sun as 89° 13′ and the greatest equation of the Sun as 1° 59′; and we find the mean longitude of the Sun in 59° 2′, the true in the sign of Gemini .....». Steinschneider does not give what follows.

284–285: These configurations of the stars with the Sun, useless to us, were used in astrology. Ptolemy himself, in the *Tetrab.*, II, 7 (περὶ τοῦ γένους τῶν διατιθεμένων, «on the kind of the events»), for drawing prognostications from eclipses about the nature of the things that will happen to nations, cities, regions, etc., prescribes this among other things: «Of the fixed stars we shall take the one that has the greatest familiarity with the very place of the eclipse, first because of its brightness, in the preceding peg (ἐπὶ τῆς παροιχομένης κεντρώσεως), according to the configurations appearing in nine ways that we have set out in the first Syntaxis [i.e. in the *Almagest*] (κατὰ τοὺς διωρισμένους ἡμῖν ἐν τῇ πρώτῃ Συντάξει τῶν ἐννέα τρόπων φαινομένους σχηματισμούς); then the one found in the peg of the ascendant or of the midheaven that follows the place of the eclipse .....».

287: The passage of al-Qazwīnī is in Wüstenfeld's edition, vol. I, p. 18, with the same readings.
