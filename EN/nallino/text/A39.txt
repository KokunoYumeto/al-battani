[«*The parallax always declines towards the side of latitude in which the degree* [*of the ecliptic*] *is that* etc.».
@fig AB01-PDF0340-F01 AB01-PDF0340-body-F01
@p 0340b026..0340b032 + 0341b001..0341b005
The passage is not clear enough and perhaps somewhat corrupt. If an arc of a great circle is drawn from the zenith to the point of the ecliptic (or rather of the parallel of latitude) at which the moon is, the parallax in latitude becomes southern or northern according as this arc, produced beyond the ecliptic (or the parallel of latitude), tends towards the south or towards the north. Let AB be the ecliptic (or the parallel of latitude), Z the zenith, L the place of the moon; let the arc ZL be produced beyond AB; the moon, which is at L, will be seen lower, at L′, and so the parallax will be towards the side that the arc ZL produced designates, namely the side opposite the zenith. So al-Battānī could have written better: «the parallax always declines towards that side of latitude which the circle from the zenith of the middle of the heaven, produced beyond that degree of the ecliptic, designates». For here it is not a question of the middle of the heaven that is mentioned in ch. XXXV, but of the zenith, which is in the middle of the visible heaven.
@p 0341b006..0341b007
The parallax in longitude, of which al-Battānī speaks afterwards, is towards the side from which the ecliptic approaches the horizon; so towards A if AL is less than 90 degrees.
@p 0341b008..0341b009
Both rules could have been stated more briefly: the parallax in longitude is towards the ascendant if this is nearer, otherwise towards the descendant. — Schiaparelli.]

@p 0341b011..0341b012
*The parallax of Venus and Mercury* is perceptible; in Mercury at perigee it is equal to the parallax of the moon at apogee.
@p 0341b013..0341b020
Here al-Battānī departs from Ptolemy's opinion, IX, 1 (ed. Halma vol. II, p. 115), that the parallaxes of all five planets are imperceptible. But before al-Battānī al-Farghānī had already written, ch. XXVII, p. 100 of Golius's edition: «In the superior planets, namely those that stand above the sun, nothing at all of this parallax [of altitude] is detected that can in any way be distinguished by the senses. As regards the sun, its parallax too is not noticed in observing; but when it is computed from the [known] distance of the sun from the earth, the greatest of it, which is gathered near the horizon, is 3 minutes. *In Venus, however, Mercury and the moon the parallax is perceptible*; especially in the moon …».
@p 0341b021..0341b029
Why the Arabs rejected Ptolemy's opinion Delambre has already noted, pp. 50 and 167–69. Ptolemy does not determine the distances of the spheres of the five planets from the earth; as regards the sun, he deduces from Aristarchus' theory of the distances of the sun and the moon a solar parallax of 2′ 51″ (or in round numbers 3 minutes), but adds that it cannot be computed by direct observations (see p. 77, note 4). For this reason, although, following the tradition of the ancients, he places Venus and Mercury below the sun, he can consider their parallaxes imperceptible. — But al-Farghānī and al-Battānī, like the other Arabs, follow the opinion of those who think the spheres of the planets contiguous, so that the greatest distance of a lower planet is the least distance of the planet immediately above it (see note *a* on ch. L). So the greatest parallax of Mercury must equal the least parallax of the moon.
@p 0341b030..0341b038 + 0342b001..0342b010
Worth noting are the criticisms that Jābir ibn Aflaḥ, in the first half of the twelfth century, made against Ptolemy[^341.1]. After setting out Ptolemy's considerations on the order of the spheres of the planets (*Alm.* IX, 1, ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 114–115), he adds these observations: the Greek astronomer places Venus and Mercury below the sun, to which he attributes a parallax of 2′ 51″ and which he uses in solar eclipses; but he denies that the two inferior planets have a perceptible parallax. Ptolemy afterwards shows that the radius of the epicycle of Venus is 43 1/6ᵖ and the eccentricity 1 1/4ᵖ, in the parts of which 60 are assigned to the radius of the eccentric. So Venus at its greatest distance would have to be more than 104ᵖ from the centre of the earth, at its least less than 16ᵖ. So if the sun, which is always more than 104ᵖ from the earth, has a parallax of about three minutes, how can Ptolemy consider Venus to be without parallax? Venus, at less than 16ᵖ from the centre of the earth, would have to have a parallax of about 1/3 of one of those parts in which we have given the geocentric distance[^342.1]. Likewise, if Mercury stood [immediately] below the sun, it would have to have a parallax of about 7 minutes when it occupied the perigee of the epicycle. It is true that the parallaxes of both planets cannot be observed at perigee, because they are then in conjunction with the sun[^342.2]; but this is possible in places near it, and especially when the planets are on the tangents drawn to the epicycle, for then they have their whole elongation. And when the centre of the epicycle is in one of the nodes, the planet lies in the plane of the ecliptic, and the parallax, then without latitude, must come to about 6′ in Venus and about 4′ in Mercury. — How then could Ptolemy, after attributing a parallax of about 3′ to the sun and none to Venus and Mercury, consider those planets to be below the sun?

@p 0345b009..0345b021
The rule that serves for this is the same as the one by which the distance of the moon at the syzygies is computed (note *d*), when the sine of the equation of the sun is used instead of the sine of the equation of the centre of the moon (5ᵖ 15′). Then 1127 : 60 = 18ᵖ 47′ 0″ will be the factor that must be used to make the reduction just mentioned. Let us investigate how the author found this formula. Let us use the same figure that *Almag.* V, 19 (ed. Halma vol. I, pp. 370–72) has: GA is the ecliptic, Q its pole, Z the zenith, BE and BD the two latitudes of the moon (the northern and the southern, according as one or the other is taken), B the degree of the moon's longitude, and lastly ZT, ZK, ZD arcs of vertical circles. Let the arcs EL, KD be drawn perpendicular to ZK; ABZ will be the angle of latitude, whose complement EBL is the angle of longitude. In the right spherical triangle ELB (= BKD) we have (see p. 181, note 1):
\[ \sin EL = \sin DK = \frac{\sin EB \times \sin EBL}{R} = \frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\,. \]
@p 0349b001..0349b016
If, because of the smallness of the triangles, we consider the figure as rectilinear, we must have the angle LEB = 90° − EBL = 90° − the angle of longitude = the angle of latitude, so that in the spherical triangle ELB or BKD we have *approximately*:
\[ \sin BL = \sin KB = \frac{\sin EB \times \sin LEB}{R} = \frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\,. \]
If the moon is northern, ZL = ZB − BL = ζ − BL; if southern, ZK = ZB + KB = ζ + BL; so, by the equations just found, we shall have, *approximately* at least,
\[ \sin ZL = \sin\left[\zeta - \text{arc}\left(\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\right)\right], \]
\[ \sin ZK = \sin\left[\zeta + \text{arc}\left(\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\right)\right]. \]
The approximation is somewhat better than that of the *Almagest*; for there it is put more simply
\[ BL = \frac{\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\,, \qquad EL = \frac{\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\,, \]
\[ ZL = \zeta - \frac{\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\,, \qquad ZK = \zeta + \frac{\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\,. \]
It remains to compute ZE = *z*[^n]. Ptolemy (see also ed. Halma vol. I, pp. 369–370) takes the triangle ZEL as rectilinear and finds
\[ z^{2} = \overline{ZL}^{2} + \overline{EL}^{2} = \left(\zeta \mp \frac{\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\right)^{2}. \]
Al-Battānī plainly did not want to put simple straight lines for arcs; he substituted their sines for the arcs and then, treating the triangle ZEL as rectilinear, stated:
\[ \sin^{2} z = \sin^{2} ZL + \sin^{2} EL = \sin^{2}\left[\zeta \mp \text{arc}\left(\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. lat.}}{R}\right)\right] + \left(\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\right)^{2}. \]

II. *Correction of the angles.* — Ptolemy, loc. cit., thinks that the corrected angle of longitude is ATZ[^n], or AHZ if the moon is to the south of the ecliptic. Al-Battānī does the same, but wrongly, as we shall say below. Both then, wrongly attributing to spherical triangles the property of rectilinear ones, make
\[ \text{corrected ang. long.} = ATZ = ABZ - BZT, \]
and, for the southern moon,
\[ \text{corrected ang. long.} = AHZ = ABZ + DZK. \]
So far Ptolemy. Al-Battānī teaches the computation of the *variation of the angle*, that is, of BZT, in this way:
\[ \sin BZT = \frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\,, \]
whence
\[ [[(α)]] \text{corrected ang. long.} = \text{ang. long.} \pm \text{arc}\left(\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\right) \]
\[ [[(β)]] \text{corrected ang. lat.} = \text{ang. lat.} \pm \text{arc}\left(\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{R}\right). \]
But the computation of the «variation of the angle», too, is not right. For $\sin BZT = R\,\frac{\sin EL}{\sin ZE}$; so, by the values of these sines found before[^350.1],
\[ \sin BZT = \frac{R\,\dfrac{\sin\beta\,\cos ABZ}{R}}{\sin z} = \frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\,\text{ang. long.}}{\sin z}\,. \]

Instead of drawing the perpendicular arc EL, let an arc of a parallel be described, so that L and E are equally distant from Z; since EL reaches at most 2°, one arc may be substituted for the other. — From the centre O of the sphere let three planes be drawn that determine on the sphere the three arcs, or sides, of the spherical triangle ZBT; at E and L let the tangents ζτ, ζβ be drawn, which plainly meet at a point ζ of the straight line OZ produced.
