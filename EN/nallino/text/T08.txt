On pp. 29–32: the mean syzygies for Egyptian years
@p 0654b002
The author has partly explained the use of the tables in ch. XLII (vol. I, pp. 92–93).
@p 0654b003..0654b011
In these tables, strangely, *Egyptian* years (or *wandering* years, without the Julian intercalation) appear joined with the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn, which elsewhere is always computed in *fixed Julian* years. When the tables are looked at attentively, it appears quite certainly that these wandering years are counted from Dhū l-Qarnayn, but that 1 Thōth, the beginning of the year, always coincides with 1 Thōth of the years of Nabonassar used by Ptolemy in the *Almagest*; so 1 Thōth of the first year from Dhū l-Qarnayn was 1 Thōth 437 of Nabonassar, 9 November 312 BC, that is, 69 days after the beginning of the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn in al-Battānī's usage. Plainly al-Battānī made this table for the use of those who wished to use the *wandering Egyptian* years of Nabonassar with Ptolemy; but why he preferred to count them from Dhū l-Qarnayn I do not know.
@p 0654b012..0654b019
Confusion may also arise from the name «*months of the Copts*» that occurs on p. 31. For according to vol. I, p. 67, ll. 19–20, the *Coptic year* is fixed, and its 1 Thōth (I, p. 68, ll. 23 ff., and p. 69, ll. 7–21) always falls on 29 August; moreover, as I have set out in the note in vol. I, pp. 244–45, al-Battānī's *era of the Copts* is a certain era of Augustus, or Alexandrian era, also used by Theon, with fixed years, whose beginning was 29 August 25 BC, or 287 years, less 3 days, after the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn. Here, however, the months are those of the *wandering* years, which strangely he also calls *years of the Copts* in vol. I, p. 70, ll. 30 ff., adding that they «are Egyptian years from Dhū l-Qarnayn»; and they are taken in the same sense in the precepts for computing the syzygies (vol. I, pp. 92–93).
@p 0654b020..0654b028
It should also be noted that 29 August 25 BC, the beginning of the era of the Copts, or of Augustus, is the first day of Thōth of the year 724 of Nabonassar, 300 of Arrhidaeus, or of the death of Alexander, and the beginning of the year 287 from Dhū l-Qarnayn in the Coptic way of beginning the year (from 29 August). So in vol. I, pp. 70 and 92–93, to convert the wandering years of the Copts counted from Dhū l-Qarnayn into fixed years, al-Battānī bids us take 287 from the given years and divide the remainder by 4. Indeed the beginning of the year 287 of Dhū l-Qarnayn, in the *Coptic* way of beginning, falls on 29 August both in the wandering and in the fixed years, as it does also in the three following years; this will afterwards happen again only in the four years of which the first is the year 287 + 365 × 4 = 1747 of Dhū l-Qarnayn. Al-Battānī's tables do not reach that year.
@p 0654b029
The columns of the tables give the following:
@p 0654b030
I. The given years or months, with which one must enter the tables.
@p 0654b031..0654b032
II. The days of the first Egyptian month (Thōth), and the sixtieths of the days, at which the first mean conjunction or opposition of the given year happens.
@p 0654b033
III. The mean longitude of the sun at the moment of time given in II.
@p 0654b034
IV. The mean anomaly of the moon at the same moment.
@p 0654b035
V. The argument of latitude of the moon, or its distance from the northern node.
@p 0654b036..0654b041 + 0655b001..0655b004
Ptolemy[^654.1] gives in III not the mean longitude of the sun but the distance (ἀποχή) of the sun from the apogee, which he wrongly thought fixed at 65° 30′; in V the argument of latitude of the moon counted from the northern limit (= longitude of the node + 90°). In the rest the construction of the tables is the same in both; but the elements for the collected years differ a little, because of the different motion of the sun in al-Battānī's tables and because of the interval of longitude between the city of Alexandria and al-Raqqa. Ptolemy set out his elements in VI, 2 (ed. Halma, vol. I, pp. 374–78), which see also in Delambre, *Histoire de l'astronomie ancienne*, vol. II, pp. 223–24; al-Battānī does not give his. To find the size of the discrepancy I compute, by the *Almagest*, the first conjunction of the *wandering* year 915 of Dhū l-Qarnayn, 1351 of Nabonassar. The last year of Ptolemy's table is 1101 of Nabonassar;

@p 0655b018..0655b024
**Pp. 29–30**, for the *collected years*. Following Ptolemy's example, he chose periods of 25 years, because 25 wandering years (or 9125 days) contain almost exactly 309 lunations; the difference to be taken from those 9125 days reaches only 0ᵈ 2′ 47″ 5‴, or 0ᵈ 1ʰ 6ᵐ 50ˢ, so that through many periods of 25 wandering years there will be only one conjunction and one opposition in the month of Thōth of the first year of the period, and the times of such conjunctions or oppositions will differ only a little from one another. — The table of the oppositions is made by taking half a lunation, or 14ᵈ 45′ 55″, from the days of the conjunctions, and the motions answering to this half lunation from the motions of the conjunctions.

@p 0655b038..0655b041 + 0656b001..0656b006
**P. 32**, for the *single years*. It agrees exactly with Ptolemy's table, VI, 3 (ed. Halma vol. I, p. 382); its computation is very easy. The excess of 13 lunations over the 365 days of the wandering year is 18ᵈ 53′ 51″ 43‴; so if we call A the days of the month of Thōth, and the sixtieths of a day, answering to the first new moon (or full moon) of some year, the first new moon (or full moon) of the following year will happen at the time A + 18ᵈ 53′ 52″. If this exceeds a whole lunation, or 29ᵈ 31′ 50″, it will be the second new moon of the month of Thōth, or (if it exceeds 30 days) the new moon of the following month; then, to find the first conjunction or opposition of the year, one must take a lunation from the sum, and from the motions of the other columns the motions answering to a lunation. — In the column of the days are to be corrected: 40″ for 50″ under the year 6, 39″ for 29″ under 11, 24″ for 34″ under 14, 23″ for 13″ under 19.

*The limits of the eclipses* (ὅροι ἐκλειπτικοί). — Al-Battānī states in chs. XLII and XLIV (vol. I, pp. 96 and 104) that he has given them «on the page of the months of the syzygies», and in that place indeed the limits occur in the Spanish version (ff. 41v and 56v); but the Escorial codex gives them here, under the table of the single years, and on p. 88. Ptolemy gave his in the table of the syzygies for the single years, teaching their computation afterwards (VI, 5, ed. Halma vol. I, pp. 388–96); because of the different equation of the sun and the different angular diameters of the luminaries and of the shadow, the *Ptolemaic* ones necessarily differ from *al-Battānī's*. Since al-Battānī nowhere teaches how he computed the limits, I had tried to correct approximately the numbers of the codex that concern the *solar eclipses*, which are affected by most evident mistakes, choosing the numbers from which the errors of the copyists seemed most easily to have arisen, and keeping as far as possible the proportions of the limits of the Almagest. That the reason for the doubt in emending may appear better, I shall set out the method by which Ptolemy fixed the limits of the *solar eclipses*. Let Σ be the sum of the semidiameters of the sun and the moon in eclipses, or the distance of the centres needed for a contact. The parallax of the moon cannot be neglected; Ptolemy considers that the greatest parallax in latitude that can occur in all the seven climes of the earth, contained between Meroë (whose longest day is 13ʰ) and the mouths of the Borysthenes (where the longest day is 16ʰ), is 58′ to the south and 8′ to the north; and that the greatest lunar parallaxes in longitude are then 15′ (in Scorpio and Pisces) and 30′ (in Leo and Gemini). So the apparent latitude of the moon needed for a contact will be at most Σ + 58′ for the northern moon and Σ + 8′ for the southern. From these latitudes, by the tables or by the rule
