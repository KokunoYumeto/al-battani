@p 0758b016..0758b031
**Terms.** — «Although the sign itself is under another's power and has the domicile of another», as Firmicus II, 6 relates, «yet its parts are divided among the single stars as if their own, and these parts are called the *bounds* of the stars: the Greeks call these bounds ὅρια». In Arabic *ḥadd* (pl. *ḥudūd*), whence the *termini* of our astrologers of the Middle Ages. A threefold doctrine of the terms or bounds is handed down in the *Tetrabiblos* I, 21 (= I, 18 and 19 of the Frankfurt version)[^758.2]. The first is the *Egyptian*, which, without any evident and certain principle, distributes the degrees of each sign unequally among the five planets[^758.3]. The second, the *Chaldaean*, suited to the government of the triplicities, distributes the degrees of each sign on this principle: 8° + 7° + 6° + 5° + 4° = 30°. Neither pleases Ptolemy: he judges the Egyptian too irregular, the Chaldaean simpler and more probable; yet more credit is to be given to the Egyptian because of its ancient tradition. But afterwards he puts forward a *third* doctrine, which he says he found in «an ancient volume partly consumed by age», and which is more rational than the Egyptian. — Although errors are not wanting in the numbers of the codex, it is easily recognized that this table of ours gives the terms of Ptolemy: I have emended everything on the authority of the *Frankfurt* version of the Tetrabiblos, from which Bouché-Leclercq (p. 211, relying on the Greek-Latin edition received by Junctinus into the *Speculum Astrologiae*) differs only in the sign of Gemini, assigning 6° to Jupiter and 6° to Mars[^758.4].
@p 0758b032

@p 0758b034 + 0759b001..0759b011
The Arabs mostly follow the Egyptian system. For Albohazen I, 5 (pp. 15–16 of the editions of 1551 and 1571) observes: «Although the ancient sages disagree about the terms and hold five opinions — one of which is that of the *Egyptians*, another that of *Ptolemy*, the third that of those *of Babylonia*, the fourth that of the *Indians*, the fifth that of *Attarathyh* — yet the opinion of the greater part, and the more truthful and the more tested, is the opinion of the Egyptians, since few use the terms of Ptolemy, and all reject the opinions of the others altogether and do not deign to look at them, because they are far from the truth». Albumasar, *Introductorium* V, 8: «some.... are the terms of the Egyptians, others of the Chaldaeans, others of Ptolemy, others of Aristotue, others of the Indians»; but he observes that the Egyptian are used most of all[^759.1]. Alchabitius I, 8 (f. aa 5v of the edition of 1485) gives only «the terms of the Egyptians, and they are said to be those of Hermes»; and ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān, in his commentary on the Tetrabiblos, states: «Hermes, Dorotheus, and Mellius[^759.2] and other astrologers chose the Egyptian terms rather than the others».
@p 0759b012..0759b019
**Triplicities.** — The τρίγωνα of the Greeks, the *trigona* or *triquetra* of the Latins, the *muthallathāt* (sing. *muthallatha*) of the Arabs, which the translators of our Middle Ages mostly rendered, rather ineptly, *triplicitates*. The twelve signs of the zodiac are divided into four equilateral triangles, so that each contains only signs of the same sex (masculine or feminine). Over each triangle two planets preside as lords, to whom a third planet is added as a kind of partner; of the lords (οἰκοδεσπόται or τριγωνοκράτορες, *rabb*, pl. *arbāb*) one holds the power (οἰκοδεσποτεία) by day, the other by night. In the figure the outer part of the circle shows the lords by day, the inner part the lords by night. For the scheme of the triplicities is this:
@t spec=lll 0759b020..0759b025
1st, fiery: | Aries, Leo, Sagittarius | with the Sun (by day), Jupiter (by night), Saturn
2nd, earthy: | Taurus, Virgo, Capricorn | with Venus (by day), the Moon (by night), Mars
3rd, airy: | Gemini, Libra, Aquarius | with Saturn (by day), Mercury (by night), Jupiter
4th, watery: | Cancer, Scorpio, Pisces | with Venus (by day), Mars (by night), the Moon

On this division into triplicities there seems to be no disagreement; cf. *Tetrabiblos* I, 19 (= I, 16 of the Frankfurt version), Firmicus II, 11, Zahel [= Sahl ibn Bishr], *de interrogationibus* ch. I, p. 111v (Venice 1519, with ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān), Albumasar V, 11 (under the false title «on the terms of the Chaldaeans»), Alchabitius I, 4 (f. aa 3v–aa 4r), *Propädeutik* 52–53, *Mafātīḥ al-ʿulūm* 225–26, etc.[^759.3]

**Faces.** — Unknown to Ptolemy, they are called by the Greeks δεκανοί or also πρόσωπα «faces, likenesses»[^759.4]; by the ancient Latins *decani*; by the Arabs *wajh* (pl. *wujūh*) «face», *ṣūra* (pl. *ṣuwar*) «images»[^759.5], *darījān*[^759.6], *dahaj*[^759.7]; by the astrologers of the Middle Ages *facies*, rarely *decani*. They are the thirds of the signs; for the signs of the zodiac are divided into three equal parts of 10 degrees each, so that there are 12 × 3 = 36 decans or faces. Among the Egyptians they were consecrated to particular gods; among the Greek astrologers, who departed from the ancient Egyptian tradition, they are assigned to the Sun, the Moon and the five planets on this principle: beginning from Mars, to whom the first 10° of Aries belong, the other heavenly bodies are placed in the order of the spheres. This system of the *Greeks* is given by the figure of our codex; the same is found in Firmicus[^760.1], Paul of Alexandria, Demophilus, Alchabitius (I, 8, f. aa 5v–aa 6r), ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān (on the *Tetrabiblos* I, 22), Albumasar V, 12, the *Propädeutik* p. 53, Albohazen I, 3 (pp. 3–4); and on it rest the predictions of Pseudo-Abū Maʿshar about men and women according to the face that the ascendant occupied at the time of birth[^760.2].
@p 0760b009..0760b020
Besides this system, which Albohazen I, 5 (p. 16) calls that of the Greeks, and Albumasar, loc. cit., that «according to the Persians, the Babylonians and the Egyptians», two others are mentioned by the Arab writers. «Likewise [the ancient sages] disagree», says Albohazen I, 5 (p. 16), «about the faces, and hold three opinions: one of which is that of the *Greeks*, and it follows the order of the heavens, beginning from Mars; another is that of the *Indians*, and it is begun upon the order of the triplicities; another is that of *Alkindi*[^760.3]: of these the first is the more truthful, and they agree on it, and the greater part of men uses it». — The Indian system is taught by Albumasar V, 13; although the printed Latin version suffers from corruptions[^760.4], one certainly gathers that the lords of the faces are the seven planets, so arranged that first come the planets that have a domicile[^760.5] in the signs of the first triplicity (Aries, Leo, Sagittarius), then those that have a domicile in the signs of the second triplicity (Taurus, Virgo, Capricorn), etc.; so the series is this: Mars, the Sun, Jupiter, Venus, Mercury, Saturn, etc.
@p 0760b021..0760b034 + 0761b001..0761b005
The system of al-Kindī is set out in another place of Albohazen (II, 2, p. 53), where one would least have expected it: «Further, the error into which Alkindus fell is that he denied the division of the ancient sages for the faces of the signs, and he says in that book of his of 4. *aphorisms*[^760.6] what we now set down word for word, as it is in that book. And each of the signs (he says) is divided into three parts, and in each part there are ten degrees, and these parts are called faces, because they are the significators of the faces; and the greater part of men gave the first face of Aries to Mars, the second to the Sun, and the third to Venus; and so, according to this order, in all the faces of the signs, by the order of the heavens of the planets, one after another, until the last face of Pisces falls to Mars. But this division is not well laid down, nor does it agree with itself: for the first face of Aries follows the last face of Pisces, and so by this division two faces, one after the other, are given to one planet. But the division will be right and whole according to our understanding, as we have set it down in our larger book, which is called the *Book of the secrets of the stars*, in which we have made the division of the faces by the order of the signs in this way: we gave the first face of Aries to Mars, who is the lord[^760.7] of Aries, the second to Venus, who is the lady of Taurus, the third to Mercury, who is the lord of Gemini, and so on in sequence to the end of the signs; in the same way we would have the first face of the next fiery sign be Mars's, and by this order the last face of Scorpio will be Jupiter's, who is the lord of Pisces, and the last face of Pisces likewise Jupiter's, who is the lord of Pisces, and the division of the first face, that is, of Aries, returns to Mars. Thus far Alkindus»[^761.1][^n].

On the decans according to the Greek astrologers Bouché-Leclercq has treated pp. 215–35; on pp. 43–44 he also rightly claims an Egyptian origin for the decans, as does Fr. @p 0761b012..0761b036
The Syriac writer Bardesanes, born in AD 154 and dead in 222, calls the faces by the Greek name *deqansē* ܕܩ̈ܢܣܐ[^761.2]; in the eleventh or twelfth century AD the book of the *Cause of Causes* has ܕܩ̈ܢܣܐ and *parṣūphē* ܦܪ̈ܨܘܦܐ (πρόσωπα). But in Barhebraeus, *Astr. Syr.*, pp. 102 and 230 of the text, 86 and 198 of the translation, the word ܕܩ̈ܢܣܐ denotes the *fixed stars*, which is strange enough and seems to me to be explained in this way. Among the Greek astrologers one frequently meets lists of the constellations that rise together with the single decans (τὰ παρανατέλλοντα), as appears from the fragments of Teucer[^761.3] and many other writings; likewise lists of those that they called παρανατέλλοντες, that is, of the more notable fixed stars (λαμπροὶ ἀστέρες) that rise together with a given sign or a given degree of the zodiac[^761.4], or also with a given decan. For these παρανατέλλοντα, «co-rising [signs]», and παρανατέλλοντες, «co-rising [stars]», were of great moment in predictions; therefore Demophilus, in Porphyry's *Introductio in Cl. Ptolemaei opus de effectibus astrorum*, Basel 1559 (at the end of the commentary of Pseudo-Proclus on the Tetrabiblos), p. 199, observes: Ὑπόκεινται τούτοις τοῖς δεκανοῖς οἱ εἰρημένοι ἐν τῷ ζωδιακῷ κύκλω παρανατέλλοντες[^n] «under these decans lie [the stars] that are called co-rising with the zodiac»[^761.5]; Paul of Alexandria, of the fourth century AD, has a chapter περὶ τῶν λϛʹ δεκανῶν καὶ τῶν παρανατελλόντων ἐν αὐτοῖς ἀστέρων καὶ τῶν προσώπων αὐτῶν «on the 36 decans, and the stars that rise with them, and the planets that are lords of the decans» (in Bouché-Leclercq p. 227, n. 3); Pseudo-Ptolemy in the Καρπός or *Centiloquium*, no. 95: κινδυνεύει τά παρανατέλλοντα ἑκάστω δεκανῷ δηλοῦν τὴν προαίρεσιν τοῦ γεννωμένου καὶ τὴν τέχνην ἣν μετέρχεται «[the constellations] that rise with the single decans may signify the inclination of the native and the art to which he tends»; and in a certain Byzantine astrologer we find mentioned τῶν παρανατελλόντων τὰ δεκανικὰ ἀποτελέσματα «the decanic effects of the co-rising [stars or constellations]». A larger supply of examples is in Boll, pp. 77, 78, 79. Hence it came about that οἱ παρανατέλλοντες signified the *fixed stars*, never the planets. — From the connection of the decans with constellations and with single stars, by then frequent, a new meaning of the word ܕܩ̈ܢܣܐ could arise.

**Domiciles.** — In Greek οἶκοι and also τόποι, *domicilia* among the ancient Latins, in Arabic *bayt* (pl. *buyūt*), *domus* and *domicilia* among our astrologers of the Middle Ages. After Ptolemy (*Tetrabiblos* I, 18 = I, 15 of the Frankfurt version) there is no disagreement among the astrologers about the domiciles; the twelve signs of the zodiac are so distributed among the seven planets that the Sun and the Moon receive one sign each, the others two each, as their houses, as it were, in which they may freely rejoice (χαίρειν). So the planet is the lord (οἰκοδεσπότης, *ṣāḥib*, *rabb*) of its domicile. As is gathered from the figure itself, the domiciles are these:

**Exaltations.** — The ὑψώματα of the Greeks, the *sublimitates* of Pliny, the *altitudines* of Firmicus, the *sharaf* (pl. *ashrāf*) «elevation, dignity, glory» of the Arabs, whence the *exaltationes* of the astrologers of the Middle Ages[^762.1]. The exaltation is the sign of the zodiac in which a planet has its greatest power (so Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* I, 20 = I, 17 of the Frankfurt version; *Propädeutik* pp. 50–51; *Mafātīḥ al-ʿulūm* p. 225), or a particular degree of that sign (as most astrologers hold). On the signs all agree; on the degrees there is perhaps a disagreement only in the exaltation of Saturn. The numbers gathered from Albumasar V, 5 («on the dominion of the stars according to the other astrologers»), Alchabitius I, 3 (f. aa 3v), al-Akhḍarī pp. 61–63, Muḥammad al-Sūsī, loc. cit., the Jew Ibn ʿEzra (*Opera astrologica*, Venice 1507, p. iv f.), and this Escorial codex of ours (where in Cancer يد 14° stands wrongly for يه 15°) agree entirely with the amounts set out in the poem of Dorotheus of Sidon[^762.2]:
