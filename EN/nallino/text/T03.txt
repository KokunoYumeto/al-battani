@p 0647b022

@p 0647b023..0647b030
Two systems of signs occur: the one, which I shall call I, on the left side of the page, the other (II) on the right. II, which is left out in the Spanish version (f. 29r), seems to me *spurious*; it is wholly useless, since I is already given, and it is the less convenient, because to find the sign of a given year one must always add the sign of the month al-Muḥarram to the sign of that year written in the table. Thus the sign of the first year of the hijra will be 7 + 5 = 5, the sign of the second 4 + 5 = 2, and so on. In system I the sign of the year is already in the table, and an addition is needed only to find the signs of the months that follow the first (al-Muḥarram). Al-Battānī, loc. cit., sets out system I; II he does not even mention[^647.1].

@p 0648b003..0648b008
«Collected years» (*sinūn majmūʿa*) mean, among the Arab astronomers, the sum of the years from the epoch of the era, not a span of years counted from any moment of time. «Single years», or, as they are called in Arabic, «spread-out years» (*sinūn mabsūṭa*), are years taken one by one and counted from any time. The names of the *collected* and the *spread-out* years crept from the Arabic writings into the *Toledan* and the *Alfonsine Tables* and many others of the Middle Ages and of more recent times.
@p 0648b009..0648b010
Among the Arabs, as among the ancient Latins and all astronomers, the week begins with Sunday (the first weekday); Saturday (the seventh weekday) closes the week.
@p 0648b011..0648b027 + 0649b001..0649b006
Al-Battānī puts the epoch of the hijra on *Thursday* (the fifth weekday), 15 July 622. Of the Arab astronomers[^648.1] whom I have consulted, only Ḥabash and al-Khāzinī (f. 22v, etc.) follow the Muslim civil usage of putting the epoch of the hijra on Friday (the sixth weekday), 16 July. It is worth giving in his own words what Ḥabash says, f. 2v: «Since I had found that the narrators of the sacred traditions[^648.2] and the men most expert in computing put the new moon (مستهَلّ) of al-Muḥarram of the year of the Arab[^648.3] Prophet Muḥammad (may God be gracious to him and grant eternal salvation to him and to the pure family of his house) on a Thursday, I investigated whether this was true (يصحّ) according to computation. I found that the conjunction of the luminaries for the new moon of al-Muḥarram of the first year of the hijra was at the city of Mecca after the middle of the night whose morning (صبيحة) was a Wednesday [i.e. the day of Mercury], and by computing I found that the appearance of the new moon at Mecca was in the night of the Friday of the new moon of al-Muḥarram of the year of the hijra. So we found nothing that pointed to Thursday, either in the conjunction or in the appearance; whence it became plain to us that those people had counted al-Muḥarram from the night of Thursday only according to common usage, [making], throughout their computation, the elapsed month of Dhū l-Ḥijja one of 29 days[^648.4]. So we have returned to the appearance of the new moon, and have fixed the beginning of al-Muḥarram of the first year of the hijra on the night of Friday at Mecca, [whose] morning was the 28th day of the month Afrawardīn-māh of the year 44 of King Abarwīz[^648.5], and 9 years and 338 days before King Yazdajird. Likewise, the night whose morning was the 16th day of Tammūz [July] of the year 933 of Alexander; and from the first day of the first Tishrīn[^649.1] to 15 Tammūz, counting the whole 15th, there were 288 days. Likewise the morning of the 23rd day of the month Kiyahk of the year 1389 from Bukhtanaṣṣar; and from the first day of Tūt to 22 Kiyahk, counting the 22nd day, there were 112 days, which, with the five epagomenal days (*al-ayyām al-lawāḥiq*) added, make 117. We have added these five because we count from the [Persian month] Daymāh[^649.2], [after which], when 12 [months] have elapsed, the year of the Copts is complete».

@p 0649b007..0649b010
According to the civil usage of the Muslims the day is counted from sunset, so that the night belongs to the following morning. The astronomers count the day from noon (vol. I, p. 221); so the night of Friday in civil usage becomes, with them, the night of Thursday. The discrepancy of a day in the epoch of the hijra is explained in this way. Ḥabash, however, relied on other computations.
@p 0649b011..0649b025
The Prophet seems to have prescribed the use of lunar months to the Meccans in the year 9 of the hijra, in which he probably published the verses IX, 36–37 of the *Qurʾān*[^649.3]; he repeated the precepts in the following year in his last address[^649.4]. Which calendar the Meccans used before is not clear; for the conjectures of very learned men see A. Caussin de Perceval, *Mémoire sur le calendrier arabe avant l'Islamisme* (Journal Asiatique, series IV, vol. 1, 1843, pp. 342–79); Mahmoud Effendi, *Mémoire sur le calendrier arabe avant l'Islamisme et sur la naissance et l'âge du prophète Mohammad* (Journal Asiatique, V, 11, 1858, pp. 109–92); A. Sprenger, *Ueber den Kalender der Araber vor Muḥammad* (Zeitschrift der deutschen morgenländischen Gesellschaft, vol. XIII, 1859, pp. 134–75); J. Wellhausen, *Reste arabischen Heidentums*, 2nd edition, Berlin 1897, pp. 94–101; H. Winckler, *Altorientalische Forschungen*, series II, vol. II (1900), pp. 324–50 and 374–81. Winckler[^649.5] later changed his opinion a little, stating that in the Prophet's time the Meccans had a *solar* calendar, brought after 525 by the Christians of southern Arabia under the leadership of Abraha, and that the Prophet, after taking Mecca in the year 8 H, restored the very ancient *lunar* calendar of the Meccans, which had once belonged to the cult of the moon-god Hubal.

@p 0649b026..0649b033
Some, whom al-Ṭabarī reviews (*Annales*, series I, vol. III, pp. 1250–56), say that the epoch of the era was instituted by Muḥammad himself; most, and more rightly, say by the caliph ʿUmar, in the year 16 (or 17, or 18). The epoch is the 1st day of al-Muḥarram of the year in which the Prophet, leaving the city of Mecca, reached al-Madīna; and he arrived, according to the Arab astronomers and ours, on Monday 8 Rabīʿ I in civil usage (20 September 622), or on Sunday the 7th of the same month (19 September) in the astronomical reckoning of time. See Mahmoud Effendi, loc. cit., p. 121; Loth, *al-Kindî als Astrolog*, pp. 293–94; al-Bīrūnī, *Chronology*, p. 330 of the text, 327–28 of the translation. Those Arabs who choose the day 12 Rabīʿ I are wrong.
