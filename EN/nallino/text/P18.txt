V. *The translations of the astronomical work.* — There are three: two Latin, the third Spanish.
@p 0058b002..0058b004
*a) The Latin of Robert of Chester.* — It is well known that Ptolemy's Planisphaerium has come down to us complete only in the Latin translation of Rudolf of Bruges[^58.1][^n], who as translator inserted here and there observations both of his own and of the Arab Maslem (i.e. Maslama al-Majrīṭī).
@n Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part II, p. XVI) report that A. A. Björnbo, *Hermannus Dalmata als Uebersetzer astronomischer Arbeiten* (Bibliotheca Mathematica, 3rd series, vol. 4, 1903, pp. 130–33), has settled the question of the author and the year of this version, which was still open when Nallino wrote: the author of the preface is the translator of the book himself, Hermann of Dalmatia, or Secundus, and the version was made in 1143.
@p 0058b005..0058b014
At the beginning of the book Ptolemy teaches how the meridian, the colures and the tropic circles are to be drawn in the plane of the equator (that is, in stereographic projection), and how afterwards the ecliptic is to be drawn, which, without a suitable demonstration, he also takes to be represented by a circle. Then the Latin translator adds this: «Metitur itaque (ait Ptolemaeus) descriptio nostra utrumque arcum *NG*, et *GH* partibus XXIII. punctis fere LI. ex eis quae CCCLX. totum *ABGD* circulum metiuntur, quae par est distantia [sic] utriusque tropici a circulo aequinoctiali. Quem locum a Ptolemaeo minus diligenter perspectum cum Albatene miratur et Alchoarismus: quorum hunc quidem ope *nostra* Latinum habet, illius vero commodissima translatio Roberti mei industria, Latinae orationis thesaurum accumulat: Nos discutiendi verbi rationem in libro nostro de circulis damus» (our drawing, then (says Ptolemy), measures each of the arcs *NG* and *GH* at about 23 parts 51 points, of those of which 360 measure the whole circle *ABGD*, which is the equal distance [sic] of each tropic from the equinoctial circle. This passage, examined by Ptolemy less carefully than it should have been, both Albatene and Alchoarismus wonder at: of whom Latin has the latter by *our* help, while the most convenient translation of the former, by the industry of my Robert, adds to the treasury of the Latin language. We give the account of how the statement is to be examined in our book on circles)[^58.2].

Delambre, *Histoire de l'astronomie ancienne*, Paris 1817, vol. II, p. 434, confusing the Latin translator with Maslem, explains the passage thus: «Albategnius, dit le traducteur arabe Maslem, a remarqué que cette démonstration de Ptolémée laissait beaucoup à désirer: Alchoarism a été du même avis. Le traducteur latin partage ce sentiment; et pour compléter la démonstration, il renvoye à un ouvrage que nous n'avons pas» (Albategnius, says the Arab translator Maslem, remarked that this demonstration of Ptolemy left much to be desired; Alchoarism was of the same opinion. The Latin translator shares this view, and to complete the demonstration he refers to a work that we do not have). That *Albatene* is al-Battānī was not called into doubt by Steinschneider either, on Baldi, p. 452 (extr. p. 27), nor by Wüstenfeld[^58.3][^n].

@p 0058b022..0058b028
But in none of his books did al-Battānī have occasion to mention this construction of the planisphere, not to say to censure it. Al-Khwārizmī had such an opportunity in his lost book on constructing the astrolabe, which, however, we do not know that the Latins of the Middle Ages knew and translated[^58.4]; in the astronomical work (*zīj*) it seems to me hardly likely. But perhaps for «*miratur*» (wonders at) of the printed edition we should read «*miraretur*» (would wonder at); as though the Latin translator and glossator, to show off his learning, had said: both those famous astronomers would undoubtedly have censured Ptolemy's error, had they had occasion to deal with this matter.
@p 0058b029..0058b032 + 0059b001..0059b006
I do not think that the name *Albatene* is corrupt and designates a man other than al-Battānī. For the Robert who is here said to have translated him into Latin is plainly Robertus Cataneus, mentioned in the preface of Rudolf of Bruges to the Planisphaerium (p. 232), whose purpose it was to render Arabic books into Latin. He is usually called Robertus Retinensis, Ketenensis, Cestrensis, Castrensis, etc.; English by nation[^59.1]; alive in the years 1141 and 1143; he died at Pamplona in Spain[^59.2]. That this same Robert composed astronomical canons for the city of London according to al-Battānī's tables I have already said on pp. xxxiv–xxxv; from this it is gathered that Robert had al-Battānī's whole work, chapters and tables, in his hands, and what had been inferred from the words of Rudolf of Bruges is confirmed. Of the version of Robert of Chester nothing survives today, so that we do not know whether it comprised the whole work or only a part[^59.3].
@p 0059b007..0059b022
*b) The Latin of Plato of Tivoli.* — It has been printed twice. The first edition is entitled *Rudimenta astronomica* Alfragrani. *Item* Albategnius *astronomus peritissimus de motu stellarum ex observationibus tum propriis, tum Ptolemaei, omnia cum demonstrationibus geometricis et additionibus* Ioannis de Regiomonte, Nuremberg 1537. Alfraganus takes up 26 leaves, Albatenius 90, numbered separately. This second part is preceded by the *Praefatio Platonis Tiburtini in Albategnium*, which begins: «Inter universa liberalium artium studia, quae Graecos, quaeque etiam prius invenisse constat Aegyptios, quae stellarum scientiam profitetur disciplina, et est et habetur iure princeps» (among all the studies of the liberal arts that, as is agreed, the Greeks, and still earlier the Egyptians, discovered, the discipline that professes the science of the stars both is and is held by right to be the first). The version begins on f. 1v with these words: «In nomine Domini incipit liber Mahometi filii Gebir filii Crueni [read Cineni], qui vocatur Albategni, in numeris stellarum, et in locis motuum earum, experimenti ratione conceptorum, in quo LVII. capitula continuantur» (in the name of the Lord here begins the book of Mahometus son of Gebir son of Crueni [read Cineni], who is called Albategni, on the numbers of the stars and the places of their motions, established by way of experiment, in which 57 chapters are contained). There follows the list of the chapters, after which comes this (f. 3v): «Machometus Tinen filius Acharrani, qui et Albategni dictus, inquit: in cunctorum operum initiis omniumque rerum exordiis, principatus, et excellentia Dei laudi nomine, cuius exaltetur ac ipsius gloriae deputetur.....» (Machometus Tinen son of Acharrani, who is also called Albategni, says: at the beginnings of all works and the openings of all things, primacy and excellence to the praise of the name of God, whose [name] be exalted and be ascribed to his glory.....). It ends on fol. 90r: «Simili quoque modo si necesse fuerit, ut per eas altitudo Lunae, vel aliarum stellarum accipiatur, cum longitudinis arcum a zenith capitis de 60 minueris, reliquum erit altitudinis arcus» (in the same way too, if it is necessary that the altitude of the Moon or of other stars be taken with them, when you subtract the arc of the distance from the zenith of the head from 60, the remainder will be the arc of the altitude)[^n].
@n So the print; the arc of the altitude is the complement of the zenith distance, and the two codices quoted on p. lii read «de 90».
@p 0059b023..0059b031 + 0060b001..0060b004
The second edition is entitled Mahometis Albatenii *de scientia stellarum liber cum aliquot additionibus* Ioannis Regiomontani. *Ex Bibliotheca Vaticana transcriptus*, Bologna 1645; it has 228 pages, besides 8 unnumbered leaves at the beginning. The editor is Bernardinus Ugolottus, who dedicates the book to Ferdinand II, Grand Duke of Tuscany, and says this in the preface: «We present Albategnius to you, friendly reader, exactly as we received him, transcribed from the Vatican Library by order of Luca Valerio, the distinguished mathematician and once public professor at Rome; that is, lacking the tables and a few figures, and translated from the Arabic idiom into Latin by Plato of Tivoli in a half-barbarous style». From this and from the title of the edition you would think that the book was printed from a manuscript codex of the Vatican. Not at all; for, as Halley had already noticed, it differs from the earlier edition only in adding many other misprints to its misprints and, at the beginning of chapter XXVII, in putting a marginal gloss (mentioned by me on p. 204) in place of Plato's words. The copy of the Vatican Library was therefore nothing but the Nuremberg edition itself.

@f 59.3 0059nr001..0059nr012
With no foundation at all Wüstenfeld asserts, p. 46 (and Suter, *Die Mathematiker*, p. 46, agrees with him), that Robert translated al-Battānī's work in the recension of Maslama al-Majrīṭī. Maslama had reduced to a compendium only al-Battānī's tables of the equations of the luminaries and the planets; Robert, on the contrary, we know to have adapted also the tables of all the mean motions to the meridian of London (the equations remain unchanged everywhere on earth). Wüstenfeld was undoubtedly led into error by Maslama's observations on the Planisphaerium, reported here and there by Rudolf of Bruges.
