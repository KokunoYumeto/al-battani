162–163: Theon's strange definitions are also in the unpublished *Astrology* of Vettius Valens, book II, in the chapter on the winds of the stars (περὶ ἀνέμων τῶν ἀστέρων). For, on the testimony of Salmasius (*de annis climactericis*, pp. 421–22), Valens, putting with the other astrologers the exaltation (ὑψοῦσθαι) of the Sun in 19° of Aries, and thence considering a square whose vertices are 19° of Aries, 19° of Cancer, 19° of Libra and 19° of Capricorn, says that from 19° of Aries to the same degree of Cancer the Sun καταβαίνειν τὸν βοῤῥᾶν (descends the north), from 19° of Cancer to the like degree of Libra καταβαίνειν τὸν νότον (descends the south), from 19° of Libra to 19° of Capricorn ἀναβαίνειν τὸν νότον (ascends the south), and from 19° of Capricorn to the same degree of Aries βοῤῥᾶν ἀναβαίνειν (ascends the north). And similarly of the other planets. The Moon he considers differently, book I, in the chapter περὶ εὑρέσεως βαθμῶν καὶ ἀνέμων τῆς σελήνης (on the testimony of Salmasius, p. 422): from Leo to Libra καταβαίνειν τὰ βόρεια (to descend the northern parts), from Scorpio to Cancer[^n] καταβαίνειν τὰ νότια (to descend the southern parts), from Aquarius to Aries ἀναβαίνειν τὰ νότια (to ascend the southern parts), from Taurus to Cancer ἀναβαίνειν τὰ βόρεια (to ascend the northern parts); which I think Valens said because, according to the astrologers, the Moon has its exaltation in 3° of Taurus; the vertices of the square are therefore Taurus, Leo, Scorpio and Aquarius. — Julian of Laodicea agrees with al-Battānī's usage, asserting in the passage mentioned before (p. LXXI) that the Sun, Venus and Mercury, standing at the longitudes 214° 8′ 50″, 231° 28′ and 224° 32′, «descend to the south» (κατέρχεται νότον), and the Moon and Saturn, at 48° 10′ and 24° 28′, «ascend to the north» (ἀνέρχεται βοῤῥᾶν). I think there is a copyist's corruption in Jupiter (154° 12′) and Mars (137° 35′), which are also said ἀνέρχεται βοῤῥᾶν.

163–164, n. *b*: Likewise al-Khāzinī, f. 151r–v, and Kūshyār ibn Labbān (in the passage that I mentioned on p. 325) give a table of right ascensions computed from the beginning of Capricorn. One of the problems of the book *de saphaea*, whose author is al-Zarqālī, is: to find the longitude of a star from its right ascension computed from the beginning of Capricorn (i.e. from the degree with which the star culminates) and its declination; Steinschneider translated this badly, *Études sur Zarkali*, Bullett. di bibliografia, vol. XVIII, 1885–86, p. 345 (= p. 57 of the offprint). Tables of right ascensions for every minute of the zodiac, from the beginning of Capricorn, were also computed by Abū Bakr ibn al-Musharraf, in the ninth century of the hijra (the fifteenth AD), it seems (*Catal. of the Library of Cairo*, vol. V, p. 238, mīqāt no. 209). And from the same point of Capricorn the Egyptian Muḥammad al-Akhṣāṣī counted the ascensions of 112 fixed stars, for about the year AD 1650, in the small book *al-durra al-muḍīʾa fī l-aʿmāl al-shamsiyya*, of which there are two copies also in the *Cairo Catal.* V, 245, mīqāt nos. 60 and 81; see the tables published by Edw. Knobel, *On a catalogue of stars in the Calendarium of Mohammad Al Achsasi Al Mouakket*, in Monthly Notices of the R. Astronomical Society, vol. LV, no. 8, 1895, p. 429.[^n]
@n Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part II, p. V, nos. 5710, 5758, 5771, 5772, 5798, 5808), some later writers calling them *maṭāliʿ al-zawāl*, «the ascensions of the culmination», or *maṭāliʿ al-qubba*, «the ascensions of the Dome [of the Earth]»; and they refer to Barhebraeus, *Astron.*, p. 170 of the text, 150 of the translation. The Addenda are translated with Part II's front matter.

198, 16–22, and n. 1: That letter is not from Regiomontanus to Bianchini, as Delambre asserts, but from Bianchini to Regiomontanus; it has now been better edited by M. Curtze, *Urkunden zur Geschichte der Mathematik im Mittelalter und der Renaissance*, Leipzig 1902, vol. I, pp. 240–41: «I have seen that some authors give a teaching for finding the declination of stars that have latitude. Their teaching is to take the latitude of the star and the declination of the degree of the ecliptic in which the star appears to be, and, if both are on the same side, namely the northern or the southern, to add them together, but if one is southern and the other northern, to subtract the smaller from the greater; and what results after the addition or the subtraction they call the distance of the star from the equinoctial, of which they say the sine must be taken. Now at first sight this teaching seems false, because the arc of the latitude and the arc of the declination each proceed from their own pole, and they are two different arcs cutting each other at the centre of the star, nor can the sine [*solam sinus*, sic] or the chord alone be taken from them. They also say that this sine must be multiplied by the sine or the chord of the remainder of the greatest declination, and the product divided by the sine of the remainder of the known complement of the declination of the ecliptic; and in this way, they say, the true declination of the star with its latitude is found. Certainly I do not know how to draw this demonstration, nor do I find that Albategni demonstrated it; but in telling the rule he concludes it without more ado. I wonder also at John the Englishman[^82.1], a most skilled and learned man, who in his commentary on the Toledan Tables constructed by Arzachel asserts this without demonstration».

204, n. *a*: That Plato wrote the fraction of a day $\tfrac{1}{130}$, not $\tfrac{1}{131}$ as the editions have, is clear from the agreement of the Vatican, Paris and Venice codices (I did not consult others on this point) and of the book entitled *Almagesti parvum* (on which see the preface, pp. XXVI–XXVII). This book asserts on f. 10r: «Egiptiorum antiquissimi ex babilonia sicut per suas considerationes deprehenderunt ipsum [anni tempus] ex .CCCLXV. diebus et quarta diei et una parte ex .CXXX. diei partibus constare dixerunt» (the most ancient of the Egyptians, from Babylon, as they found by their observations, said that it [the length of the year] consists of 365 days and a quarter of a day and one part of 130 parts of a day). In his Arabic copy Plato seems to have found قل, 130, in place of قك, 120.
