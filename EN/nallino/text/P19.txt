Manuscript codices are not lacking. One is described by W. Schum, *Beschreibendes Verzeichniss der Amplonianischen Handschriften-Sammlung zu Erfurt*, Berlin 1887, p. 587, Quart no. 350: *Geber seu Albategni seu primum Almagesti de numeris et motibus stellarum*[^60.1]. Plato's preface comes first; it breaks off abruptly in chapter LV: «duos gradus per occasum loci celi in quo» (two degrees through the setting of the place of the heaven in which), which are in the Nuremberg ed. f. 85r, l. 24 = my version p. 134, l. 15. — In another codex of the same library (Schum p. 609, Quart no. 364, I, f. 1r–v) there are «*Tabule multiplicationis et divisionis et note ex libro Albategni extracte.* Iste due tabule extracte sunt de libro Albategni filii Gebir..... cuius titulus est de divisione circuli celestis» (tables of multiplication and division, and notes, extracted from the book of Albategni. These two tables are extracted from the book of Albategni son of Gebir..... whose title is on the division of the celestial circle). The end: «gradus exibunt si Deus voluerit» (the degrees will come out, if God will). They are therefore taken from chapter II (on the division of the celestial circle), i.e. f. 4v–5v of the Nuremberg ed., pp. 7–9 of my version[^60.2].

The copy of the Ambrosian Library in Milan was described to me by I. Schiaparelli as follows. It is marked «*H. 109. piano superiore*» and contains several astronomical treatises. It is written by two different hands: the first, as far as the end of ch. XLVI, is clear enough and elegant; the second, to the end of the work, is very hard to read even for those practised in reading medieval documents. The figures are missing, though a blank space is always left for them. Some blank spaces show that the codex from which the Ambrosian copy derives was already defective or very badly written. In the first 56 chapters there are only small discrepancies, of no importance, from the printed editions; but in chapter LVII, the last of the book, there is great diversity. Its title is indeed «*in perfectione operis*» (on the completion of the work), as in the editions; but the opening is entirely different. Then comes an appendix entitled «*De opere isto quod ad caeli similitudinem formatur*» (on the making of this instrument that is formed in the likeness of the heaven), which deals with the construction of a celestial globe and whose first words are: «Sphaeram aeneam ex omni parte tornatam in rotunditatem..... politae superficiei et in tornatorio instrumento ut ita dicam tornatam» (a bronze sphere turned round on every side..... of polished surface and turned, so to speak, on a turning instrument); it breaks off abruptly: «qui per polos transiens duos..... ad duo aequalia..... et sibimet nostra [?] parare non ambigimus. Ex quatuor» (which, passing through the two poles..... into two equal..... and we do not hesitate to prepare ours [?] for itself. From four). After a blank space it goes on thus: «et uniuscuiusque similitudinem imprime. Cumque postmodum omnes stellas posueris, duos clavos ad duos polos» (and impress the likeness of each one. And when you have afterwards set all the stars, two nails at the two poles) etc. — There follows a second appendix, *De compositione lateris in aspiciendo* (on the construction of the brick for observing), which is the description of the quadrant, and in the editions the first part of the chapter. Lastly a third heading, *De compositione alhidadae per quam fiunt observationes* (on the construction of the alidade by which observations are made); but the corresponding exposition, which is in the editions and ends the book, is missing.

What the Ambrosian codex has imperfectly is far better in two other copies, the Marcian[^61.1] and the Vatican[^61.2]. For in both the last chapter is entitled thus: *Capitulum 57.*ᵐ *in libri perfectione et in faciendo instrumentum ad celi similitudinem quod ovum* [M: *omni*] *appellatur nec non in constitutione duorum instrumentorum aspectus per que* [M omittit] *fiunt observationes* (chapter 57, on the completion of the book and on making the instrument in the likeness of the heaven that is called the egg [M: *omni*], and also on setting up the two instruments of sight by which [M omits] observations are made). It begins: «Quoddam ergo [V: igitur] eneum aut lapideum aut ligneum quadratum cuius quadratura.....» (a certain square, then, of bronze or stone or wood, whose squareness.....). It is the description of the mural quadrant, which is also in the editions, and that of the alidade (or triquetrum) follows it; this part ends: «çenit capitum de 90. minueris reliquum erit altitudinis arcus» (when you subtract [the distance from] the zenith of the heads from 90, the remainder will be the arc of the altitude), which are the last words in the printed editions. But both codices then go on thus[^61.3]: «Rerum quidem occasiones originesque radicum numeri geometricalis demonstratorium inmitantes [sic], quas occasiones et origines in hoc libro scripsimus et monstravimus, semper invariabiliter permanebunt, si ullus [V: nullus] unquam in eis scrupulus remanebit. In eo autem quod observationibus et instrumentis deprehenditur, in augmento aut [V: vel] diminutione falsitas reperiri poterit [M: potest]. Illud quod quidem evenit [M: illud etiam quod evenerit] quia rei veritas sciri non potest, cum per longum tempus dimissum fuerit, si quid in illo [?] quod sentiri vel deprehendi possit apparuerit illud erit perparvum; si vero per brevis temporis spacium fuerit dimissum, licet sit paucillum, nil tum tamen videbitur. Ac vero falsitatis illud quod propter instrumenti divisionem seu propter situm aut tortuositatem contingit si per idem instrumentum.....» (the causes of things and the origins of the radices, imitating [sic] the demonstrations of geometrical number, which causes and origins we have written down and shown in this book, will always remain unchanged, if any [V: no] doubt about them ever remains. But in what is found by observations and instruments an error by excess or by defect may be found [M: can be found]. That which happens [M: that also which may happen] because the truth of the matter cannot be known: when it has been left for a long time, if anything in it [?] appears that can be perceived or detected, it will be very small; but if it has been left for a short space of time, though it be very little, nothing will then be seen. And that part of the error which arises from the division of the instrument, or from its position or its crookedness, if by the same instrument.....). These are the general considerations with which, in the Ambrosian codex and in the Arabic text (my version, pp. 138–39), the last chapter rightly begins.

Hunc autem in 4. equa diligenter dividens, quemlibet illorum 4. punctorum centrum constituas, supra quod circulum primo circulo equalem circumducito, qui per 2. spere polos transiens primum circulum secundum rectum angulum in 2. dividat equa. Post hec quemlibet primi circuli quadrantem in 90. partes dividens, ex eis secundum quantitatem declinationis que est 23. g. et 35. sume; et si volueris totum circulum in 360. partes ad circulum directum partire, ita ut sit circulus directus, de quibus cum circino secundum declinationis quantitatem accipiens, alterum circini pedem in altero 2. polorum pone, alterum vero ad secundum circulum vertens, locum circuli quem tetigerit puncto signabis. Similiter ad alterum polum faciens circulum imprime, positionis [?] contrarium vertens, et ita 2. circulos super 2. punctos se se in opposito secantes circumduces, quorum alter equinoctialis, alter vero signorum circulus nuncupatur» (and after we have reached this point in this our book with God's help and have said, as far as we could, whatever was necessary, it seemed fitting to us, in order to put an end to it, that before the construction of the tables is begun we should show the making of the instrument that is formed [M: will be formed] in the likeness of the heaven and is called [M: will be called] the egg [M: *omni*], and also the making of the two instruments represented in the Almagest, one of which we call the brick, the other the alidade; and when these have been plainly shown, we shall, God willing, construct the tables. — *On the making of the instrument that is formed* [M: *informed*] *in the likeness of the heaven and is called the egg* [M: *omni*]. Take a bronze sphere of any size, made truly round on every side, of polished surface, and [M omits] turned, so to speak, on a turner's instrument; on it let two poles be marked opposite each other along a diameter; dividing the space that lies between the two poles into two equal parts you will mark it, and about that point you will describe with the compasses a circle that divides the sphere into two equal parts. Dividing this circle carefully into four equal parts, make any one of those four points a centre, about which draw a circle equal to the first circle, which, passing through the two poles of the sphere, divides the first circle at a right angle into two equal parts. After this, dividing each quadrant of the first circle into 90 parts, take of them according to the amount of the declination, which is 23 d. and 35; and if you wish to divide the whole circle into 360 parts towards the straight circle, so that it is the straight circle, taking of them with the compasses according to the amount of the declination, put one foot of the compasses on one of the two poles and, turning the other to the second circle, you will mark with a point the place of the circle that it touches. In the same way, doing this at the other pole, impress the circle, turning it contrary to the position [?]; and so you will describe two circles that cut each other at two opposite points, of which one is called the equinoctial circle, the other the circle of the signs).
