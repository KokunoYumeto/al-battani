That the father of our astronomer too was among those who moved from Ḥarrān to al-Raqqa is not improbable. From the astronomical work itself it is very clear that al-Battānī spent the greatest part of his life in that city. There he observed the heavens over a very long span of years so as to establish the elements of the tables (p. 6, ll. 2–3), adapted to the meridian and the latitude of that city; for many years after he measured the course of the Sun most diligently (p. 43), determining the solstices and the equinoxes often, but especially in the years 882 and 883 (pp. 42, 44); in 879–880 he fixed the longitudes of certain fixed stars (p. 124); and lastly, in the years 883 and 891, he observed eclipses of the Sun and the Moon (pp. 56, 57). — In the year 901, however, he stayed for no short time at *Antioch*, for there, with astronomical instruments too, he was able to observe an eclipse of the Sun (23 January) and one of the Moon (2 August) (pp. 56, 57).

Where the false report was taken from, which is not rarely spread about by our scholars[^26.1], that al-Battānī was a prince or king of Syria, I do not know; I have never found anything of the kind in the Arabic writers.
@p 0026b014..0026b019
That, wholly intent on his studies, he shrank from vain praise and did not pursue the favours of princes may be inferred from what he prescribes on p. 139 for good observers to do; it appears still more from his very great modesty, of which I shall speak in § 4, no. III. Yet that the learned men of his age already held him in high esteem is shown with certainty by the epistle that Ibn al-Furāt had asked of him, by his conversations with Jaʿfar ibn al-Muktafī, and by his observations, already mentioned by Thābit ibn Qurra (see p. 302).
@p 0026b020..0026b025 + 0027b001..0027b004
About those Banū l-Zayyāt[^26.2] with whom al-Battānī went to Baghdād the authors relate nothing. I think it most probable that they were the family of the very famous Abū Jaʿfar Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Abān al-Zayyāt, often called briefly Ibn al-Zayyāt, who, born of a humble family, but a good poet and a man most eminent in literary studies, was the minister (wazīr) of the caliphs al-Muʿtaṣim (AD 833–842) and al-Wāthiq (842–847), and was put to death by order of al-Mutawakkil on 19 Rabīʿ I 233 H, i.e. 2 Nov. 847[^26.3][^n]. His son Abū Mūsā Hārūn ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Malik al-Zayyāt collected poems and narratives with the greatest zeal[^27.1]; and his great-great-grandson Abū Ṭālib Aḥmad ibn al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAlī ibn Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd al-Malik al-Zayyāt was a friend of the writer Ibn Waḥshiyya, whose books he handed down[^27.2], and a few years younger than al-Battānī.

@p 0027b005..0027b022 + 0028b001..0028b002
Already far advanced in age, when 55 lunar years, about 54 solar years, had passed since the time he began to observe, al-Battānī died at a place called *Qaṣr al-Jiṣṣ* or *Qaṣr al-Jaṣṣ*, that is, «Palace of gypsum»[^27.3]. This was one of the palaces that the caliph al-Muʿtaṣim (AD 833–842) had built in the neighbourhood of the city of Sāmarrāʾ: a little to the west of the Tigris, on the canal called Nahr al-Isḥāqī, and to the north of the palace called al-Maʿshūq (or, as they say today, al-Maʿshūqa), whose ruins von Oppenheim mentions, *Vom Mittelmeer zum Persischen Golf*, Berlin 1899–1900, vol. II, p. 221. In it, according to certain authors, on 8 Shawwāl 372 (26 March 983), ʿAḍud al-Dawla, the ruler of Persia, was killed by his cousin Bakhtiyār ibn Muʿizz al-Dawla[^n]; after that time no mention of the palace, which was probably destroyed, is made by the Arabic writers. — The name *Qaṣr al-Ḥaḍr* in the life written by Ibn Khallikān is nothing but a *taṣḥīf*, as the Arabs say, that is, a corruption in the writing of the source that the famous author had used, in place of Qaṣr al-Jiṣṣ; it arose the more easily because the very ancient town of al-Ḥaḍr[^27.4], though largely destroyed after the coming of Islam, held a notable place in the old histories of the Arabs and was known to everyone, while the other place was, on the contrary, unknown to most. Yet Ibn Khallikān himself admits that he found *Qaṣr al-Jiṣṣ* written in another book[^27.5]; and that this is the true reading is shown, besides the most certain testimony of Ibn al-Nadīm, Ibn al-Qifṭī and Abū l-Fidāʾ, by this very fact, that the word Qaṣr is never found put before the name al-Ḥaḍr, mentioned hundreds of times by the Arabs, and that it is not likely that a man travelling from Baghdād to al-Raqqa, and one far advanced in age, made his way through the ruins of al-Ḥaḍr, which would have been out of the way and, because of the deserts that lie between al-Ḥaḍr and al-Raqqa, extremely difficult.
