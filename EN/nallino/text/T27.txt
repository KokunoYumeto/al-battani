On pp. 138–139: the stations of the planets
@p 0694b007
On these tables see ch. XLVI (vol. I, p. 114) and the spurious appendix D (vol. I, p. 147).
@p 0694b008..0694b013
They differ in the minutes from *Ptolemy's*, XII, 7 (ed. Halma, vol. II, pp. 354–55), because Ptolemy took as the argument of the tables not the *true centre* but the *mean* (τὸ περιοδικὸν μῆκος). But they agree with Theon's, vol. III, pp. 11–15, and the comparison with these made it easy to check the numbers of the codex[^694.2]. It must also be noted that the sum of the stations is always 360°; for, as will be clear from what follows, if 180° − ψ is the corrected anomaly that produces the first station, or the beginning of the retrogression, 180° + ψ will be the corrected anomaly that produces the second station, or the end of the retrogression.
@f 694.2 0694nl005..0694nr005
But in Theon the first station of Jupiter under 42° and 54° of the argument is wrongly 124° *21*′ and 128° *46*′ for 124° *28*′ and 128° *43*′, as al-Battānī rightly has. Moreover, as Schiaparelli points out, the minutes of the stations of Mars under 90° were wrong in Theon and in the Escorial codex (25′ and 35′ for the correct 22′ and 38′).
@p 0694b014..0694b016
I shall set out briefly Ptolemy's doctrine of the stations (στηριγμοί) and the retrogressions (προηγήσεις) of the planets (*Almag.* XII, 1–7, ed. Halma, vol. II, pp. 312–355), so that the method of computing the tables may be known.
@p 0694b017..0694b021
From the centre of the earth let a straight line be drawn to the position of the planet on the circumference of the epicycle, and let it be produced so that it cuts the epicycle. Whenever the ratio of half the straight line contained in the epicycle to the outer part of the line is greater than the ratio of the velocity of the epicycle (on the circumference of the deferent) to the velocity of the planet (on the circumference of the epicycle), the planet is *retrograde*; whenever these ratios are equal, the planet is *stationary*.
@p 0694b022..0694b031
We have seen (p. 238) that the centre of the epicycle moves with uniform motion about a certain point different from the centre of the earth and from the centre of the deferent. Let *m* be the mean elongation of the centre of the epicycle from the longitude of the apogee, that is, the mean centre (p. 240); it is asked how far the epicycle moves with its true motion with respect to the centre of the earth while with its mean motion it passes through a small angle *a*. Let the equations of the centre answering to the amounts *m* + *a* and *m* be computed, and let the difference of the equations be subtracted from the arc *a* or added to it, as the case requires; the arc completed by the centre of the epicycle with its true motion, that is, the *true velocity of the epicycle* at that time, will result. When this is known, let the time *t* answering to the mean motion *a* be found from the tables; it will then be easy to derive the corresponding mean motion of the anomaly (that is, of the planet on the epicycle), to which must be added, or from which must be subtracted, what was subtracted from or added to the arc *a*[^694.3]; the *true velocity of the planet* on the epicycle will result.
@f 694.3 0694nr006
That is, adding what was subtracted before, etc.
@p 0694b032..0694b036
Now one must find how far the planet is from the true apogee of the epicycle at the beginning of the retrogression or at the beginning of the progression. From the centre E of the earth let a straight line cutting the epicycle in two points T and K be drawn in such a way that ½ TK (or LT) is to TE as the velocity of the epicycle to the velocity of the planet; T will then be the point of the epicycle at which the planet is seen stationary and begins to retrogress. Let HL be drawn; it is necessarily perpendicular to TK.
@p 0695b001..0695b005
Because of the constructions made, the ratio of LT to TE is known, and so also that of 2LT (or KT) to TE, and that of KE to TE. — The straight line HD is known, since it is the radius of the epicycle, and so is HE, since it is the distance of the centre of the epicycle from the centre of the earth, to be computed by the rules set out by me elsewhere; so PE and DE become known. But[^695.1] PE × DE = KE × TE; and we have seen that the ratio of KE to TE is known; so KE and TE will also become known singly, and from them KT, its half LT, and LE.

@p 0695b015..0695b016
Now the *true* arc of the epicycle is sought that the planet traverses from the beginning to the middle of the retrogradation.
@fig AB01-PDF0695-F02 AB01-PDF0695-body-F02
@p 0695b017..0695b034
Let E be the centre of the earth, E′ that of the equant and H that of the epicycle; so P is the true apogee of the epicycle, D the true perigee of the epicycle and Q the mean apogee of the epicycle. Let T be the planet at the point of the epicycle at which it begins to retrogress. The arc TD is already known from what precedes; yet the planet does not traverse exactly this arc in half the retrogression, for while the planet approaches the true perigee D on the epicycle, the centre of the epicycle moves farther from the apogee of the deferent. That is, at the middle of the true retrogradation the centre of the epicycle will be at *h* and the true perigee of the epicycle at *d*; so the true arc of retrogradation Td will be known approximately when the angle that the line of the true motion of the epicycle describes in the time of the supposed half retrogradation TD is subtracted from the known angle DET. The mean motion of the epicycle in this space of time is found directly from the tables; let it be *n*. Let the equations of the centre be taken that answer to the mean centre *m*, mentioned above, and to the amount *m* + *n*, and let the equations found be added to their mean centres or subtracted from them, as the case requires; the true centres will result, whose difference will be the arc completed by the epicycle with its true motion in the time of the half retrogradation TD. Let that arc be taken from the known angle DET; there will remain approximately the true arc of retrogradation sought, twice which will be nearly the true arc described by the planet against the order of the signs in the time of the whole retrogression. The solution is indeed approximate, but sufficient.
@p 0695b035..0695b040
Hence the construction of the tables is clear. By the way just described, Ptolemy seeks the first and the second station for the greatest, the mean and the least distance of the centre of the epicycle from the centre of the earth. Let I be the difference between the greatest and the mean distance of the epicycle, II the difference between the distance of the centre of the epicycle at a given point of the deferent and the mean distance, and III the difference between the station for the epicycle in the apogee of the deferent and the station for the epicycle at the mean distance; he then puts, for the given position of the epicycle on the deferent, \[ \text{station} = \text{station for the mean distance} \pm \frac{\mathit{II} \times \mathit{III}}{\mathit{I}}\,. \]
@p 0696b001..0696b008
Proceeding in the same way also for the positions of the epicycle that lie between the mean and the least distance of the centre of the epicycle from the earth, he fixed the stations for every six degrees of the *mean*[^696.1] elongation of the epicycle from the apogee of the deferent. In doing so he supposes that those stations grow or diminish in proportion to the decrease or increase of the geocentric distance of the epicycle with respect to the mean distance; which, though not true, produces an insensible error. — The two columns assigned to each planet in the tables give the true, or corrected, anomalies (αἱ τῆς διευκρινημένης ἀνωμαλίας ἀποχαὶ ἀπὸ τῶν φαινομένων ἀπογείων τῶν ἐπικύκλων) that produce the first and the second station.
