Only one known author besides al-Battānī mentions such a *sidereal year of the Egyptians and the Babylonians*; for Ptolemy himself, whom al-Battānī cites afterwards, does not know it, as I shall say in note *b*.
@p 0293b011..0293b018
The reading of the Arabic text, 365 1/4 + 1/120 (that is, 365ᵈ 6ʰ 12ᵐ), differs from the printed translation of Plato, «ex 365 diebus et quarta ultraque parte ex 131 diei partibus» («of 365 days and a quarter, and beyond that one part of 131 parts of a day»)[^n], which can only mean 365 1/4 + 1/131 (that is, 365ᵈ 6ʰ 10ᵐ 59.6ˢ). So too a glossator (perhaps Regiomontanus) understood it, whose words in the margin of the Nuremberg edition are these: «ex 365 diebus, 15 minut. et 27 secund. et 30 ter. fere» («of 365 days, 15 minutes, 27 seconds and about 30 thirds»), that is, 365ᵈ 6ʰ 11ᵐ[^n]; and the Bologna edition took this marginal gloss into the text in place of Plato's true words. Nor, lastly, should it be passed over in silence that the Vatican codex of Plato has «ex 365 dierum [*sic*] et quarta unaque parte ex 130 diei partibus» («of 365 days and a quarter and one part of 130 parts of a day»), that is, 365ᵈ 6ʰ 11ᵐ 4.6ˢ.

@p 0293b019..0293b026
It is not easy to judge which is the genuine reading. In chapter LII (p. 127) the astrologers (Greek?) who supposed a trepidation of the fixed stars are credited with a (tropical?) year «exceeding 365 1/4 days by a fifth of an hour, or about a fifth», that is, 365 days 6ʰ 12ᵐ, or about this length. It is not impossible that those astrologers are the same as the ancient Egyptians and Babylonians of this chapter; but even if this were certain, we should have no criterion for settling the true reading. For «by a fifth of an hour» agrees exactly with the 12 minutes of the Escorial codex, while «or about a fifth» can agree not badly with Plato's 10ᵐ 59.6ˢ (or rather 11ᵐ 4.6ˢ, as in the Vatican codex).
@p 0293b027..0293b037 + 0294b001..0294b005
The reading of the Arabic codex, however, seems more probable to me. Not only does the comparison I shall make below yield, among the Indians and the Persians, a year of 365 days, 6 hours, 12 minutes or a few seconds *more* (not less); but in a late Arab writer I find a passage that agrees excellently with the Escorial codex. Saḥnūn ibn ʿUthmān al-Maydawī al-Wānsharīshī, who wrote a commentary on the astronomical poem that ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Akhḍarī finished in the month of Dhū l-Qaʿda 939 H (June 1533), says the following: «What [al-Akhḍarī] relates of the non-Arab year[^293.1] [that it comprises 365 1/4 days] is what is commonly set out. It is also said [by others that this year comprises] a part of 120 parts of a day; such is the opinion of the people of Egypt and Babylon (*qawl ahl miṣr wa bābil*), who used for this the conjunction of the sun with some fixed or wandering star until the time when the sun returned to it. This opinion is manifestly false in the highest degree. ‹For the truth is that the time of the year [is counted] from the conjunction of the sun with some fixed point of the sphere until its return to the same point, namely one of the equinoctial or solstitial points.› So relates al-Jādirī»[^294.1]. If the whole passage of al-Battānī, p. 40, ll. 28–36, is compared with this, it will appear either that al-Battānī and the late al-Jādirī drew from the same source, or that al-Battānī himself is al-Jādirī's first source.

@p 0294b006..0294b015
But let us now seek the origin of such a sidereal year. Relying on al-Battānī's authority and following the reading of the Bologna edition, Fréret (*Mémoires de l’Académie des Inscriptions*, old series, vol. XVI, pp. 214–215) ascribes the sidereal year of 365ᵈ 6ʰ 11ᵐ (which differs little from the value accepted today, 365.256374ᵈ = 365ᵈ 6ʰ 9ᵐ 10.75ˢ) to the Egyptians and the Chaldaeans; Montucla (*Histoire des Mathématiques*, 2nd ed., Paris 1799–1802, vol. I, p. 59), Bailly (*Histoire de l’Astronomie ancienne*, Paris 1775, pp. 149, 165–166, 392, 403), Lepsius (*Die Chronologie der Aegypter*, Berlin 1849, vol. I, p. 197) and others, the most recent of them P. Tannery (*Recherches sur l’histoire de l’astronomie ancienne*, Paris 1893, p. 306), to the Chaldaeans only. This sidereal year served well to prove that the Chaldaeans knew the annual precession of the equinoctial points long before Hipparchus[^294.2].

Schiaparelli, *Le sfere omocentriche di Eudosso, di Callippo e di Aristotele*, Milan 1875, pp. 20–23 (= Pubblicazioni del R. Osservatorio di Brera, no. IX), and by Th.-H. @p 0295b001..0295b012
Letronne's whole reasoning, however, depends on an error of computation that Martin did not notice[^295.1]. For 1° (or 3600″) divided by 80 years gives a yearly arc of 45″, not 40″. The daily motion of the sun in Ptolemy is 0° 59′ 8″, or 3548″; divided by 45″, this shows that the arc of 45″ is run through in the 78.844th part of a day. Let the minutes of time that a day contains, that is, 1440ᵐ, be divided by 78.84; there will result 18.264ᵐ, or about 18ᵐ 16ˢ, not 16ᵐ 19ˢ as Letronne has it. Let this space of time be added to Ptolemy's tropical year; one will find 365ᵈ 5ʰ 55ᵐ + 18ᵐ 16ˢ = 365ᵈ 6ʰ 13ᵐ 16ˢ, which differs from the sidereal year mentioned in al-Battānī (whichever reading is accepted as good, the 12ᵐ of the codex or Plato's about 11ᵐ). So I think that all that Letronne and Martin write about the origin of such a year must be rejected. It remains to inquire whether the length of the solar year that al-Battānī and al-Wānsharīshī (after al-Jādirī) ascribe to the Egyptians and the Babylonians (al-Battānī also to the astrologers who followed the theory of trepidation) is mentioned elsewhere too.

@p 0296b013..0296b027
The year of 365ᵈ 6ʰ 12ᵐ seems to have been known to the ancient Persians too. Almost all the Arab writers who discuss the old chronology of the Persians relate that the Persians had a year of only 365 days, but intercalated one month of thirty days every 120th year, so that those 120 years were equal to 120 years of 365 1/4 days, since 120 : 4 = 30; and this until the Arab rule was set up in Persia (about 650); afterwards, however, they never used the intercalation. @p 0297b025..0297b032 + 0298b001..0298b008
If a conjecture may be put forward in such obscure questions, I suspect that al-Battānī's information is true. Those Babylonians (from whom the astrologers of chapter LII probably do not differ) would then be Chaldaeans, contemporaries of Hipparchus or a little later, who could have derived such a sidereal year from observations carefully made over so many centuries. The most recent investigations show well enough that the astronomical and astrological science of the Chaldaeans flourished in the Hellenistic period too and did not neglect the old observations; so that, besides the Greek astronomy handed down to us by Ptolemy, another existed at the same time in Mesopotamia, fragments of which are preserved on clay tablets with cuneiform inscriptions[^297.5]. A Babylonian, indeed, as Schiaparelli points out to me, was that Seleucus who, on the evidence of Pseudo-Plutarch (*De placitis philosophorum*, II, 1), dared after Aristarchus to teach the heliocentric system and the infinity of the world[^298.1]. Much that is read in the Indian astronomers and does not agree with the doctrines of the Almagest, above all observational data, seems to come from Chaldaeo-Hellenistic science. So it would be no wonder if the Chaldaeans had taught that sidereal year at the beginning of the Christian era to the Indians and the Persians, who seem to have given little attention to exact celestial observations[^298.2]. And in fact Kugler, p. 91, shows that a sidereal year of 365.25953ᵈ = 365ᵈ 6ʰ 13ᵐ 43.4ˢ was known to the Babylonians in the fourth and third centuries BC (what he says of al-Battānī on p. 92 should now be corrected).

*The observation of the autumnal equinox.* — Since al-Battānī follows the usage of the Byzantine indictions, putting the beginning of the Roman year on the first day of September, 19 Aylūl 1194 after Dhū l-Qarnayn will be 19 September of the year 882, not of 883. With this agrees 8 Pachōn, if it is referred to the vague years (without intercalation) of the era of Nabonassar[^298.3]. From the epoch of the era of Nabonassar (26 February 748 BC) to the beginning of the year 882, 748 + 881 = 1629 Julian or Roman years elapsed, that is, 1629 vague years + 1629/4 days = 1630 vague years + 42 days. The first day of the month Thoth has therefore gone back 42 days (besides a whole vague year) with respect to 26 February; so 1 Thoth 1631 of Nabonassar = 15 January 882, and 8 Pachōn (which is the 248th day of the Egyptian year) = 19 September 882[^n].
@n By the usual reckoning the era of Nabonassar begins on 26 February 747 BC, and the vague year that begins on 15 January 882 is its 1630th; the dates 15 January and 19 September 882 are right.

Not to the fixed years (of 365 days, with the Julian intercalation) of the Egypto-Coptic era mentioned in chapter XXXII and elsewhere, or of the era of Diocletian or of the Martyrs; both begin on 29 August, so that 8 Pachōn becomes 3 May. That al-Battānī meant the era of Nabonassar is clear from the very fact that in the Arabic text he calls the month *bākhūn*, after the Greek παχών, not after the Arabic *bashans*. Al-Battānī and the other Arabs give the Coptic months in the Greek form (which arose from the Memphitic Coptic) when they use the era of Nabonassar, in the Arabic form (from the Theban Coptic) when they use the eras of Augustus or of Diocletian. Likewise a little before, where he reports Ptolemy's observations of the equinoxes, *atūr* (ἀθύρ) and *fārmūth* (φαρμουθί) for the *hatūr* and *barmūdha* that are read in chapter XXXII (p. 67).

It remains to inquire how the words «4 3/4 hours before the rising of the sun on the 19th day of Aylūl» are to be understood, which, by the astronomical way of reckoning the day (that is, from noon), would mean 19 Sept., 13ʰ 15ᵐ. Had al-Battānī meant the astronomical day here, he would have referred the hour to noon, not to sunrise; it is probable, then, that the author had in mind the Muslim civil day, which begins at sunset. The observation was therefore in the morning hours of the civil 19th day, from whose noon only the astronomical 19th day begins; and since at the time of the equinox days and nights are equal, the time given will be 18 Sept. 882, 13ʰ 15ᵐ mean time of al-Raqqa, in the astronomical usage. — This interpretation becomes quite certain from the interval of 743 vague years, 178 days and about 17ʰ 36ᵐ that al-Battānī states to have elapsed between his observation and Ptolemy's, the difference of longitude between Alexandria and al-Raqqa being put at 2/3 of an hour. From the tables of R. Schram (*Hilfstafeln für Chronologie*, in the Denkschriften d. k. k. Akademie der Wissenschaften zu Wien, mathem. Classe, 1882, vol. XLV) one learns that in the year 882 the autumnal equinox fell on 18 September, 11ʰ 49ᵐ mean time of Greenwich. According to the astronomical observations made by Chesney in 1835, the longitude of al-Raqqa is 39° 3′ 57″ E of Greenwich, 2ʰ 36ᵐ in time; so the hour of al-Battānī's observation corresponds to 13ʰ 15ᵐ − 2ʰ 36ᵐ = 10ʰ 39ᵐ mean time of Greenwich. Al-Battānī therefore observed the equinox 1ʰ 10ᵐ before the true moment of time derived from Schram's tables.

[If we consider the astronomical instruments of that age, al-Battānī's observation must be judged excellent.
