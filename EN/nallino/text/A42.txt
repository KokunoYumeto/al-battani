[The precepts concern the second equation of the computation of the evection. If for a double elongation of 180° the apparent radius of the epicycle (and so also the speed with which the equation of the anomaly grows) increases from 5° 1′ to 7° 40′, by how much will it grow for a double elongation of 2ε°? Plainly by about $\frac{2^\circ\,39'}{5^\circ\,1'}\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon^\circ}{180^\circ}$. In the same proportion the speed of the equation of the anomaly will grow, in the upper segment of the epicycle against the order of the signs, in the lower segment in the order of the signs (from west to east). Since this mean hourly speed is 33′ of the circumference of the epicycle, at the eye of the observer, who stands twelve times as far away as the centre of the epicycle, it will subtend an arc twelve times smaller, or $\frac{33'}{12} = 165''$; of this quantity the part given by the ratio set out before must be taken. So the apparent hourly motion of the anomaly on the epicycle will grow (and the hourly motion of the moon on the ecliptic will decrease when the moon is at apogee, increase when it is at perigee) by the hourly quantity
\[ 165''\,\frac{2^\circ\,39'}{5^\circ\,1'}\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon^\circ}{180^\circ} = \frac{33''}{18}\cdot\frac{159}{301}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^\circ}{1^\circ} = \frac{5247''}{5418}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^\circ}{1^\circ}\,. \]
The coefficient differs so little from unity that it can be taken as about 1; we shall then say that, *because the epicycle comes nearer to the observer when the moon is outside the mean conjunction, its apparent hourly motion in the apogee part of the epicycle is retarded each hour by as many seconds as there are degrees in the elongation* ε°, *and this hourly motion is accelerated by as many in the perigee part of the epicycle.* Here al-Battānī supposes the moon to be near the perigee or the apogee of the epicycle; at the greatest equations of the anomaly the effect is nothing. But plainly the author thought a rough approximation enough.

[The reason why 1/6 + 1/8 of the difference of longitude is added to the *lunar anomaly*. By the precept of p. 93, ll. 38–40, and by its rough proof, which he now gives, al-Battānī wants in some way not to neglect the computation of the evection when the time of the true conjunction is some hours away from that of the mean. Let us take the first of the cases given by al-Battānī, namely that at the time of the mean conjunction the true place of the sun on the ecliptic precedes the true place of the moon; let ε be the difference of longitude in degrees. The moon will approach the sun by one degree almost exactly every two hours; so the true conjunction will occur about 2ε hours after the mean, the mean elongation (which was nothing at the time of the mean conjunction) will be ε at the true conjunction, while the *mean* longitude of the moon will *exceed* the mean longitude of the sun by ε degrees, and the mean lunar elongation will be 2ε degrees towards the east[^n], that is, in the positive direction.

Let AK be the eccentric circle of the moon, O its centre, E the earth, N the centre of the equant, or the point about which the motion of the centre of the epicycle always appears uniform (see p. 237), so that EN = EO. Let the point S indicate the direction of the sun; let the angle AES be the mean elongation, and AEQ its double; Q will be the centre of the epicycle, the point P the origin of the mean anomalies, or the mean apogee, R the true apogee, PR the equation of the anomaly *to be added* to the mean anomaly to give the true one. It must be shown that in places near the conjunction the angle RQP[^n] is 1/6 + 1/8 = 7/24 of the mean elongation QES, or 7/48 of the double elongation QEA. Since the angles at E and N are all very small, we can use a well-known theorem of optics and affirm that the angles QEA, QNA, under which the arc QA is seen from the points E and N, are to each other in the inverse ratio of the distances of these points from the arc; so QEA : QNA = NA : EA, whence (QEA − QNA) : QEA = (NA − EA) : NA, or NQE : QEA = EN : NA. And since, according to Ptolemy, NE = 10ᵖ 19′ if EA is taken = 60ᵖ, and since NQE = RQP = the *share*, or equation of the argument, we shall have
\[ \text{equation of the argument} = \text{double elongation}\ \frac{10^{p}\,19'}{70^{p}\,19'} = \text{elongation}\ \frac{20^{p}\,38'}{70^{p}\,19'}\,. \]
This will be the nearer the truth the smaller the angles considered; but it is always exact enough for the use of this astronomical computation. The ratio $\frac{20^{p}\,38'}{70^{p}\,19'}$ makes 0.2934 in decimals; al-Battānī derives 1/6 + 1/8 = 0.2916 from his rougher computation. The agreement is excellent. — Schiaparelli.]

[«*In this Ptolemy* …». — Al-Battānī now wants to show, rather obscurely, why he considered before the moon in the position on the epicycle to which an equation of 3° answers, whereas at first sight the case might be thought of greater moment in the position answering to an equation of 5°. The author says that, with an equation of about 3° and a correction of the anomaly of 1 1/2°, the error arising from neglecting the new correction reaches its greatest amount; but that with the equation = 5° the correction is nothing or of small moment. That this is true will be plain from what follows. Let C be the centre of the epicycle, E the earth. Let the arcs *ab* and αβ be 7/24 of the elongation, *ab* at the place where the equation CE*a* = 3°, αβ at the place where the equation CEα = 5°. Plainly, if the correction of those 7/24 of the elongation is neglected, the error at the first place is the angle *a*E*b*, much greater than the error made at the second place, where it appears nothing or negligible.
