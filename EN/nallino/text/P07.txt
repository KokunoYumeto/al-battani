**§ 3. Spurious writings.**
@p 0032b026..0032b033 + 0033b001..0033b004
There exist some writings in Latin, never mentioned by the Arabic writers, that have been wrongly attributed to al-Battānī either by the codices themselves or by learned men of our time. It is well known that the Arabic names were corrupted by the Latins of the Middle Ages in an altogether incredible way, so that very different authors are often confused with one another. Thus the physician Ibn al-Baṭrīq became *Albaterich*, *Albaterius*, and from that sometimes *Albatenius*[^32.4]. Nor does the confusion always come from errors of writing alone. The Latin codex no. 16658 of the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, written in the thirteenth century and containing the Toledan Tables of Arzachel (al-Zarqālī), is entitled: «*The teaching of the tables according to* Arzerchel the Spaniard, *who is called* Albaitegni»[^32.5]; and the same tables in a codex of the Vatican Library, Ottob. 309, bear this title: «*Astronomical canons* of Albategni»[^33.1]. Albertus Magnus, conversely, in his *Speculum Astronomicum*, while quite certainly meaning al-Battānī, speaks of «Azarchel the Spaniard, *who is called* Albategni»[^33.2].
@p 0033b005
Besides, the following have sometimes been falsely attributed to al-Battānī:
@p 0033b006..0033b014
I. *A compendium of the works of Archimedes.* In the catalogue entitled *Libri imprimendi in lingua Arabica Romae in typographia Serenissimi Magni Ducis Hetruriae cui praeest Io. Bapt. Raymundus* are mentioned «*the geometrical works of Archimedes reduced to a compendium by* Albettam»[^33.3], a name corrupted from Alheitam = [Ibn] al-Heytham[^33.4]. Yet Baldi (b. 1553, d. 1617), Montucla[^33.5], Wenrich[^33.6], Chwolsohn (vol. I, p. 613) and Steinschneider on Baldi, p. 458 (extr. 31), thought that *Albettani* should be read, and made our astronomer the author of that compendium. Steinschneider himself, however, later[^33.7] called the matter into question, and rightly suspected that the name Ibn al-Haytham lay hidden in that *Albettani* (or rather *Albettam*, as it is in the catalogue).
@p 0033b015..0033b020
II. At Vienna there is a miscellaneous Latin codex in which, among other writings, the *Tabulae codicum manu scriptorum praeter graecos et orientales in Bibliotheca Palatina Vindobonensi asservatorum*, Vienna 1864–75, vol. IV, p. 77, list this: «No. 2528 ..... 12) f. 75a–76b Albategnius, *Treatise on the instruments of observation that are set out in the Almagest.* Begins: *Almagesti abbreviato libro primo.* — Ends: *usque ad medium lunam quae relinquitur*».
@p 0033b021..0033b024
This little book, of only three pages, Steinschneider already mentioned on Baldi (only in extr. p. 31), judging: «Dubito molto, che questo trattato sia d'Albattani» (I much doubt that this treatise is al-Battānī's)[^33.8]. To this opinion I gladly subscribe; I think the little work gives excerpts from the Almagest, or at most from al-Battānī's last chapter.
@p 0033b025..0033b028 + 0034b001..0034b013
III. *A compendium of the Almagest.* — Speaking of Ptolemy, Ibn al-Qifṭī (in Casiri I, 384) counts al-Battānī among those who reduced the Almagest to an epitome; and so it came about that Ḥājjī Khalīfa[^33.9], Wenrich[^33.10] and Chwolsohn I, 613 also attributed to al-Battānī a compendium of the Almagest. But Steinschneider very well observed[^34.1] that by those words Ibn al-Qifṭī was referring only to the astronomical work, which comprises the doctrines of Ptolemy. Plato of Tivoli himself, in the preface to his translation of al-Battānī, says: «and since I found that Albategnius too was a perfect imitator of him [namely of Ptolemy] among the Arabs, one who compresses the prolixity of Ptolemy (*compendiosae* [sic]) and, emending his errors, which (*quae* [sic]) are indeed very rare, imputes them not to him but to the radix of Abrachis [i.e. Hipparchus].....». Similarly Rudolf of Bruges, in the preface with which he furnished Maslama's recension of the Planisphaerium of Ptolemy, translated by him from the Arabic into Latin in 1144: «Albeten very conveniently abridges the *Almagest*»[^34.2], alluding to the astronomical work. So too, lastly, Albertus Magnus, in the passage mentioned above: «What, however, was said at length in it [i.e. in the Almagest] for the sake of diligence is conveniently abridged by Azarchel the Spaniard, who is called Albategni, in his book that begins thus: *Universa*, etc.; and there some things are corrected which he said happened not through Ptolemy's error but from the supposition of the radix of Abrachis, which, however, do not seem to fight against the faith»[^34.3].
