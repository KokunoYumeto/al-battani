II. *Observations on the Arabic witnesses.* — It remains for us to illustrate these Arabic testimonies, sometimes also bringing emendations and additions to them.
@p 0020b023..0020b024 + 0021b001..0021b012
A few remarks on Arabic proper names must be made first[^20.11]. Among the Muslims parents give their children *names*, or *first names (ism)*, of which the number is small; those in more frequent use hardly reach a hundred. Surnames, or family names, are almost entirely lacking; in their place the *father's name*, often also that of the grandfather, the great-grandfather, the great-great-grandfather, etc., is usually given. So in our astronomer's case: Muḥammad (which is the first name), son (*ibn*) of Jābir, son of Sinān. Moreover, what the Arabs call the *kunya*, a name compounded with the word *Abū* «father»[^21.1], is not rarely added for adults; so in our author's case: Abū ʿAbd Allāh «father of ʿAbd Allāh», probably because his first son was named ʿAbd Allāh[^21.2]. Finally, *relative names (nisba)* are also used, derived from the names of regions, cities, tribes, crafts, etc., as gentile, patronymic or possessive names; they are formed with the suffix *-ī*, more rarely *-ānī*, *-āwī*. So in our author's case al-Ḥarrānī indicates a man who came from the city of Ḥarrān, or was born in it, or lived in it; similarly al-Raqqī designates a man connected in any way with the city of al-Raqqa. One man can take several *nisbas*, as happened with our author (al-Ḥarrānī, al-Raqqī, al-Battānī, al-Ṣābiʾ). Sometimes a son also takes his father's nisba, even when it does not well suit him.
@p 0021b013..0021b019
The relative name, or nisba, al-Battānī I do not find explained by the writers of the first five centuries of the hijra. In the sixth century Ibn al-Akfānī, a man most eminent in the knowledge of the sacred traditions (*muḥaddith*), asserts that the name is to be pronounced al-Bittānī, against the common reading[^21.3]; but I do not know whether he also taught where it came from. — In the same century al-Samʿānī[^21.4], in his very famous but still unpublished lexicon of relative names, does not mention al-Battānī, as I am told by the distinguished scholar de Rosen, who at my request most kindly went through the St. Petersburg codex of the lexicon.
@p 0021b020..0021b024
But from the middle of the same sixth century of the hijra there are some who think that the name Battānī is derived from a certain rural district *Battān*, in the region of Ḥarrān. The first is ʿAlī al-Bayhaqī, as we have seen on p. xi. Afterwards Yāqūt, in his great geographical lexicon[^21.5], writes: «Battān, one of the rural districts (نواحي) of Ḥarrān. From it took his name Muḥammad ibn Jābir al-Battānī, author of the astronomical work, whom Ibn al-Akfānī records with the vowel *i* after the *b*[^21.6]».
@p 0021b025..0021b028
Al-Shahrazūrī, Ibn Khallikān and Abū l-Fidāʾ I have already quoted; al-Dhahabī, in his lexicon of the proper names that are easily confused with one another in writing[^21.7], says: «Al-Bittānī[^21.8]. Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān al-Ḥarrānī al-Bittānī al-Ṣābiʾ, author of the astronomical work; he died after [the year] 300. Bittān[^21.9] is one of the villages (قرى) of Ḥarrān».
@p 0021b029..0021b030 + 0022b001..0022b005
Lastly al-Fīrūzābādī, who died on 12 Shawwāl 817 (25 Dec. 1414), has this in his very famous lexicon, the *Qāmūs*, to which I add, within the signs < >, the explanations of the commentary that Murtaḍā al-Zabīdī composed on that lexicon[^22.1]: «B..ttān, with *i* <according to Ibn al-Akfānī> or with *a* <which is the common reading> and a double *t* <in both readings>, a village in Ḥarrān, whence Aḥmad <so in the codices, but rather, according to what is in the *Tabṣīr*[^22.2] and in the *Muʿjam*[^22.3], Muḥammad> ibn Jābir <ibn Sinān al-Ḥarrānī> al-Battānī <al-Ṣābiʾ>, the astronomer <author of the astronomical work; he died after [the year] 800>[^22.4]».
@p 0022b006..0022b017
But I have questioned the historical and geographical writers of the Arabs in vain about the village or rural district of Battān (Bittān), apart from Yāqūt, just quoted; in vain I have turned over maps and books written by the travellers of our time. The very few things that Yāqūt and the biographers relate about Battān are such as to give room for a strong suspicion that they derived the name of the village, otherwise wholly unknown to them, from the nisba al-Battānī itself; which plainly happened more than once in Yāqūt's lexicon. And because there is no disagreement about al-Battānī's origin from Ḥarrān, they could, by an easy conjecture, place the invented village in the neighbourhood of that very famous city. — Besides, nothing prevents us from thinking that Battān was rather a street or a quarter of the city of Ḥarrān, or that the name al-Battānī was taken from the dialect of Ḥarrān. Nor need the nisba Battānī be derived from *Battān*, since it can also be formed from other names, such as *Batt*, *Battā*, *Battāʾ*. I think it wiser to confess our ignorance than to indulge in vain conjectures; it is enough for us that it is quite certain that al-Battānī and his family came from the *region of Ḥarrān*.
@p 0022b018..0022b030 + 0023b001..0023b003
Chwolsohn I, 611 put forward another opinion: «According to al-Nadīm and [Ibn] al-Qifṭī he derived his origin from Ḥarrān; but, by the plain testimony of Ibn Khallikān, he lived in al-Raqqa. We, on the contrary, think that he was born in Baltān (Batne), lived in al-Raqqa, and was called al-Ḥarrānī only because he was a Sabian; for they [i.e. the Sabians] almost all had that name». Whether our author was a Sabian we shall see below; the rest I consider to rest on error. For Chwolsohn I, 480 thinks that *Baltān* is the *Bathnae* or *Batnae* (Βάτναι) of the ancients, a city of Osrhoene, lying about halfway between Edessa (today Ūrfa) and Birejik بيره‌جك (on the Euphrates)[^22.5]; founded by the Macedonians, taken by Trajan in AD 116. It was very famous among the Syrians under the name {img:AB01-PDF0022-G01} *Baṭnān*[^22.6], called *Sarūj* by the Arabs, today *Eskī Sarūj*. In Isidore of Charax, *Mansiones Parthicae*, ch. III[^22.7], the fort Κοραία ἥ ἐν Βατάνῃ is mentioned, where Βατάνη, the name of the town, seems to have been transferred to its territory[^22.8]; one of the codices has Βιτάνῃ. This was the reason why Chwolsohn took Battān to be Batnae. But on how slippery a foundation such an opinion rests appears at once from this, that the genuine form of the name, used by the Syrians, was *Baṭnān*, from which the Arabic *Battān* could in no way arise; for the Syriac *ṭ* ܛ was never turned by the Arabs into *t* ت, whether Syriac words were taken into Arabic through common speech or through the writings of learned men[^23.1].
