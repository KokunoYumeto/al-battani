@c 0289b005
**a**) — p. 38.
@p 0289b006..0289b007
To compute *the distance between two stars*, on the great circle that passes through both.
@p 0289b008..0289b012
The author puts a geometrical proof before his rules, which are set out on pp. 39–40, as he had already done in chapter XII; and to the proof he also adds an example in numbers, as he does later in chapter XXX, pp. 59–61. Already in note *b* on chapter XII (p. 186) I remarked that it is a probable conjecture that al-Battānī was the first to establish these formulas, and for that reason thought it necessary to prove their truth geometrically.
@p 0289b013..0289b019
Regiomontanus strongly disapproves of the author's construction; «verum esset», he says, «si arcus GE esset circuli magni, et ipse primo bene inventus. Sed dico tibi ille processus intricatus est, et modicae reputationis. Utitur enim lineis curvis tanquam rectis, quod tametsi Ptolemaeum fecisse constet, ipse tamen arcus breves loco linearum rectarum accepit, noster vero indifferenter quantoscumque» («it would be true if the arc GE were of a great circle, and had itself first been rightly found. But I tell you that this procedure is intricate and of little worth. For he uses curved lines as if they were straight; and though it is certain that Ptolemy did this, Ptolemy took short arcs in place of straight lines, while our author takes arcs of any size indifferently»). He then proves that the quadrilateral of the four chords AB, BG, GE, EA is symmetric, and can therefore be inscribed in a circle; lastly he proves that chord $GE = R\,\frac{\text{chord of the arc } AB}{\sin(\text{arc } FG)}$, where GE is an arc of a smaller circle.
@p 0289b020..0289b025
Delambre, pp. 33–34, judges no less harshly that the author's solution is «décidément mauvaise, sans aucune ambiguité» («decidedly bad, without any ambiguity»); he solves the numerical example with logarithmic tables and finds cos AG = cos 64° 20′ 27″; «Albategni la trouve de 54° 19′, trop faible de 10° 1′ 27″» («Albategni finds it to be 54° 19′, too small by 10° 1′ 27″»); and he concludes: «Albategni, en terminant ce chapitre, dit qu’il sert, comme le précédent, pour les nativités..... Pour la gloire d’Albategni, il faut croire ces deux derniers chapitres interpolés par quelque astrologue ignorant et charlatan» («Albategni, in ending this chapter, says that it serves, like the preceding one, for nativities..... For Albategni's glory we must believe these two last chapters to have been interpolated by some ignorant astrologer and charlatan»).
@p 0289b026..0289b032
But had Delambre considered the numbers of al-Battānī's computation attentively, he would have found that 54° 19′, which Plato has, is a slip of the pen or of the typesetter for 64° 19′; so, neglecting the seconds, which the author leaves out, the error is only one minute, and it plainly comes from the difference in interpolating the tables of sines. Likewise, if the rules of pp. 39–40 are written in the present trigonometric manner, one finds the fundamental rules by which we solve spherical triangles. It is impossible, then, that the geometrical constructions from which a true computation and sure rules are derived should be false.
@p 0289b033..0289b038 + 0290b001 + 0290b002..0290b013
From the numerical example it is very clear that by the name «line *x*» the author denotes not the arc *x* or the chord of the arc *x*, but the straight line that lies under the arc *x*, that is, the sine of that arc. The same expression occurs several times in chapter XXVIII. Nor should it be forgotten that the figure of p. 38, which Plato's translation shows with curved lines, is drawn with straight lines in the Escorial codex, as I have had it printed. Hence it becomes certain that al-Battānī had in mind the orthographic projection of the sphere, which he had already used elsewhere (as I showed in the notes on chapters XI, XII and XVI), and derived the rules from it. The ambiguities would easily be avoided by drawing al-Battānī's figure a little differently, so that the point M of the chord appears at once distinct from M of the arc, and C of the chord from C of the arc; Schiaparelli proposed to me the diagram printed here. Let the sphere be projected orthogonally onto the plane of the ecliptic; AHDB will be the ecliptic, whose pole is F; FG, FE, FB, FA will at one and the same time be the complements of the latitude and the sines of these complements. Through the two points G, E, of the same latitude, let a *great circle*[^290.1] DH be described, whose arc GE will be the distance between G and E; let the straight lines GE, BA be drawn, bisected by the line FC; lastly let the arc AG of a *great circle* and its chord be marked. Let the midpoints of the chords be distinguished by the letters M and C, the midpoints of the arcs by the letters M′ and C′.

@f 290.1 0290nl001..0290nl003
Not a smaller circle, that is, a circle parallel to the great circle BA, as Regiomontanus and Delambre interpreted it.
@p 0290b014..0290b019
This done, the proportion GM : BC = FG : FB[^290.2] is manifest in the plane triangle FBC; that is, if L and L′ are called the longitudes of the stars and β the latitude,
\[ \sin\frac{GE}{2} : \sin\frac{L - L'}{2} = \sin(90^\circ - \beta) : \sin 90^\circ. \]
But $\sin 90^\circ = R$, and the arc GE/2 is half the distance sought; therefore
\[ \sin\frac{GE}{2} = \sin\frac{L - L'}{2} \times \frac{\cos\beta}{R}, \]
which is al-Battānī's rule, stated on p. 40, ll. 1–2 (and 39, ll. 26–28).
@f 290.2 0290nl004..0290nr006
The codex and Plato add «= FM : FC», which, though true, appears entirely useless, for the knowledge of these lines is neither required nor taught. It must therefore be regarded as an interpolation, which arose because the writer of the archetype from which the Escorial codex and Plato's codex derive wrongly put, in lines 23, 24, 30 and 31, FM ف م for FG ف ط, and FC ف ج for FB ف ب. The corruption pointed out on p. 38, note 6, also comes from this.
@p 0290b020..0290b023
There are also some corruptions or interpolations in the text, which I have pointed out in their places in my translation; they cannot be ascribed to al-Battānī himself, since they are most foolish, wholly superfluous and of no consequence in the author's numerical computation or in the rules of pp. 39–40. The hand of an unskilled reader appears, whose traces we find plainly elsewhere too.
@p 0290b024..0290b027
According to the end of the chapter, these rules are most useful for computing the profections in nativities; but that they serve non-astrological questions too appears from al-Battānī's own words where he teaches how to compute the distance of the moon from the sun in a certain and absolute way (ch. XLI, p. 87, ll. 11–13).
@c 0290b028
**b**) — p. 39, ll. 1–12.
@p 0290b029..0290b034 + 0291b001..0291b003
Once the distance of the point G from E on a great circle is known by the formula
\[ \sin\tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{distance} = \sin\frac{L - L'}{2} \times \frac{\cos\beta}{R}, \]
the author seeks the distance of the point G from A, also on a great circle. So he observes that in the *rectilinear* quadrilateral GEBA (which is symmetric, and so can be inscribed in a circle) Ptolemy's theorem, stated at the beginning of the chapter (p. 37, ll. 38–40), holds; so that one has
\[ \overline{GA}^2 = \overline{GB}^2 + GE \times BA, \]
where the sides GB, BA are chords of known arcs, and GE has just been determined[^n]. So the diagonal GA becomes known, which is the chord of the arc GA of a *great* circle, that is, of the distance sought. The solution can rightly be called elegant.

@p 0291b005..0291b006
He now states the general rules for computing the *distances between two stars* (in arcs of great circles).
@p 0291b007..0291b010
In spherical quadrilaterals, of the two sides whose chords are parallel he calls the one nearer the ecliptic (or the ecliptic itself) *the first side*, the one nearer the pole *the second side*. The other two sides, which are equal and whose chords are not parallel, he calls *the third* and *the fourth side*.
@p 0291b011
He distinguishes four cases:
@p 0291b012..0291b027
I. — When one of the stars is on the ecliptic and the other has the latitude β. — The solution is the one I have already set out in note *b*, after al-Battānī himself; he puts $L - L'$ (that is, the difference of the longitudes) = the first side, $2\,\text{arc}\bigl[\sin\bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\bigr) \times \frac{\cos\beta}{R}\bigr]$ = the second side (that is, the arc GE, on which see note *a*), and lastly the chord of β = the third or the fourth side. So he states the rule:
\[ [[(α)]] \text{crd distance} = \sqrt{\text{crd first side} \times \text{crd second side} + \text{square of the third side}}. \]
From this formula of al-Battānī it is not difficult to derive our cos dist. = cos β cos (L − L′). Since chord $x = 2\sin\frac{x}{2}$, if we put sines for chords and square both members of equation (α), there results[^n]
\[ 4\sin^2\frac{\text{distance}}{2} = 2\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\,2\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta}{R} + 4\sin^2\frac{\beta}{2} = 4\sin^2\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta}{R} + 4\sin^2\frac{\beta}{2}. \]
But $\sin^2\frac{x}{2} = \frac{R^2 - R\cos x}{2}$; so, dividing everything by 4,
\[ \frac{R^2 - R\cos(\text{dist.})}{2} = \frac{R^2 - R\cos(L - L')}{2} \times \frac{\cos\beta}{R} + \frac{R^2 - R\cos\beta}{2}, \]
whence, multiplying by 2 and dividing by *R*,
\[ R - \cos(\text{dist.}) = \cos\beta - \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos(L - L')}{R} + R - \cos\beta, \]
and lastly $\cos(\text{distance}) = \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos(L - L')}{R}$.
@n The print has cos β / 2R three times in these formulas; Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part I, p. LXXIII) correct it to cos β / R.
@p 0291b028..0291b032
II. — When the stars have the same latitude. — The chord of the distance is then the second side itself; that is, as he taught before and as is derived from the geometrical construction of p. 38 (on which see note *a*):
\[ [[(β)]] \text{chord of the distance} = 2\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta}{R}, \]
or $\sin\frac{\text{dist.}}{2} = \sin\bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\bigr)\frac{\cos\beta}{R}$.
@p 0291b033..0291b034 + 0292b001..0292b026
III. — When the latitudes β and β′ of the stars are different, but on the same side of the ecliptic. Al-Battānī then sets
\[ \text{first side} = L - L', \qquad \text{second side} = \text{double arc}\Bigl[\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos(\beta - \beta')}{R}\Bigr], \]
\[ \text{third side} = \text{fourth side} = \beta - \beta', \]
\[ \text{first side of the longitude} = \text{double arc}\Bigl[\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta}{R}\Bigr], \]
\[ \text{second side of the longitude} = \text{double arc}\Bigl[\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta'}{R}\Bigr]. \]
Al-Battānī's rule is:
\[ [[(γ)]] \text{chord of the distance} = \sqrt{\text{crd I side of long.} \times \text{crd II side of long.} + \text{crd}^2\ \text{III side}}, \]
which is plainly proved geometrically in the same way as rule (α) (see note *b*). The fundamental formula of our spherical trigonometry is contained in it implicitly. For by squaring both members of the equation and bringing in sines one finds
\[ 4\sin^2\Bigl(\frac{\text{distance}}{2}\Bigr) = 2\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta}{R} \times 2\sin\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta'}{R} + 4\sin^2\Bigl(\frac{\beta - \beta'}{2}\Bigr). \]
Dividing everything by 4, and considering that $\sin^2\frac{x}{2} = \frac{R^2 - R\cos x}{2}$,
\[ \frac{R^2 - R\cos(\text{distance})}{2} = \sin^2\Bigl(\frac{L - L'}{2}\Bigr)\frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta'}{R^2} + \frac{R^2 - R\cos(\beta - \beta')}{2} = \]
\[ = \frac{R^2 - R\cos(L - L')}{2} \times \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta'}{R^2} + \frac{R^2 - R\cos(\beta - \beta')}{2}. \]
Let everything be multiplied by 2 and divided by *R*; there will result
\[ R - \cos(\text{distance}) = \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta'}{R} - \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta'\,\cos(L - L')}{R^2} + R - \cos(\beta - \beta'). \]
But $\cos(\beta - \beta') = \frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\beta' + \cos\beta\,\cos\beta'}{R}$; so, taking away the equal terms of opposite sign,
\[ \cos(\text{distance}) = \frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\beta'}{R} + \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta'\,\cos(L - L')}{R^2}, \]
which we, taking $R = 1$, now write
\[ \cos(\text{dist.}) = \sin\beta\,\sin\beta' + \cos\beta\,\cos\beta'\,\cos(L - L'). \]
If for β and β′ their complements Δ and Δ′ are taken, that is, the distances from the pole of the ecliptic, the formula becomes
\[ \cos(\text{dist.}) = \cos\Delta\,\cos\Delta' + \sin\Delta\,\sin\Delta'\,\cos(L - L'), \]
which is derived at once from the consideration of the spherical triangle FDE, in which the angle DFE = L − L′, and which is always used today in this problem.
@p 0292b027..0292b030
IV. — When the latitudes β and β′ are different and on two different sides. It differs from case III only in that β + β′ must be taken for the β − β′ of the third side. Then $\cos(\beta + \beta') = \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta' - \sin\beta\,\sin\beta'}{R}$, so that the formula becomes
\[ \cos(\text{dist.}) = -\frac{\sin\beta\,\sin\beta'}{R} + \frac{\cos\beta\,\cos\beta'\,\cos(L - L')}{R^2}. \]
@p 0292b031..0292b034 + 0293b001..0293b005
Al-Battānī's solution is most elegant, and all the more worth mentioning because, after S. Günther, *Studien zur Geschichte der mathematischen und physikalischen Geographie*, Halle 1877–79, part V, pp. 306–308, Georgius Amirucius is said to have been the first to solve this problem without dividing the figure into two right-angled spherical triangles[^292.1]. Amirucius considers the geographical distance of two places on the earth; he uses chords, but follows a way different from al-Battānī's. Our author's method, however, so much older, seems to me also easier and more elegant than that of Amirucius. So this chapter XXVI, which Regiomontanus and Delambre attacked so bitterly, appears to be one of the chief parts of the work, once some mistakes of the copyists are cleared away and certain perhaps more obscure expressions are rightly understood.
