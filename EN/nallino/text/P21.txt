@p 0066b001..0066b012
For this reason M. Steinschneider[^66.1], with whom Suter[^66.2] agrees, thinks that the version of the *commentary* of Aḥmad ibn Yūsuf ibn al-Dāya on the *Centiloquium* (Καρπός) *of Pseudo-Ptolemy*[^66.3] must also be attributed to Plato of Tivoli, because at its end there is this: «Perfecta est huius libri translatio 17. die mensis martij. 12. die mensis gumedi secundi: anno arabum 530» (the translation of this book was completed on the 17th day of the month of March, the 12th day of the month of Jumādā II, in the year of the Arabs 530), i.e. 12 Jumādā II 530, or 17 March 1136 by the astronomical usage. For Steinschneider asserts that John of Seville, *to whom the codices attribute that version*, never used the Arabic chronology. But he is badly mistaken; for, to give an example, the version of Alfraganus, which we know with full certainty to have been made by John, is said in some codices[^66.4] to have been finished «XXIV die quinti mensis lunaris anno Arabum DXXIX existente XI die mensis Martii ere MCLXXIII» (on the 24th day of the fifth lunar month, in the year of the Arabs 529, it being the 11th day of the month of March of the era 1173); and in fact, by the astronomical usage of the Muslims, 24 Jumādā I 529 H is 11 March 1173 of the Spanish era, or 11 March AD 1135. Let this version of the Commentary on the Centiloquium, then, be restored to John of Seville, the friend of Plato of Tivoli.
@p 0066b013..0066b028
*c) The Spanish version.* We know that it existed in two codices. One, lost today, belonged in the seventeenth century to the library of D. Juan Cortés; it was described by Nicolás Antonio[^66.5], whose words Rico y Sinobas reports[^66.6]. The other is kept in the Bibliothèque de l'Arsenal in Paris[^66.7]. It begins: «*Aquì se comienza el libro de los canones de Albateni que mandò escrevir el muy noble Rey Don Alfonso, à quien Dios dé vida e salud por mucho tiempo*» (here begins the book of the canons of Albateni that the very noble King Don Alfonso, to whom may God give life and health for a long time, ordered to be written). In the Paris codex (f. 1–3) there follows a list of the chapters, of which Nicolás Antonio makes no mention. Then in both: «Capítulo I del prólogo de este libro. Dixo Mohamad [P: Mahomat] fijo de Geber Albateni [P: Albatheni], que la primera cosa en que debe home comenzar en cada libro, es de loar à Dios e alabarlo, que dió entendimiento all home con que se pueda [P: puede] allegar à estas sciencias preciosas. E [P: Et] yo Mahomad [P: Mahomat] el sobredicho, despues que estudié [P: studié] mucho en esta sciencia, *que es la sciencia* [P omittit] de la astrología, y aturé de pensar en ella.....» (chapter I, of the prologue of this book. Mohamad son of Geber Albateni said that the first thing with which a man ought to begin in every book is to praise God and extol him, who gave man the understanding by which he can attain these precious sciences. And I, the said Mahomad, after I had studied much in this science, *which is the science* [P omits] of astrology, and persevered in thinking about it.....). Many things of the Arabic text and of Plato's, then, which concern the art of rhetoric rather than astronomy, are left out. The book ends like Plato's version and the Arabic text: «Et otrosí si ovieres mester de tommar con ellas su altura de la luna o de qual estrella quier en que mengües el cerco del arredramiento que es del zonte de la cabeza de XC. e lo que fincare serà ell arco de la altura» (and likewise, if you need to take with them the altitude of the Moon or of any star you will, subtract the arc of the distance from the zenith of the head from 90, and what remains will be the arc of the altitude)[^66.8].
@p 0066b029..0066b031 + 0067b001..0067b002
Rico y Sinobas holds that King Alfonso's name has been falsely attached to this version, for the reason that both codices contain other writings that are wrongly presented as translated by Alfonso's order[^66.9]. Then, on a most slippery foundation, he asserts that the work can belong only to the fifteenth century[^67.1], forgetting that the Paris codex is, on Ochoa's testimony, of the fourteenth century. Both are illustrated with figures.
