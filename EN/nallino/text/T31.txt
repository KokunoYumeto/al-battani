@p 0739b022..0739b036 + 0740b001..0740b006
The names *Head of Apollo* and *Head of Hercules*, by which stars nos. 38 and 39 are designated[^739.7], occur neither in the other catalogue, nor in the *Almagest*, nor in the Arab astronomers edited so far; yet they were not unknown to the Arabs, for Ptolemy uses them in the *Tetrabiblos*, I, 9 (= I, 7 of the Frankfurt version): τῶν δὲ ἐν ταῖς κεφαλαῖς δύο λαμπρῶν ὁ μὲν ἐν τῇ προηγουμένῃ τῷ τοῦ Ἑρμοῦ, καλεῖται δὲ καὶ Ἀπόλλωνος· ὁ δὲ ἐν τῇ ἑπομένῃ τῷ τοῦ Ἄρεως, καλεῖται δὲ καὶ Ἡρακλέους, «of the two bright ones in the heads, the one in the preceding head [has an effect like] that of Mercury and is also called [the head] of Apollo; the one in the following head [an effect like] that of Mars, and is also called [the head] of Hercules». The *Arabic* version of the Tetrabiblos, ff. 203r–202v: واما الكوكبان النيران اللذان في الراسين فان قوة احدهما وهو المتقدم مثل قوة كوكب عطارد ويقال لهذا الكوكب افالن واما الكوكب المتاخر منهما فمزاجه شبيه بمزاج المريخ ويقال لهذا الكوكب تلاوس (read ايرقلاوس); which is rendered thus in the Latin version from the *Spanish* (ed. 1519, f. 12v): «and of the two great stars that are in the two heads, the power of the preceding one of them is such as that of mercury, and these stars are called [read: this star is called] in Greek *affalon* [*afālon*]: this is the foot of the twin[^739.8]. And the other star[^739.9] of these two has a temperament like that of mars and is called in Greek *hercides* [read hercules]: this is the hand of the twin[^739.10]». Plato, translating this passage of the Tetrabiblos, writes *auellar* (or rather *anellar*, reading انالر for افالن) and *abracaleus* (ابرقلاوس for ايرقلاوس, *īraqlāwus*). — The Arabs also knew these names from the writings of Teucer translated into Arabic; but in the Greek fragments of Teucer in Fr. Boll, *Sphaera, neue griechische Texte und Untersuchungen zur Geschichte der Sternbilder*, Leipzig 1903, pp. 17, 43–44, 128, they are so interchanged that Apollo designates the following Twin and Hercules the preceding one. They belong to the astrological tradition, not to the true astronomical one; they are already in Varro (see Boll, pp. 122–26, 128).

@p 0740b014..0740b018
How these amounts are computed al-Battānī taught in vol. I in their places. Formulas more convenient for *logarithmic* computation are set out for the first four amounts in all books on spherical astronomy. Let λ be the longitude of the star, β its latitude, α its right ascension, *m* the ascensional difference, φ the latitude of the given city and ε the obliquity of the ecliptic; then, an auxiliary amount N being taken such that $\tan N = \dfrac{\tan\beta}{\sin\lambda}$, we shall have:
@m 0740b019..0740b024
\tan\alpha = \dfrac{\tan\lambda\cos(N + \varepsilon)}{\cos N}
\text{(col. I)} \qquad \tan\delta = \sin\alpha\tan(N + \varepsilon)
\text{(col. II)} \qquad h = \text{complem. } \varphi \pm \delta = 54^\circ \pm \delta
\sin m = \tan\delta\tan\varphi
\text{(col. III)} \qquad P = 90^\circ \pm m
\text{(col. IV)} \qquad \tan A = \dfrac{\tan\alpha}{\cos\varepsilon}\,.

@p 0742b005..0742b011
Of the six amounts that this table gives, two (III and IV) can be taken as fundamental, for from them, combined with the terrestrial latitude 36° and the obliquity of the ecliptic 23° 35′, all the others are derived. III alone suffices to determine I and II, by which it is itself determined; V and VI are computed by means of III and IV. So, having made by different methods and on different hypotheses the computations by which those amounts are derived from one another, I could mostly recognize and heal the faulty numbers. Very few stars are free of error; some are vitiated in three or four places. When the labour was finished I found the following:

*b*) The amounts I, II, III mostly fit one another, at least very nearly. Sometimes, however (about once in five stars), I and II agree with each other but differ from III by some minutes; nor can the error be ascribed to the amount III (the semidiurnal arc), since this, though differing from I and II, always fits the amounts V and VI very well.
