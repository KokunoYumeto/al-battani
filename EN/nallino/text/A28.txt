@p 0302b009..0302b019
*Anomaly*, ἀνωμαλία (*ikhtilāf al-ḥaraka*, «variation, inequality of motion»), properly denoted the difference between the true longitude and the mean longitude of the sun or of a planet. Geometrically, on the hypothesis of the eccentric, it was the angle contained by the two lines drawn from the centre of the sun to the centre of the ecliptic, or of the world, and to the centre of the eccentric; on the hypothesis of the epicycle, the angle made by the two lines drawn from the centre of the ecliptic to the centre of the epicycle and to the centre of the sun. Very often, however, al-Battānī (as presently in what follows) and the other Arabs call this angle or this difference *taʿdīl*, «equation»[^302.1], whence the present astronomical name equation of the centre. By the name anomaly[^302.2], on the other hand, they denote, as the Greeks often did and the moderns always do, what would more rightly be called the *argument of the anomaly* or the *anomalistic motion*, that is, the arc of the eccentric or of the epicycle that the sun or the planet has run from the apogee to the given moment of time[^302.3].

@p 0304b007
II. — *The method of computing the longitude of the apogee of the sun.*
@p 0304b008..0304b020
As he himself admits on p. 43, l. 11, al-Battānī follows the method of Ptolemy, which depends on knowing the equinoxes and the summer solstice, and also the length of the tropical year, from which the mean daily motion of the sun is derived. Let the mean motion of the sun in the spring of a given year be *v*, in the following summer *a*; the eccentricity of the orbit of the sun, or the greatest anomaly, is $FE = \sqrt{FS^2 + ES^2}$, where from al-Battānī's proof, with the radius of the eccentric = 60ᵖ = *R*, it is clear that
\[ FS = \sin\Bigl[v - \Bigl(90^\circ + \frac{(v + a) - 180^\circ}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr] = \sin\frac{v - a}{2}, \]
\[ ES = \sin\frac{(v + a) - 180^\circ}{2} = -\cos\frac{v + a}{2}. \]
We can therefore write:
\[ \text{eccentricity} = FE = \sqrt{\sin^2\frac{v - a}{2} + \cos^2\frac{v + a}{2}}. \]
The longitude of the apogee T is the arc AH, whose complement is the arc HB, or the angle FES. But plainly
\[ \sin(90^\circ - \text{long. of the apogee}) = \sin FES = R\,\frac{ES}{FE}, \]
where the quantities ES, FE have just been computed.
@p 0304b021..0304b033
This method of Ptolemy is also set out at length by al-Bīrūnī, *Chron.*, pp. 183–85 of the text, 164–67 of the translation; afterwards he adds: «But when the *moderns*[^304.2] had recognized that it is very difficult and almost impossible to determine the times of the solstices exactly, they preferred in their observations the points that mark the middles of the four seasons of the year, that is, the middles of the fixed signs [that is, 15° of Taurus, 15° of Leo, 15° of Scorpio, 15° of Aquarius]. The method, however, that my master Abū Naṣr Manṣūr ibn ʿIrāq[^304.3], a client of the caliph, found for solving this problem rests on knowing any three points of the ecliptic and on knowing the length of the solar year exactly. In my book entitled *The request for testimony on the discrepancies in observations* I have shown that this method is as much better than the method of the modern astronomers as theirs is better than the method of the ancients»[^304.4]. And in fact in this way points of the ecliptic can be chosen whose determination is easier than that of the solstices; it seems to be the same solution that Delambre proposed, *Hist. de l’astron. ancienne*, vol. II, pp. 121–133. — Of the difficulty of measuring the time of the solstice I have already spoken on p. 212[^n].

@p 0305b001
III. — *The motion of the apogee of the sun.*
@p 0305b002..0305b005
Delambre, p. 36, rightly denies that al-Battānī knew the *proper* motion of the solar apogee, as Bailly and many others wrongly say; in fact, at the end of chapters XXXIII and XLIV, he gives the apogee of the sun only the motion of the other apogees and of the fixed stars, that is, the motion of precession.
@p 0305b006..0305b014
Delambre, however, is wrong to wonder that al-Battānī did not compare his solar apogee with that of Hipparchus and Ptolemy (who have 65° 30′), and so derive a peculiar annual motion of the apogee of 25 seconds (by comparison with Ptolemy's apogee) or of 21 (with Hipparchus's). Al-Battānī understood very well that the Greek longitude of the solar apogee was of no use; for Ptolemy[^305.1], though he gives the motion of precession to the fixed stars and to the apogees of the planets, thinks the apogee of the sun immovable, affirming that in the year 463 after the death of Alexander (139) he found it at the same longitude, 65° 30′, at which Hipparchus had found it about 265 years before! Theon himself, lastly (*Tables manuelles*, ed. Halma, vol. I, p. 54), in the fourth century, always puts the apogee of the sun at 65° 30′[^n].
@n Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part I, p. LXXIV) add here that Theon of Smyrna, in the first half of the second century, gives an anomalistic year of 365 1/2 days, so that the solar apogee would run round the whole zodiac in 1461 years; the Addenda are translated with Part I's front matter.
@f 305.1 0305nl001..0305nl008
*Almag.* III, 4 (ed. Halma vol. I, p. 184); likewise in the *Hypotheses*, ed. Halma, Paris 1820, pp. 44–45. — Proclus, *Hypotyposes*, ed. But Proclus (5th century) also denies the precession of the equinoxes.
@p 0305b015..0305b034
How, then, could al-Battānī derive from the statements of the Greeks the small proper motion of the solar apogee, which is 11.46″ a year and in the interval of 745 years from Ptolemy to al-Battānī reaches only 2° 22′ 18″? It is already of the greatest consequence that the Arabs declared false the opinion of the Greeks on the immobility of the solar apogee, and recognized that it shares in the motion of the precession of the equinoxes, by which the longitudes of the fixed stars increase. Al-Masʿūdī writes, *al-Tanbīh*, p. 222: «The astrologers and astronomers of our time do not disagree with the sages of the Indians, the Chaldaeans, the Egyptians, the Greeks, the Romans and others in putting the apogee of the sun in the sign of Gemini; the disagreement among them concerns only its immobility and its motion, as we have said. Thābit ibn Qurra al-Ṣābiʾ al-Ḥarrānī[^305.2] wrote an epistle to defend Hipparchus's opinion that the apogee of the sun moves, against what Ptolemy had said»[^305.3]. — Caussin, p. 234, relates the following after Ibn Yūnus: «Dans ce même chap. VIII, Ebn Iounis rapporte deux observations faites par les Perses, postérieurement à Ptolémée, qui ont servi à reconnoître le mouvement de l’apogée du soleil que Ptolémée croyoit immobile. Par la première de ces observations, qui remonte à l’an 470 environ, ère vulgaire, l’apogée du Soleil fut trouvé dans 17° 55′ des gémeaux; et par la seconde, 160 ans environ après, 630 ère vulgaire, dans 20° des gémeaux» («In this same chapter VIII, Ibn Yūnus reports two observations made by the Persians after Ptolemy, which served to recognize the motion of the apogee of the sun, which Ptolemy believed immovable. By the first of these observations, which goes back to about the year 470 of the common era, the apogee of the sun was found at 17° 55′ of Gemini; and by the second, about 160 years later, 630 of the common era, at 20° of Gemini»). — Ibn Ezra[^305.4], who died in the second half of the twelfth century, says in his preface to the Hebrew translation of the *Foundations of the tables of al-Khwārizmī*, written by a certain Ibn al-Muthannā, that Ptolemy wrongly believed the apogee of the sun to be immovable, whose motion, equal to the motion of the fixed stars, «the learned men of Ishmael», that is, the Arabs, were the first to recognize[^305.5].

But al-Masʿūdī is gravely mistaken when he says that the Indians too ascribe to the apogee of the sun this motion of 1 degree in 100 years, or 36″ a year (which is the motion of precession according to Ptolemy). For the Indians do hold that the apogees of the sun and the planets move, but very slowly. In the book *Sūrya-siddhānta*, Burgess's translation, ch. The *proper* motion of the apogee, on the other hand, being very small, can be derived only from observations carried on over a very long space of time and with the help of the best instruments. So it is no wonder that the Arabs mostly did not know it; for the discrepancies, and very small ones, that appeared to them in the motion of the longitude of the apogee with respect to the motion of precession they necessarily put down to the fault of the observations, knowing that it is very difficult to measure the place of the solar apogee. Al-Zarqālī[^306.5], however, on the evidence of Aboul Hhassan, ch. XII, p. 132, by comparing the observations of Ptolemy with al-Battānī's and his own, established that the apogee of the sun completes 1° in every 299 Roman years, that is, $12\frac{12}{299}''$ a year[^n], which agrees wonderfully with the value 11.46″ accepted today; but he held that this motion takes place forward and backward alternately on the circumference of a small circle, because, as appears from his *Toledan Tables*, the apogee seemed to have advanced 16° 47′ in the 740 years from Ptolemy to al-Battānī, but to have gone back 4° 27′ in the 193 years from al-Battānī to al-Zarqālī. — The Jew Levi ben Gerson, or Leo de Balneolis (1288–1370), in his astronomy written in Hebrew, derived the motion of the apogee of the sun by diligently comparing his own observations with older ones as 1° in 43 2/3 years, that is, 1′ 19″ 39‴ $53.6^{\mathrm{IV}}$ a year[^307.1]. And since he had accepted al-Battānī's annual precession of 54″ 33‴, he put the *proper* motion of the apogee of the sun at 25″ 3‴. — Of the European astronomers, Kepler and Longomontanus were the first to distinguish the motion of the apogee of the sun from the motion of the fixed stars, that is, of precession; see I. B. Riccioli, *Almagestum novum astronomiam veterem novamque complectens*, Bologna 1651, vol. I, pp. 157–58.

To compute the *equation of the centre*.
@p 0308b025..0308b034 + 0309b001..0309b013
The geometrical constructions of pp. 46–47 are taken from *Almag.* III, 5 (ed. Halma vol. I, pp. 190–199); al-Battānī's formula, on the other hand, is not in Ptolemy:
\[ \sin(\text{eq. of the centre}) = \frac{R\,\frac{e\sin m}{R}}{\sqrt{\bigl(\frac{e\sin m}{R}\bigr)^2 + \bigl(\frac{e\cos m}{R} + 60^{p}\bigr)^2}}, \]
where *e* is the eccentricity or, on the hypothesis of the epicycle, the radius of the epicycle[^308.1]; *m* the arc of the anomaly, that is, the arc of the eccentric or of the epicycle that the star has run from the apogee to the given moment of time. The rule is very easily derived from the constructions of Ptolemy and al-Battānī. On the hypothesis of the epicycle, where the equation of the centre = the angle HEF,
\[ \sin HEF : MF = \sin FME : EF, \quad\text{that is,}\quad \sin(\text{eq. of the centre}) : MF = \sin 90^\circ : EF, \]
and since sin 90° = *R*,
\[ \sin(\text{eq. of the centre}) = R\,\frac{MF}{EF}. \]
\[ [[1)]] EF = \sqrt{\overline{MF}^2 + (AE + AM)^2} = \sqrt{\overline{MF}^2 + (60^{p} + AM)^2}. \]
\[ AM : AF = \sin AFM : \sin AMF = \cos HAF : R, \]
whence $AM = \frac{AF \times \cos HAF}{R} = \frac{e\cos m}{R}$.
\[ MF : AF = \sin HAF : \sin AMF, \]
whence $MF = \frac{AF \times \sin HAF}{R} = \frac{e\sin m}{R}$. Let these values of the lines AM, MF be put into 1); there will result
\[ EF = \sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{e\sin m}{R}\Bigr)^2 + \Bigl(\frac{e\cos m}{R} + 60^{p}\Bigr)^2}, \]
which is the denominator of al-Battānī's formula.

\[ \tan(\text{eq.}) = \tan HEF = R\,\frac{MF}{60^{p} + AM} = R\,\frac{e\sin m}{60^{p}R + e\cos m}, \]
