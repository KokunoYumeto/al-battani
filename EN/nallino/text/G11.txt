{ar:كَسَرَ} i, *to take a fraction* from ({ar:من}); 9$_{14}$ {ar:فانّه يُكْسَر له من الجنس الذي هو اعلى منه واحدًا فيُحْسَب ستّين جُزْءًا} «a unit is taken for it from the next higher order and counted as 60 parts»; 158$_{19}$ {ar:على حَسَب ما يُمْكِن وما لا تحتاج معه ان تكسِر من الساعات ساعة} «as far as can be done, so that there is no need to break any of the whole hours». — {ar:تَكْسِيرٌ} *the area, the measure of a surface*, 149$_{9,\,15,\,19}$, 150 several times, 164$_{11,\,14}$, 165 several times. Cf. al-Farghānī, ed. Golius, ch. VIII, p. 31: {ar:كان ما يجتمع من ذلك مساحة بسيط جميع الارض مكسرا} «and what is gathered from it will be the area of the whole surface of the earth»; ibid.: {ar:وتكون مساحة جميع ربع المسكون مكسرا} «the area of the whole inhabited quarter of the earth will contain in its surface ...». On these places Golius notes, p. 75: «{ar:مكسرا}. The geometers mostly use this word when they express by a number of measures the capacity of a surface or of a body, gathered by multiplying the dimensions into one another. When it is an area, it is {ar:تكسير السطح}; when a solid, {ar:تكسير المجسّم}. So also {ar:ذراع مكسرة} denotes now *a square cubit*, now *a cubic* one. The verb itself properly means *to break into parts*. The reason why it was transferred here seems to be that this capacity can be represented no better than by dividing the spaces themselves into square or cubic pieces; or that, when the quantities of the dimensions are multiplied into one another, the parts of the product usually come out more numerous than their parts». The first explanation is the more probable, if one compares the way in which the geometers demonstrate the computation of surfaces and volumes. The verb {ar:كَسَّرَ} is in Dozy, II, 465,*b*, and Friedlaender, 99,*b*. Ibn Ezra uses שִׁבּוּר (breaking)[^800.2], which corresponds exactly to the Arabic {ar:تكسير}, in the proper and in the transferred sense.
