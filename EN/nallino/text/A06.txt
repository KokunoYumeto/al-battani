@p 0253b034..0253b042 + 0254b001..0254b005
Plato wrongly has 3500. — Those ancients, then, reckoned the degree of the earth's circumference at 75 miles; the 65 (in Plato 85) that are read at the end of the chapter seem to be an error, as I said on p. 19, note 8. Such a degree is of Graeco-Syrian origin. The Roman mile (1479.5 metres) contains seven *philetaerian* stadia (213.048 m), which were in very wide use in Egypt and Syria in the decline of the Roman empire. The Graeco-Syrian writers believed that the stadia of which Ptolemy gives 180,000 to the circumference of the earth were philetaerian; they therefore held that the equator of the earth contains 27,000 miles, and the degree 75 miles. Some Arabs, however, confusing the Roman miles with the Arabic ones (1973 m), took over the numbers of the Syrian writers without change. Compare what I discussed in *Il valore metrico del grado di meridiano secondo i geografi arabi*, in Guido Cora's *Cosmos*, vol. XI, Turin 1892, pp. 50–53 (= pp. 9–11 of the offprint); add that Jacob of Edessa, who died in 708 or 710, writes in his Syriac *Hexaemeron* that some make the degree 90 miles, others 75 (A. Hjelt, *Études sur l’Hexaméron de Jacques d’Édesse*, Helsingfors 1892, p. 20).
@p 0254b006..0254b011

They had found that, between about 35° and 36° of latitude, the degree is 56 2/3 Arabic miles, that is, 111,814.67 m; their error was therefore only 877 metres. See what I wrote, loc. cit., pp. 53–63, 105–121[^n] (= pp. 12–39 of the offprint).

@p 0254b013..0254b019
The Indians held that the island of *Laṅkā* (Taprobane, today Ceylon), placed on the equator, occupied the middle place in the longitude of the earth; the Arabs, not rightly understanding the Indian tradition, held that the central meridian of the earth passed through an island known by no name and lying on the equator between India and Ethiopia. The place where the central meridian cuts the equator they called *qubbat al-arḍ*, «the dome of the earth», as the author says, or simply *al-qubba*, «the dome, the cupola»; whence the corrupt word *Catha* in the old Latin translation of the astrology of ʿAlī ibn Abī l-Rijāl[^254.1].

*Albohazen Haly filii Abenragel, De iudiciis astrorum libri octo, doctorum aliquot virorum opera in Latinum sermonem conversi, postremo a barbarie vindicati et puritati linguae donati, per Antonium Stupam.* Basel 1571, book VIII, ch. 8, p. 374.
@p 0254b020..0254b030
It was also known by the name *qubbat Uzayn* (or *Azīn* ازين); for the Indians held that the first meridian of the island of Laṅkā passed through the city of *Ujjayinī*, very famous among them (Ptolemy's Ὀζήνη, today Ujjain اجين in the province of Mālwa in central India, 75° 51′ 45″ E of Greenwich, 23° 11′ 10″ N), which flourished greatly in trade in the first three centuries of the Christian era and was perhaps the chief seat of astronomical studies in India. The Arabs mostly confused *Uzayn* or *Azīn* (for, with the signs of the vowels left out as usual, ازين can be read either way) with the island of Laṅkā itself, and put it on the equator; later writers, leaving out a dot in the writing, pronounced it *Arīn* (ارين), whence *Arin*, *Arim*, *Arym* in our writers of the Middle Ages. Read the excellent discussion of Reinaud, *Introduction générale à la Géographie d’Aboulféda*, Paris 1848, pp. CCXXXVI–CCLIV; of the Arabs, al-Bīrūnī above all should be consulted (*India*, pp. 158–159 of the text, vol. I, pp. 306–308 of the English translation).

[For the area of a spherical quadrilateral bounded by two parallels and two meridians is not found by multiplying the angular extent in latitude by the angular extent in longitude, since the degrees of one parallel, in linear measure, necessarily differ from the degrees of another. But even if one could compute areas as the author says, the computation would be wrong: half the circumference ought to be multiplied not by a sixth of the whole 360°, but by a third of the 180° that the latitude spans from pole to pole, so that the area found would be not 1/12 but 1/6 of the earth[^255.1]. — The area of the spherical zone between the equator and the parallel of 60° is to the whole sphere as the sine of 60° is to the diameter; so half that zone will be to the whole sphere as the sine of 60° (that is, ½√3, or 0.8660) to twice the diameter (that is, 4, the radius being taken as 1). Instead of 0.8660 : 4, or 0.2165 : 1, those ancients found 1 : 12, or 0.0833 : 1, which is nearly three times less than the truth. — Schiaparelli.]

@p 0255b012..0255b015
*The description of the seas and the lands.* — In the translation I have already pointed out, in their places, the Arab authors who drew from the same unknown source from which al-Battānī took his information; and I said that our author's passage had already been edited and translated into French by Reinaud. It remains to inquire what this common source is.
@p 0255b016..0255b032
The oldest of all the Arab writers who tell such things about the seas in their books still extant today are Ibn Rusta, who wrote between the years 285 and 300 H (898–913), and Qudāma ibn Jaʿfar, who died in the year 337 H (which began on 10 July 948); al-Kharaqī, on the other hand, as we shall see below, says that he borrowed his information from al-Jayhānī (who wrote a little after 301 H, 913–14) «and other sages». All of them, therefore, belong to the age of our author. But a passage of al-Masʿūdī, *Tanbīh*, p. 51, sheds more light for us; for of the Indian sea he tells the following: «According to those who have applied themselves to measuring and depicting the earth by its places in astronomical longitudes and latitudes, the length of this sea from east to west comprises 8000 miles; its breadth to the north 2700, and [also] 1900 is said. So Ptolemy reports, and others who lived before and after him; the last who has attained these things in Islam is Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī, in his epistle on the seas and their ebb and flow, and his pupil Aḥmad ibn al-Ṭayyib in his epistle on the uses of the seas, the mountains and the rivers». Al-Masʿūdī mentions the same men in the *Prairies*, vol. I, p. 276, where he speaks of the breadth of the sea of the Byzantines[^255.2]. In this way we reach a time before the age of al-Battānī; for al-Kindī, who had ordered a translation of Ptolemy's Geography to be made for himself[^255.3], died about the year 260 H (which began on 26 October 873); his pupil Aḥmad ibn al-Ṭayyib al-Sarakhsī in the year 286 (which began on 16 January 899).

Consider now that the degree of 75 miles comes from the Graeco-Syrian writers, as I showed in note *a*; add what I shall prove in the second part of this work[^255.4], namely that unmistakable Syriac traces are to be recognized in al-Battānī's tables of the longitudes and latitudes of cities: it will not be a rash conjecture that the early Arabs received that geographical information and those measures of the seas from the Syrians. And the conjecture becomes quite certain, since in fact all these measures are found already in the famous Syriac writer Jacob of Edessa[^256.1].

@p 0256b003..0256b029
If we now ask whence the Graeco-Syrians collected all this, we can only agree with the words of al-Masʿūdī quoted above, that it takes its first origin from Ptolemy's *Geography*. Let the miles be turned into degrees, taking the degree as 75 miles and making no distinction between the degree of the meridian and the degrees of the parallels (an error which, as we saw in note *c*, those authors had already made): everything will be found to agree perfectly with Ptolemy's map. It is surprising that Reinaud[^256.2] and Lelewel[^256.3] not only did not recognize this but at times denied it. For the former, considering the reading of the codex that gives the Indian sea 1900 (instead of 900) miles of breadth beyond the equator, writes on p. CCLXXXIV: «Mille et neuf cents milles, à raison de soixante et quinze milles par degré, donnaient un peu plus de vingt-cinq degrés. Voilà un des motifs qui me font croire qu’Albateny n’a pas admis le système de Ptolémée, relativement à la prolongation du continent africain du côté de l’est» («Nineteen hundred miles, at seventy-five miles to the degree, gave a little more than twenty-five degrees. This is one of the reasons that make me believe that Albateny did not accept Ptolemy's system as regards the extension of the African continent towards the east»). So, in drawing the picture of the world according to al-Battānī (or rather according to the ancient Greeks and Syrians whom al-Battānī copied), he makes the African coast, immediately south of the equator, turn west from the farthest peninsula of the Somalis in an almost straight line and join the coast of the Atlantic Ocean. Lelewel, vol. I, p. 37, note 86, attacks this, rightly observing, among other considerations, that to determine the breadth of the Indian Ocean is the same as to put land to the south of it; but afterwards he adds: «Mais nourrir l’idée d’une mer interne Hind, n’est pas suivre le système de Ptolémée ou copier sa carte, et je désire disculper Albateni d’une semblable accusation» («But to entertain the idea of an inner sea of India is not to follow Ptolemy's system or to copy his map, and I wish to clear Albateni of such an accusation»). And on p. 37, note 83, after reporting some of al-Battānī's measures of the seas, he remarks quite wrongly: «Comptant 75 milles au degré, ces nombres paraissent trop forts même pour le système de Ptolémée. La longueur des deux mers intérieurs [that is, the sea of the Byzantines and the Indian sea], 5000 et 8000, ferait évaluer la longueur de l’habitable sur le grand cercle à 185 1/3 degrés [*sic*, but 13000 : 75 = 173 1/3], à quoi il faudrait ajouter quelques degrés de plus. Pour adapter ces nombres à la carte ptoléméenne, il faudrait compter au moins 80 milles au degré du grand cercle» («Counting 75 miles to the degree, these numbers seem too large even for Ptolemy's system. The length of the two inner seas, 5000 and 8000, would put the length of the inhabited land on the great circle at 185 1/3 degrees, to which some degrees more would have to be added. To fit these numbers to Ptolemy's map one would have to count at least 80 miles to the degree of the great circle»). An error of arithmetical division, then, was perhaps the chief reason why Lelewel denied the Ptolemaic origin of this chapter of al-Battānī; a lesser reason was that at that time the Arabic and Syriac writers who give the same measures were not known.

«The nearest extremity», «the nearest direct course», etc., of almost all the Arab writers can only be explained as «nearest to us»; and since both the Arab authors and the Syrians (from whom the Arabs received those notions about the seas) lived in Syria and in Mesopotamia, the part of the sea of al-Qulzum nearest to them must plainly be the northern one. Nor can «the end» be understood otherwise, since it is opposed to the beginning, that is, to the strait we call Bāb el-Mandeb. Jacob of Edessa confirms it most certainly; for he writes in the *Hexaemeron* (p. LXVI of Hjelt's translation): «in latitudinem extrema parte australi 400 fere milliaria, septentrionali vero ad ducenta aut fortasse minus patet» («in breadth it extends about 400 miles at its southern end, but at the northern up to two hundred or perhaps less»). This being so, one might think that the true reading is the one Qudāma gives, and that in this passage the name *Green Sea* belongs to the Mediterranean (as the Arabs sometimes, though rarely, say), which can rightly be called adjoining the northern end of the sea of al-Qulzum. There are, however, difficulties: it seems hardly probable that Qudāma alone kept the good reading and that all the others, even those older than Qudāma, corrupted the text; nor could one explain why the name *Green Sea* is never mentioned in the passages of these descriptions that deal with the Mediterranean.
@p 0266b012..0266b025
The true interpretation can, I think, be drawn from a passage of the Syriac astronomy of Barhebraeus, edited very recently by Nau. The author, who wrote in 1279, used the oldest Syriac authors and the Arab writers alike in describing the seas, lakes and rivers; of the Red Sea (*yamā sūmāqā*) he has the following (p. 135 of the text): «The second [gulf that arises from the Indian Ocean] is the Red Sea, which is small and extends 400 miles in length; the breadth of its extremity, which is the *yamā dh-sūph*, comprises 200 miles». This *yamā dh-sūph* ܝܡܐ ܕܣܘܦ, which is read already in the oldest Syriac translations of the Old and the New Testament, is the Hebrew name *yam sūph* adapted to the nature of the Syriac language; it means «the sea of weeds» and denotes the stretch of the Red Sea in which Pharaoh, pursuing the Israelites, perished most miserably with his men. The first Syrian who compiled that description of the seas from Ptolemy's Geography would not leave out a name most famous in the sacred traditions; but the Arab who translated the Syriac, not knowing well what plant the word *sūph* answered to, rightly rendered it, for the sense, as «*Green Sea*». — So al-Battānī's reading is the best; Qudāma's المتصل, «joined, adjoining», is an error or a false correction for المسمى, «called».
