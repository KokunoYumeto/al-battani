*The knowledge of the new moon* is of the greatest moment among the Muslim nations, not only for Arabic chronology but also because of the precepts of religion on the fast of the month of Ramaḍān. For the fast begins when the new moon of the month of Ramaḍān appears, and ends when the new moon of the month of Shawwāl is seen. So the Muslims must look out for the new moon on the last (the twenty-ninth) day of the month of Shaʿbān[^354.1], and, if they see it, begin the fast. The first day of Ramaḍān is called the «day of doubt» (*yawm al-shakk*); for on it it can happen that, although the air is clear, the new moon remains invisible, so that it is uncertain whether people must fast or not. When this day has passed the fast must always be begun, even if the new moon cannot be seen because of the conditions of the air. On that «day of doubt», if there is some impediment in the air by which the moon cannot appear, the fast must be begun when the religious judge (*qāḍī*) of the city has accepted the testimony of a single truthful person who asserts that he has seen the new moon, whether a man or a woman, free or a slave[^354.2]. But if there is no impediment in the air, no one's testimony is accepted until many people have seen the new moon, from whose testimony the matter is most certainly established[^355.1]. — They make nothing of astronomical computations of the new moon, except a few theologians and jurists of the Shāfiʿite school, among them Ibn Surayj (d. 306 H, 918).

@p 0355b022..0355b023
[Besides clear air, the author lays down three conditions on which, above all, it depends whether the crescent of the moon can be seen at the time of setting after the new moon.
@p 0355b024..0355b029 + 0356b001..0356b008
I. *The angular distance of the moon from the sun*, which measures the size of the visible crescent. Let T be the observer on the surface of the earth, TS the direction of the sun, L the moon, LS the straight line that joins the moon to the sun. Because of the very great distance of the sun, the straight lines TS, LS can be considered parallel. The illuminated hemisphere of the moon will be CED, which is determined by the plane CD perpendicular to TS and LS; the hemisphere that can be seen by the observer is AFB, determined by the plane AB perpendicular to TL; the part of the moon visible in the shape of a crescent will be the lune CLA, whose breadth in degrees is the angle CLA = STL, namely the angle that the centres of the sun and the moon make at the eye of the observer T. — Al-Battānī says that, given mean conditions in which these observations are made, the lunar crescent first becomes visible when the angle STL is 12 degrees. He adds that these 12° differ very little from the mean motion by which the moon moves away from the sun in a day, namely 12° 11′; so this roughly confirms the opinion of those who say that the moon can be seen only about 24ʰ after the true conjunction. As the basis of the computation he chooses 12° 11′.
@fig AB01-PDF0356-F01 AB01-PDF0356-body-F01
@p 0356b009..0356b017
II. *The light of the lower sky at the place where the moon is at the time of setting*, which is determined by the depth of the sun below the horizon at that moment. With the moon and the sun placed on the equator (which mostly does not happen, but is the mean condition of things), it appears from the observation just set out that this depth suffices whenever, from the setting of the sun to the setting of the moon, as many minutes have elapsed as answer to an intervening arc of 12°, that is, 4/5 of an hour. In these 4/5 of an hour the moon moves 2/5 of a degree, or 24′, away from the sun; so if, when the moon sets, the elongation of the two luminaries is 12° 11′, when the sun sets it will be 24′ less, namely 11° 47′. Al-Battānī writes 11° 45′ in round numbers.
@p 0356b018..0356b027
But when the line joining the two luminaries is not parallel to the equator, the arc of 12° 11′ cannot be the limit of the appearance. In the very clime of the city of al-Raqqa that line can be nearly parallel to the horizon, because of the obliquity of the ecliptic and the latitude of the moon; sometimes, too, it becomes perpendicular to the horizon. Then that magnitude is not the measure of the depth of the sun below the horizon and of the light of the sky. — So al-Battānī lays down as a general rule that 4/5 of an hour is the arc of the sun's parallel that has passed the horizon between the setting of the sun and the setting of the moon; then he supposes, as a general rule, that under mean conditions 12° of the equator, or 4/5 of an hour, have passed between the two settings, which, as I said, answers to the difference of 11° 45′ at the time of the setting of the sun. Then the depth of the sun below the horizon will be nearly constant; in the clime of al-Raqqa it can vary between about 10° and 8°, according to the season of the year.
@p 0356b028..0356b035
III. *The distance of the moon from the earth.* Al-Battānī supposes that the appearance of the moon also depends on the apparent breadth of the luminous crescent, which, other conditions being equal, is proportional to the horizontal parallax of the moon and follows the same laws of variation. In computing these variations he considers the changes of the distance of the moon according to its position on the epicycle; and he supposes that the mean state of things obtains when the moon is at its mean distance, that is, is as far from the earth as the centre of the epicycle, and the sixtieths of the correction of the anomaly for the parallax are 30′ in the third column[^356.1] of the table in vol. II, p. 89. He supposes too that the constant quantities 12° and 4/5 of an hour (of which I spoke above) answer to the mean lunar distance.
@f 356.1 0356n001..0356n002
So al-Battānī, p. 87, for he computes two series of numbers for the two columns. Otherwise it is the second; it is headed «The shares of the distances of the moon».
@p 0356b036..0356b042 + 0357b001..0357b002
With these suppositions al-Battānī reasons in this way. The condition under which the moon is visible arises from three variable factors, of which one, by growing, can make up for the diminution of another. To give an example, if the elongation of the moon from the sun is greater, the crescent will be proportionally broader; so a smaller depth of the sun below the horizon will be needed to make the moon visible, and the fundamental *arc of seeing* (11° 45′) can be diminished in that proportion. If the moon is nearer the earth than at the mean distance, the apparent size will grow proportionally, and a smaller depth of the sun will be needed for the appearance of the moon. If, lastly, the elongation and the nearness are both greater, the corrections to be applied to the mean *arc of seeing* are to be put together. — From this al-Battānī derives his rules, which can be set out thus:
@p 0357b003..0357b008
I. On the given day, at the time of the setting of the sun, let the places of the sun and the moon be derived from the tables (the parallax must be included in this), and let it be found by what interval of time the setting of the place of the moon so computed follows the setting of the sun. Let A be this interval, expressed in degrees. — Plainly, if the moon then had the regular crescent that answers to 12° 11′ of elongation, and the mean distance from the earth, the problem would be solved by comparing A with the fundamental arc of seeing, 11° 45′. The moon will be visible that evening if A is greater than 11° 45′.
@p 0357b009..0357b016
II. If at that time the elongation of the moon from the sun differs from 12° 11′, let the elongation (the parallax included) be computed as it appears to an observer on the surface of the earth[^357.1]; let its ratio to the fundamental elongation of 12° 11′ be computed. If the ratio exceeds 1, let the fundamental arc of seeing, 11° 45′, be diminished in proportion; if, on the contrary, the ratio is less than 1, let the arc be increased in the inverse proportion. There will come out the *arc of seeing* reduced to the fundamental elongation of 12° 11′. For if the true elongation is greater than the fundamental one, the crescent of the moon must be proportionally broader, and the moon therefore visible under a smaller arc of seeing. This arc B, so reduced, is the one that would have to be compared with the number A if the moon occupied the mean distance from the earth.
@f 357.1 0357nl001..0357nl006
This is done closely enough by treating this elongation as the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the latitude of the moon and its difference in longitude from the sun. Those who want a more exact computation al-Battānī bids (p. 87, ll. 11–13) use other precepts.
@p 0357b017..0357b029
III. But if the moon does not keep the mean distance, one must enter with the corrected lunar anomaly into the third[^357.2] column of the table in vol. II, p. 89, which serves for the analogous reduction of the parallax; the horizontal parallax and the apparent breadth of the lunar crescent decrease in the same proportion as the distance. The mean crescent answers to the number 30 of column III, the least to the number 0, the greatest to the number 60. A variation of 30 parts makes 1/12 of the apparent length of the crescent itself; so, if *n* is the number of the third column that answers to the given anomaly, when $n < 30$ the crescent will be diminished in the ratio of 1 to $1 - \frac{1}{12}\cdot\frac{30 - n}{30}$, and when $n > 30$ it will be increased in the ratio of 1 to $1 + \frac{1}{12}\cdot\frac{n - 30}{30}$. If the crescent is to be increased (that is, if *n* is greater than 30), let the arc of seeing B computed before be diminished in the same ratio; if the crescent is to be diminished ($n < 30$), let the arc B be increased in the inverse ratio. There will come out the quantity C, which will be the *arc* corrected for both variations, namely of the elongation and of the distance from the earth. With C, lastly, the number A must be compared; if this is greater, the moon will be visible in a clear and pure sky; if A is smaller, one must wait for the next day.

He explains the choice of these numbers in a rather strange way, saying that the first is the *least* relative motion of the sun and the moon on the ecliptic that can occur (namely when the sun has its greatest hourly motion and the moon its least), the second the *greatest* relative motion, when the sun moves as slowly and the moon as fast as possible. But however that may be, the condition of things represented by these numbers does not differ much from the condition chosen in the preceding solution. II. Al-Battānī, p. 91, ll. 1, 5, 9, 11, 34, mentions the use of *tubes* in observations, by which, as is well known, a great part of the rays of diffused celestial light, which disturb vision, are kept out. This is the oldest mention of all of tubes among the Arabs; for those who have written the history of optics know only the tube fitted to a large sextant with which Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī observed the sun in the observatory of Marāgha (founded in 657 H, 1259); instead of the alidade (diopter), the tube led the rays of the sun to the divisions of the sextant.
@p 0361b018..0361b030
Among the *Greek astronomers* there is no mention of tubes, except for the little book of Pseudo-Plutarch *On the face in the orb of the moon*, which concerns observing the moon. Aristotle, *On the generation of animals*, V, 1, compares vision to a motion: «so things far away would be seen best if a kind of tube (αὐλός) extended from the eye straight to the thing to be seen; for then the motion that proceeds from the visible thing would nowhere be dispersed. But since this is not so, it must be that the farther it reaches, the farther things are seen»[^n]. Polybius, X, 43 ff., mentions a dioptra fitted with two tubes, with which Cleoxenus and Democletus observed signals made by distant soldiers. In the *Talmud* it is related that Rabbi Gamaliel had a tube for measuring the distances of places and the depths of valleys. In Europe, lastly, Walafrid learned the use of the tube in 825 at Reichenau[^n]. — See R. Wolf, *Handbuch der Astronomie, ihrer Geschichte und Litteratur*, Zürich 1890–93, vol. I, pp. 318–19, § 133; and S. Günther, *Das gläserlose Sehrohr im Altertum und Mittelalter*, in Bibliotheca Mathematica, ed. G. Eneström, Neue Folge, vol. VIII, Stockholm 1894, pp. 15–23.

Schiaparelli points out to me a passage of the very ancient Chinese book called *Shù-King*[^361.1], in which this is related of King Shun (2255–2205 BC): «He observed the jewelled (or jewel-like?) sphere and the jewel-adorned tube, and learned the seven directors [the planets]». But from the information that the distinguished sinologist L. Nocentini most kindly gave me, it is plain that this version is not certain in every part and rests on commentaries of the second century BC. Moreover, from the description of the very famous interpreter Ts‘ai C‘in (1167–1230), who used those older commentaries and says that the tube was placed crosswise, it is by no means clear whether the tube served to observe the stars or rather the armillary sphere itself[^n].
