The famous Halley (1656–1742) too, thinking that great profit could be drawn from his so accurate observations, tried with no small labour to emend in many places the numbers corrupted in both editions of Plato's translation[^47.8], though not always with complete success; and he judged al-Battānī thus (loc. cit., pp. 913–14): «This author was certainly of admirable acuteness for his age, and most practised in conducting observations, as appears from this, that, having taken the moments of the equinoxes, he would seem to have restored the motion of the Sun entirely, if, departing further from Ptolemy's footsteps, he had seen that the eccentricity of the Sun must be bisected.....[^48.1]. But his observations, the only ones we have through so many centuries that elapsed between Ptolemy and Regiomontanus, deserve by right to be preserved and placed among the most precious κειμήλια of Urania; especially as they afford excellent uses in demonstrating the inequality of the annual time, as I am trying to prove on another occasion». Two years later, noting that certain English merchants had come as far as al-Raqqa, called Yarecca by them, he wrote[^48.2]: «The Latitude thereof was observed by that *Albatāni* with great accurateness, about eight hundred years since; and therefore I recommend it to all that are curious of such Matters, to endeavour to get some good Observation made at this Place, to determin the Height of the *Pole* there, thereby to decide the Controversie, whether there hath really been any Change in the *Axis* of the Earth, in so long an Interval». Afterwards he urges travellers or merchants living there to observe accurately the phases of lunar eclipses in the cities of *Baghdād*, *Aleppo* and *Alexandria*, and in this way to determine their longitudes, which would serve astronomical science not a little; «for in and near these Places were made all the Observations whereby the Middle Motions of the *Sun* and the *Moon* are limited: And I could then pronounce in what Proportion the Moon's Motion does Accelerate; which that it does, I think I can demonstrate, and shall (God willing) one day, make it appear to the Publick». I have chosen to report this in his own words, so that the error that is still always repeated today, that Halley deduced the secular acceleration of the lunar motion from al-Battānī's observations, may not be repeated[^48.3]. The most distinguished English astronomer had indeed suspected it, considering the ancient eclipses; but he did not determine its amount in his writings.

Al-Battānī's observations were used in the study of the motion of the Moon by Rich. Dunthorne, *A Letter to the Rev. Mr. Richard Mason, concerning the Acceleration of the Moon*[^n] (Philosophical Transactions, no. 492, 1749, pp. 162–72). For he says on p. 163: «Then I compared the four well-known Eclipses observed by *Albategnius* with the Tables, and found the computed Places of the Moon in three of them considerably too forward: This, if I could have depended upon the Longitude of *Aracla*, would very much have confirmed me in the Opinion, that the Moon's mean motion must have been swifter, in some of the last Centuries than the Tables make it; though the Differences between these Observations, and the Tables, are not uniform enough to be taken for a certain Proof thereof»[^49.1]. Afterwards, comparing the eclipses of Ptolemy and of Theon (16 June AD 364), and two observed at Cairo by Ibn Yūnus, with the tables of recent writers, he deduced (p. 171) that the variation is 10″ in 100 years and that it increases in proportion to the square of the centuries.

In the second half of the eighteenth century too, several astronomers celebrated al-Battānī with much praise, but rather often mixing false things about him with true ones. La Lande, in the first edition of his Astronomy, Paris 1764, adduces his observations to prove that the obliquity of the ecliptic constantly diminishes (vol. II, p. 1027, § 2179; cf. the 3rd ed., Paris 1792, vol. III, pp. 57–58, § 2743), to determine the motion of the solar apogee (vol. I, 486, § 982; cf. 3rd ed., II, 74, § 1313), and the true amount of the precession (3rd ed., I, 314, § 917); but he is in error when he asserts that al-Battānī fixed that motion of precession at one degree in 70 years (vol. I, 106, § 245; 3rd ed., I, 123, § 355), and the apparent diameter of the stars of the first magnitude at 45″ (vol. II, 1052, § 2226). Bailly too deals with al-Battānī[^49.2]; but, besides the errors in his life already mentioned, he says on p. 596 nothing but the falsest things about his trigonometry, drawn from Regiomontanus's very faulty observations on chapter XXVI[^49.3], and he wrongly relates that al-Battānī determined the *proper* motion of the apogee of the Sun, 4″ 32‴ a year[^49.4]. J. F. Montucla increases this error, asserting that he not only knew the proper motion of the apogee of the Sun but also suspected a motion of the apogees of the planets distinct from the precession[^49.5]. All those men plainly borrowed their information from the writings of Riccioli and others rather than from the astronomical work itself.

Delambre, *Histoire de l'astronomie au moyen âge*, Paris 1819, pp. 16–62, tried to draw out al-Battānī's doctrines from Plato's translation as far as could be done; but the obscure and unskilled translator often led so great a man into serious errors. And Delambre, solving many problems according to his own mind rather than the author's, and sometimes mixing his own formulas with al-Battānī's, was the reason why later Hankel[^49.6] and Cantor[^49.7], following in his footsteps, held false opinions about al-Battānī. For of the many who after Delambre have written the history of mathematics or of astronomy, only von Braunmühl, *Vorlesungen über Geschichte der Trigonometrie*, Leipzig 1900 f., vol. I, pp. 50–54, has ventured to turn over Plato's translation again, treating al-Battānī's trigonometry far better than all his predecessors, as I shall say in no. IV.
