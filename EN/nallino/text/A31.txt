@p 0326b008..0326b018
[*The spheres or circles of the planets.* — The figure is very badly drawn, and the proof has been spoiled by an unskilled hand. The circle ABCD, concentric with the earth and lying in the plane of the ecliptic, cannot lie in the same plane as the circle GBFD, which is indeed concentric with the earth but inclined at a small angle to the ecliptic. So if GBFD is drawn with the rest of the figure in the plane of the sheet, the circle ABCD, which falls outside the sheet, cannot be represented by a circle with centre E. — The figure also puts the nodes B, D of these circles 90 degrees from the line GK that determines the apsides of the eccentric; this happens in no planet, nor could it have seemed true to al-Battānī. — The lines ETN, EXZ ought not to be tangent to the epicycle, for the planet must be considered to occupy any place on the epicycle. Nor do they lie in the *ecliptic*, but in the plane of the *inclined circle* GBFD and of the eccentric GHKL; and even this is true only when the epicycle is *wrongly* supposed to lie in the plane of the eccentric.

@p 0326b019..0326b033
I have tried to restore the figure as al-Battānī probably intended it; the figure printed on p. 64, following the codex and Plato, must be blamed on the unskilled writer of the archetype, who, as we have already seen elsewhere, afterwards tried to fit the text to a badly drawn figure. He represented the point A as if it lay in the plane GBFD; he also wrongly imagined that M, the centre of the eccentric, is the point about which the uniform motion of the centre of the epicycle takes place, whereas this motion takes place about the point E′, which is therefore the centre of the circle that the followers of Ptolemy called the *equant*[^326.1]. The point E′ is such that ME′ = ME; see Delambre, *Hist. de l’astr. anc.*, vol. II, p. 316.

@p 0327b029..0327b035
Undoubtedly much in this chapter that serves to explain the theory of the planets does not come from al-Battānī. The author wrote this chapter above all to declare how he had corrected Ptolemy's planetary tables (pp. 65–66); he considered the cosmographical elements already known to his readers (as in the other parts of the book), and so perhaps had mentioned the spheres or circles of the planets only summarily, with the help of a figure. This brief exposition of the theory, I think, the usual unlearned reader tried to make fuller according to his small powers, and in this way he disfigured al-Battānī's text.

@p 0330b029..0330b035 + 0331b001..0331b003
It is difficult to pass judgement on these differences, for the eccentric of the Ptolemaic hypothesis does not answer exactly to the Keplerian ellipse. The circular and uniform motion of the planet on the epicycle represents the elliptical and unequal motion of the earth about the sun only approximately, not precisely. So the ancients and the Arabs, to express the observations rightly, transferred the effect of the eccentricity and of the inequality of the motion of the earth to the eccentric; from which it follows that the eccentricities and apogees of Ptolemy and of the Arabs are nothing but compositions of the eccentricity and apogee of the planet with the eccentricity and apogee of the earth's orbit. If anyone wished to judge how much perfection those observations attained, he would have to undertake a long analysis for each planet. It is certain that the columns I have headed *errors* follow partly in fact from the imperfect theory, and partly from the fact that the data of the ancients cannot be compared directly with those of today.
@p 0331b004..0331b016
As for *Venus*, whose apogee in al-Battānī and almost all the Arabs does not differ from the apogee of the sun, it must be noted that the eccentricity of the elliptical orbit that Venus describes about the sun is very small, so that this orbit can be considered an exactly circular epicycle; the deferent then becomes the orbit of the sun itself, about which, as we know today, the planet revolves. Although al-Battānī and the authors of the *Tested Tables* did not know this last fact, they nevertheless understood that the motion of Venus can be represented well when its *eccentric deferent* is given *the same eccentricity and the same apogee* as the orbit of the sun has. So there is not only the same longitude of the apogee, but also the same eccentricity and equation of the centre, as appears from al-Battānī's tables. Ptolemy, on the other hand, used the same eccentricity and equation of the centre (*Almag.* III, 5 and XI, 11, ed. Halma vol. I, p. 201, and II, 307), but put the apogee of Venus about 20° from the apogee of the sun[^331.1][^n]. — Who judged more rightly cannot be decided until it has first been well established, with hard labour, how the eccentric of Venus represents the composition of the elliptical orbits of the earth and of Venus. — Schiaparelli.]
