The angles BED of the ecliptic and the horizon are equal at points of the ecliptic equally distant from one of the equinoxes; likewise the angle at a rising point of the ecliptic equals the angle at the opposite point, which sets at the same time. So if the planets always lay in the plane of the ecliptic, that is, if they had no latitude,
@p 0708b007..0708b008
1° the arcs of the appearances (or disappearances) would be equal at points equally distant from one of the equinoxes;
@p 0708b009
2° the arcs of appearance of the point *x* would equal the arcs of disappearance of the point *x* + 180°;
@p 0708b010..0708b011
3° at the solstitial points (90° and 270°), which are equally distant from the equinoxes and diametrically opposite to each other, the arcs of the appearances would equal the arcs of the disappearances;
@p 0708b012..0708b014
4° the arcs of appearance or disappearance of all the planets for a given point of the ecliptic would be proportional to the arcs of vision of the planets themselves; for in the formula for planets lying on the ecliptic only the arc of vision would vary for the same point of the ecliptic.
@p 0708b015
Now let us look at the tables of the superior planets.
@p 0708b016..0708b028
In *Saturn*, in Ptolemy, the numbers of the appearances (or disappearances) answering to signs equally distant from either equinox are equal; the numbers for signs diametrically opposite differ (the solstices excepted); the appearances also differ from the disappearances of the opposite points; finally, the greatest and the least amounts occur at the equinoxes. Hence it is clear that Saturn has latitudes very nearly *equal* at longitudes equally distant from 0° or 180°, *greatest* about the equinoxes and *nil* near the solstices; that is, the nodes of Saturn fall near the solstices (90° and 270°), as also appears from Ptolemy's doctrine of the latitudes[^708.1]. — But in al-Battānī's table the arc of appearance differs little from the arc of disappearance at the beginnings of Cancer and Capricorn, and the arcs of the appearances (or disappearances) at places equally distant from one of the equinoxes differ little from one another (always by less than 1°); whence one gathers that the latitudes of Saturn are *nil* at points somewhat distant from the solstices and *greatest* at places somewhat away from the equinoxes. The greatest latitudes of Mars, moreover, occur in two opposite regions of the sky, and not with the same amount; for the greatest northern latitude is 4° 21′, the southern 7° 7′. So the properties of the tables of Mars just mentioned can only be thought to have arisen from al-Battānī's having *neglected* the latitude of Mars. That this is not surprising will be clear from what follows. Following Theon's example, al-Battānī thinks that the appearances (or disappearances) of Mars are to be expected when the corrected anomaly is about 20° (or 340°). To this anomaly there answer in the tables of vol. II, p. 140, latitudes of Mars of 0° 11′ to the north and 0° 5′ to the south; taking the sixtieths of these latitudes according to al-Battānī's rules (vol. I, pp. 115–116), smaller amounts are obtained, such as, at the beginning of Aries, −0° 3′, of Taurus −0° 0′ 37″, of Gemini +0° 4′ 17″, of Cancer +0° 8′ 45″, of Libra +0° 6′ 36″, of Capricorn −0° 3′ 58″, of Aquarius −0° 4′ 47″, of Pisces −0° 4′ 35″, etc. The tables of the appearances are made for φ = 36° 0′, where the greatest angle BED is 77° 35′ (at the beginning of Libra) and the least 30° 25′ (at the beginning of Aries). Since, then, the latitudes of Mars to be taken for our purpose are very small, and the angles of the ecliptic and the horizon large, the difference in longitude between the planet and the point of the ecliptic rising with it will be so small that the angles BED (whose sine and cotangent we must use) can, without sensible error, be computed for the longitude of the planet itself. Moreover, the term ∓ lat. plan. × cot BED becomes for the most part insensible for Mars; for the greatest cotangent to be used in al-Battānī is 1.73, the radius being taken as 1 part. @p 0710b030..0710b040 + 0711b001..0711b008
Whether al-Battānī made these tables himself (*he warns against trusting them too much*) or took them already computed by other Arab astronomers was uncertain. Since the Escorial codex is without vowels, the passage of vol. III, p. 178, ll. 4–7, وقد وضعت لها اقدار الرؤْية بدرج البروج في كل اقليم على ما رصد من ظهورها في كل برج. ورسم ذلك في رؤس البروج وان كان ذلك ليس على الحقيقة من قبل ما يعرض من اختلاف عروضها ولذلك اقتصرنا على اثبات هذه الاقدار في اقليم واحد ....., admits a double interpretation, because of the double way in which the word وضعت can be furnished with vowel signs. For if وُضِعَتْ is read, the sense becomes: «for them (i.e. the planets) the amounts of the appearance *have been fixed*, in degrees of the signs, for every climate, according to what has been observed of their appearances in each sign; and this has been written at the beginnings of the signs, although it is not according to the truth, because of the variation arising in their (the planets') latitudes. So we have been content to note these amounts for one climate only ....». If وَضَعْتُ is read, it must be translated thus: «for them *I have fixed* (or: *I had fixed*) the amounts of the appearance ....», as in vol. I, p. 118, l. 14 ff.; the use of the first person singular and the first person plural of the verb side by side is not rare in al-Battānī and the Arabs. Plato translated the passage thus: «Learn that the amount of vision, in degrees of the signs, will be written for all the planets in each climate according to the observation of their appearance in each climate, which, although it does not altogether agree with the truth, because a diversity of latitudes occurs there, we have nevertheless written at the beginnings of the signs. And therefore we have entrusted its amounts to one climate only». — We shall see on p. 266 that the first interpretation (*have been fixed*) is the true one, because al-Battānī took his table already computed by others.
