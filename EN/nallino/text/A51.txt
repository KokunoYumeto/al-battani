II. — *The motion of the fixed stars* in longitude, or *the precession of the equinoxes.*
@p 0381b013..0381b017
After what Schiaparelli and Th. H. Martin[^n] have written, it is known that Hipparchus (about 130 BC) was the first to recognize the precession of the equinoxes[^381.1]. He had fixed its annual amount at 36″ at the least; later he had deduced from other observations amounts between 42″ and 46″.8. Ptolemy wrongly chose the smallest value found, and laid down that the motion of precession completes 1° in 100 Egyptian years (of 365 days), or 36″ a year[^381.2].

Al-Battānī rightly observes, however, that the fractions of the seconds, and perhaps the seconds themselves, are uncertain, and that in observations of the fixed stars at that time an error of some minutes was of no great importance; so, neglecting a very small fraction, he set that motion at 1 degree in 66 years, or 54″ 33‴ in a solar year[^381.3].[^n]

I. — *The addition of 11° 10′ to Ptolemy's longitudes* of the fixed stars.
@p 0382b022..0382b026
Ptolemy, VII, 3 (ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 25 and 27), had made much use of the observations made at Rome by Menelaus, who, comparing the stars with the moon, had found Spica, or α Virginis, at 176° 1/4 of longitude in the night between 15 and 16 Mechir of the first year of Trajan, 845 of Nabonassar (i.e. 9–10 February 98), and the northern star of the forehead of the Scorpion, or β Scorpii, at 215° 55′ in the night between 18 and 19 Mechir of the same year (i.e. 12–13 February)[^n].
@n In the Egyptian calendar of the *Almagest*, by which Nallino dates the year 885 of Nabonassar on p. 292 (20 July 137), the year 845 began on 30 July 97, so that 15 and 18 Mechir fell on 10 and 13 January 98: the nights are those of 10–11 and 13–14 January 98. The 9–10 and 12–13 February of the print are the dates of these nights in the fixed Alexandrian calendar, in which 1 Mechir is 26 January.
@p 0383b001..0383b009
Al-Battānī found that the longitudes of β Scorpii and α Leonis given by Menelaus had increased by 11° 50′ after 782 years. On this he clearly reasoned in this way: Ptolemy compiled his catalogue 41 years[^383.1] after Menelaus; in this interval the precession is 37′ 21″; so 11° 50′ − 0° 37′ 21″ = 11° 12′ 39″, that is, in round numbers, 11° 13′, must be added to Ptolemy's longitudes. But Ptolemy uses only the fractions 1/2, 1/3, 1/6 (very rarely 1/4) of a degree, so that his longitudes are expressed in whole tens of minutes. If I add 11° 13′, almost all the minutes of the longitudes will end in the figure 3, which will both seem strange and could lead someone into the error of taking uncertain numbers for exact ones. A difference of some minutes, and even of one degree, is of very little importance in the longitudes of the fixed stars; so instead of 11° 13′ I shall add only 11° 10′.[^n]
@n At al-Battānī's 54″ 33‴ a year, 41 years give 37′ 16″ 33‴ (at 54″ 28‴, 37′ 13″ 8‴), and 11° 50′ less 37′ 16″ 33‴ is 11° 12′ 43″ 27‴; either way, 11° 13′ in round numbers.
@f 383.1 0383nl001..0383nr002
So al-Battānī and al-Ṣūfī; but the true interval was 40 years; see note *a*, no. I (p. 292). The error, however, is of no importance, for the precession in a year is 54″ 33‴, an amount to be wholly neglected in this computation at that time.
@n The print has «note b»; the interval is that of note *a*, no. I, on p. 292, from the year 845 to the year 885 of Nabonassar.
@p 0383b010..0383b013
Al-Battānī's reasoning would be excellent if Ptolemy had used the same annual precession as al-Battānī. But the Greek astronomer's precession was 36″; so, for the interval of 41 years, Ptolemy had added 25′, not 37′ 21″, to the longitudes of Menelaus. Al-Battānī, then, should have added 11° 50′ − 0° 25′ = 11° 25′ to Ptolemy's longitudes, instead of his 11° 10′.

Al-Ṣūfī, in reducing Ptolemy's longitudes, avoided al-Battānī's error. For, pp. 42–43, he reckons in this way: I have compiled my catalogue for the year 1276 of the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn (beginning 1 October 964); from the time of Menelaus 866 years have elapsed. In them the precession has reached 13° 7′ 16″; Ptolemy has already added 25′ to the numbers of Menelaus, so I must add 13° 7′ − 0° 25′ = 12° 42′ to Ptolemy's. — Hence one understands why al-Ṣūfī's longitudes exceed al-Battānī's by 1° 32′, instead of the 1° 17′ that are the increase of the precession in the interval of 85 years.
@p 0383b025
II. — «*We found no perceptible difference of latitude*».
@p 0383b026..0383b035
Tycho Brahe, *Astronomiae instauratae progymnasmata*, vol. I, p. 246, sharply rebukes al-Battānī for keeping Ptolemy's latitudes of the fixed stars, «although he gives the inclination of the ecliptic to the equator much smaller than the ancients did, namely 23° 36′ [sic][^n], falling short of their record by a quarter of a degree, which could surely have brought some difference into the latitudes of the stars of his own age, once a comparison was made. But he seems to me rather to have been credulous towards his predecessors, and in this part too securely content with the findings of the ancients, than to have used any observation of his own, and that sufficiently accurate, for scrupulously investigating the latitudes of the stars; from which it is also quite clear that he either used instruments not entirely suitable and not showing everything precisely, or else carried out this inquiry too negligently».
@n Al-Battānī's obliquity of the ecliptic is 23° 35′.
@p 0383b036..0383b041 + 0384b001..0384b006
There is no one who does not see how unjust this is. First, al-Battānī says that he found not «*no difference*» but «*no perceptible*» one; and indeed, in the problems solved by means of the ecliptic coordinates of the fixed stars, a discrepancy of 1/2 a degree in the latitudes was of no importance in that age. Next, it must not be forgotten that Ptolemy's latitudes contain no fraction of a degree except those that answer to 10′, 15′, 20′, 30′, 40′, 45′ and 50′; that is, they are given in minutes not precisely but only approximately, so that a safe and absolute comparison becomes impossible. Lastly, al-Battānī knew that errors of some minutes could easily creep into his observations of the fixed stars. Rightly, then, he did not wish to emend Ptolemy's tables of the latitudes with very uncertain corrections. — Besides, in the interval of time between Ptolemy and al-Battānī the *greatest* variation of the latitudes (which happens in the stars placed near 90° and 270° of longitude), owing to the decrease of the obliquity of the ecliptic, was only 6 minutes; or a quarter of a degree, if the obliquities found by Ptolemy and al-Battānī are considered.

@p 0384b023
*The lunar stations or mansions of the Arabs.*
@p 0384b024..0384b034 + 0385b001..0385b036
In enumerating them al-Battānī fell into some errors, which al-Ṣūfī, pp. 37–38, sharply rebukes: «De même al-Battānī, lorsqu’il veut faire croire qu’il connaît les mansions de la lune et les étoiles suivant la méthode des Arabes مذهب العرب, et en s’occupant des choses étrangères à son sujet, a mis à nu son ignorance. Il a ainsi rapporté dans son livre le nombre d’étoiles que comprend chacune des douze constellations, comme les indique Ptolémée dans son livre[^n] *al-majisṭī*, et il dit, entre autre en parlant du Bélier, que *al-sharaṭāni* se trouve dans la corne et *al-buṭayn* dans la queue; c’est une erreur, car *al-buṭayn* se compose de trois étoiles disposées en triangle, ce qui est mentionné auparavant. Il dit aussi sur le Taureau que *al-thurayyā*, les Pléiades, se trouve sur le dos et *al-dabarān* à la racine de l’oreille[^384.6]; c’est aussi une erreur, car *al-dabarān*, qui est la brillante étoile rouge des cinq de la face, se trouve à l’oeil méridional. Il dit que *al-haqʿa*, *al-hanʿa* et *muqaddam al-dhirāʿayn*, appartiennent aux Gémeaux; c’est encore une faute, car *al-haqʿa* fait part du Géant, étant située dans la tête, entre les épaules; et il ne dit pas dans quel endroit des Gémeaux sont *al-hanʿa* et *al-dhirāʿ*. Or, *al-hanʿa* se compose de deux étoiles situées dans leurs pieds, et *al-dhirāʿ* consiste de deux étoiles brillantes dans leurs têtes. Il se trompe encore en mettant *al-ghafr* dans la Balance; car *al-ghafr* se compose de trois étoiles, dont deux sur le pan de la robe ذيل de la Vierge, et une sur son pied gauche; cette dernière est la plus méridionale des trois. Il place dans le Scorpion *al-zubānayāni*, les deux zubānā, ainsi que *al-iklīl*, la Couronne; c’est une double erreur: *al-zubānayāni* appartiennent à la Balance, et sont situés sur les deux bassins, tandis qu’il a cru que c’étaient les deux serres du Scorpion, c’est-à-dire ses cornes, suivant les Arabes. Il a soupçonné qu’*al-iklīl* se compose de trois étoiles situées sur le front du Scorpion, dans la figure même, tandis que ce sont trois étoiles disposées en ligne transversale, au-dessus du front du Scorpion. L’une d’entre elles, la plus boréale, se trouve dans le zubānā boréal de la Balance; c’est la huitième de la table de Ptolémée, et elle est de la quatrième grandeur. La deuxième, qui est au milieu des trois, est une des étoiles autour de la Balance, en dehors de la figure; c’est la sixième de ces étoiles. La troisième enfin, qui est la plus méridionale des trois, est également au nombre des étoiles externes de la Balance, et la huitième de celles-là. Toutes trois sont de la quatrième grandeur et forment une ligne courbe, semblable à celle que présentent les trois étoiles du front. Il dit que *al-naʿāʾim*, les Autruches, et *al-balda*, se trouvent dans le Sagittaire. C’est une faute, car *al-balda* est un endroit du ciel, en arrière d’*al-qilāda*[^385.1], où il n’y a point d’étoiles, et c’est pourquoi on le nomme *al-balda*, la Ville. J’ai vu, sur plusieurs sphères, que l’on a designé *al-naʿām al-wārid*[^385.2] par *al-naʿāʾim*, et *al-naʿām al-ṣādir*[^385.3] par *al-balda*. Les Arabes n’ont fait de mansion qu’au milieu entre les deux autruches. Sous le Capricorne il fait mention de *saʿd al-dhābiḥ*, et *saʿd bulaʿ*; c’est une faute, car *saʿd bulaʿ* se trouve dans la main gauche du Verseau, au-dessus du dos du Capricorne. Il dit encore que *al-fargh al-awwal* [= *al-fargh al-muqaddam*] et *al-fargh al-thānī* [= *al-fargh al-muʾakhkhar*], sont situées dans les Poissons, mais il se trompe en cela, parce que *al-farghāni*, les deux fargh, font partie de la figure du Cheval, vers le côté nord. Quant au premier *fargh*, la plus boréale de ses deux étoiles, se trouve sur l’épaule droite, où commence la jambe قامة, et la méridionale sur le dos où commence le cou. Already before Muḥammad the Arabs had divided the zodiac into 28 parts; for they had chosen 28 places as far apart from one another as the apparent course of the moon in a day and a night. These places they had called the stations or mansions of the moon (*manzil al-qamar*, plural *manāzil al-qamar*).
