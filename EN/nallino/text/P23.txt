VI. *The Arabic codices.* — None is known today except that of the Escorial, on which I have based my edition[^69.1]. The copy of the Ambrosian Library in Milan, of which there is an uncertain mention in Delambre[^69.2], contains, as we have seen, not the Arabic text but Plato's version. Sédillot, introd. to Oloug Beg, p. XXXII, asserts: «Nous avons cependant de fortes raisons de croire que l'original existe dans la Bibliothèque du Vatican et même à l'Escurial» (we have, however, strong reasons to believe that the original exists in the Vatican Library and even at the Escorial); but nothing except Plato's version is found in the Vatican. Sédillot was undoubtedly led into error by the false title of Plato's Bologna edition, quoted above, p. L; and the error was afterwards increased by the most diligent Houzeau, *Bibliographie générale de l'astronomie*, Brussels 1882–87, vol. I, col. 467, who distinguishes a text of the astronomical work (*zīj*) kept in the Vatican Library from the «tables» of the Escorial codex[^69.3].

Steinschneider, on Baldi, p. 454 (extr. p. 28), rightly conjectures that two codices which G. B. For one was: *Effemeridi del movimento del Sole, Luna e Pianeti; Autore Mehemet Ibin* [sic] *Iabet Ibin Sinan Algiorgiami* [sic] *Almaruff Bilaabeti*, in which Algiorgiami, or al-Jorjānī, seems to be corrupted from al-Ḥarrānī (which happens far more easily in Arabic writing than in ours), while the rest is to be restored as *al-maʿrūf bi-l-Battānī*, «known [by the name of] al-Battānī». The other: *Effemeridi et il modo di calcolare, e cavare il millesimo degli anni Arabi dalli Greci, et al contrario, Autore Mehemet Ibin* [sic] *Iabir Ibin Sinan Algiorgiani* [sic]; that is, the tables of vol. II, pp. 9–18. — Where these codices are now I do not know.
@p 0070b010..0070b017
The copy of the Escorial Library, formerly no. 903 (Casiri, vol. I, pp. 342–43), today marked no. 908, is $26\tfrac{1}{3}$ cm high and 19 cm wide; it has 245 leaves[^70.2], and 19 lines on each page (apart from the tables); thick cotton paper; written in the Maghribī, or western Arabic, script, which tends more to *naskhī* than to Kufic; the headings of the chapters in red ink. The letters that in the text denote numbers or the parts of a geometrical figure have small lines written over them. There is neither title nor colophon; f. 244v and 245, written by another hand, give sacred traditions (*ḥadīth*). From the script the codex seems to me to belong to the end of the eleventh or the beginning of the twelfth century AD.
@p 0070b018..0070b030
Almost all the vowels are missing; sometimes, in the more frequent words, some of the diacritical points too; of the other signs only {img:AB01-PDF0070-G01:1.68:0.45:s} and *hamza* are found. Hamza is almost always written in the middle and at the end of a word, rarely at the beginning; yet بدء and جزء always become بدو and جزو (in the accusative {img:AB01-PDF0070-G02:1.5:0.6:s}), ابطأ always ابطى, just as, the radical hamza being left out, بدت and تهى are constantly written for بدأت and {img:AB01-PDF0070-G03:1.67:0.66:s}, مايتين for مائتين, and اختفايه for اختفائه, اختفاؤه and اختفاءه. ي and ى occur indiscriminately, at the copyist's pleasure; *yāʾ* in the sense of *alif maqṣūra* is ى in the prepositions, ا in the rest (very rarely ى with ا written above it). Sometimes ى is written for ا at the end of words, as ابدى for ابدأ, and سوى for سواء. So تلقا is found for {img:AB01-PDF0070-G06:1.63:0.66:s} and for {img:AB01-PDF0070-G04:1.71:0.78:s}. Often, in verbs whose last radical is ي, the short final vowel is marked by the letter *yāʾ*, as سمى for {img:AB01-PDF0070-G07:1.37:0.58:s}, تليه for {img:AB01-PDF0070-G05:1.46:0.66:s}; likewise always ثواني for ثوان, etc. In the imperfect of verbs whose last radical is و, a redundant ا is always added at the end, in the Maghribī manner. The ending of the regular masculine plural is ين for ون always in the numerals and often in the rest; so too the ending of the nominative dual. The agreement of the numerals with the gender of the nouns is very often faulty.
@p 0070b031..0070b034 + 0071b001..0071b017
The codex comes from an archetype corrupted and interpolated here and there, whose writer, having a depraved copy before his eyes, had tried with his small powers to fill the gaps and to restore plainly faulty passages to the true reading. By the happiest chance he was a man very unskilled in the mathematical disciplines, so that his emendations and additions can always be distinguished from al-Battānī's true text. The chief corruptions, which arose not from the negligence of the copyists alone but also from rash emendations, are p. 26 (notes p. 186), 31 (note 192), 32 (note 194), almost the whole of chapter XXV (notes 197–99), 77 (notes 251–52), 82 (note 259), 98–99 (note 275), 131 (notes 307–08); interpolations or false restorations of gaps, p. 53 (notes 224–225), 65 (note 238), 80 and 81 (note 257), 106 (notes 281–82), 122–23 (notes 290–92), and the appendices D and F (notes 324–26). Elsewhere the writer of the archetype tried to fit the text of a geometrical demonstration to a badly drawn figure, as on pp. 50–51 (notes 223–24), 64 (notes 237–38), 91 (notes 271–72). Gaps, perhaps not small, on p. 78 (notes 254–55) and 85 (note 265). All these are common to the Escorial codex and to Plato of Tivoli, except the appendices D and F, which, as they belong to the tables, are missing in Plato; it is therefore abundantly clear that the copy that he used and the Escorial codex derive from the same archetype. I should even have suspected that Plato had the Escorial copy itself in his hands, had I not found in Plato a few words wrongly left out in the Escorial text. Other errors, however, of less moment, especially in the numbers, which come from the carelessness of copyists, are far more frequent in the old Latin version[^71.1], in which many things have also dropped out here and there, perhaps not always through the translator's fault. Interpolations later than the common archetype exist in the Escorial codex, I think, two at most, and those of very small weight; see above, p. XLIII, and n. 4 there.
