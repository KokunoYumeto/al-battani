@p 0310b023..0310b031 + 0311b001..0311b007
[*The equation of time.* — In al-Battānī's theory of this equation some things are worth noting. In those times (and still today, nearly) the greatest retardation of the sun with respect to its mean motion came about 20° of Aquarius, the greatest advance a little after the beginning of Scorpio. This is what al-Battānī means by the words «*the place of the diminution is at about 2/3 of Aquarius*» etc. Astronomers today have defined mean time so that it holds the middle place between the oscillations of the solar motion, and so that the correction to be applied to true time to find mean time (that is, the equation of time) is sometimes additive and sometimes subtractive. Ptolemy did otherwise; like Theon after him in the *Handy Tables* (see Delambre, *Histoire de l’astronomie ancienne*, Paris 1817, vol. II, pp. 630–632), he determined the course of mean time so that it agreed with true time when true time had advanced more than in all the other parts of the year, that is, at the beginning of Scorpio. Then the two times agree; in all the other parts of the year true time will lag behind mean time, and the correction to be applied to it to find the true time, that is, the *equation of time*, will always be additive. Its greatest amount therefore fell at 20° of Aquarius, as is gathered from the table of this equation that Delambre gives after Ptolemy and Theon, *Astr. anc.*, II, 632.
@p 0311b008..0311b029
Al-Battānī, on the other hand, for what reason I do not well know, supposes that mean and true time agree when true time is at its greatest retardation, that is, at 20° of Aquarius; for this is what the words mean, «*by this day-and-night* [20° of Aquarius] *compute the other days-and-nights of the year in this book*». Plainly, then, in all the other parts of the year true time will *precede* mean time; the correction, or equation, will always be subtractive, and will reach its greatest amount near the beginning of Scorpio, as appears from the table, vol. II, p. 64. Both ways lead to the same result; but Ptolemy's mean time *precedes* al-Battānī's mean time by the whole greatest equation, which Ptolemy makes 33ᵐ 30ˢ, al-Battānī 31ᵐ 12ˢ. For Ptolemy's mean time agrees with true time when true time is at its greatest advance, al-Battānī's when true time is at its greatest retardation. So al-Battānī, to make his astronomical tables comparable with Ptolemy's when both are entered with the same argument of time, had to increase all his epochs of the celestial motions by as much as those motions amount to in the interval of 31ᵐ 12ˢ. Such a correction is negligible in all the motions of the planets; for the sun it is a little more than 1′; for the moon, however, it reaches 18′. In this way at least it can be understood to some extent why he says below (ll. 28–29) *that he added 18′ to the mean longitude of the moon in his tables*. When al-Battānī's mean time is 0ʰ 0ᵐ 0ˢ, Ptolemy's reaches 0ʰ 32ᵐ 12ˢ. It also follows that the tables of Ptolemy and of al-Battānī, as regards the equation of time, cannot be compared directly with each other; for in the one the equation is nothing when in the other it is greatest. Had al-Battānī built his table on the elements that Ptolemy used, the sum of the two equations would always be 33ᵐ 30ˢ; but this sum is always slightly different, since al-Battānī's equation rests on somewhat different elements (another obliquity of the ecliptic, another eccentricity of the solar orbit, another length of the tropical year). — Schiaparelli.]

La première est celle de Ptolémée. Théon nous l’a expliquée de façon à ne rien laisser à désirer. La seconde est celle des auteurs des Tables qui commencent par le point de l’écliptique où l’équation est nulle. Suivant la troisième, on commence par le point où l’équation est soustractive (et sans doute la plus grande); dans la quatrième, on commence la Table au point où l’équation est additive; enfin, dans la cinquième, on commence au point équinoxial du printemps. Toutes ces méthodes au fond n’en sont qu’une» («He [Ibn Yūnus] counts five different methods of computing the equation of time. The first is Ptolemy's; Theon has explained it to us so as to leave nothing to be desired. The second is that of the authors of the tables that begin with the point of the ecliptic where the equation is nothing. By the third, one begins with the point where the equation is subtractive (and doubtless greatest); in the fourth, the table is begun at the point where the equation is additive; lastly, in the fifth, one begins at the vernal equinoctial point. All these methods are at bottom only one»)[^n].

[«*The given mean place of the sun*» here means the initial mean place; «*the other mean place*», the mean place that the sun has reached at that time. Plainly, as in the *Almagest*, the problem is to correct a given interval of true time so as to know the interval of mean time corresponding to it. — Schiaparelli.]
@p 0311b044..0311b045 + 0312b001..0312b011
The rules are set out not very clearly both by Ptolemy, *Almag.* III, 8 (ed. Halma vol. I, pp. 207–210), and by al-Battānī; far better in the commentary of Theon, which Delambre reports in his notes on that passage of the Almagest (ed. Halma, vol. I, p. 28 of the notes). They can be described briefly in this way. Let a given interval of true time between *t* and *t′* be converted into an interval of mean time. For each of the times *t* and *t′* let the mean and the true longitudes be derived from the tables of the sun; the difference between the two mean longitudes will be the arc of the ecliptic run in mean motion, the difference between the two true longitudes the arc described in true motion. Let the intervals of right ascension that correspond to those two arcs of the ecliptic be taken; there will come out the arcs of the equator that measure the mean time and the true time between *t* and *t′*. The difference of these two arcs of the equator, turned into parts of an equal hour, will be the correction of true time by which mean time is found, that is, the equation of time; it will be added to the unequal, or true, time when the equatorial arc of the latter is shorter than the mean one, or subtracted from the unequal time when its arc is longer than the mean one. The given interval will come out expressed in mean, or equinoctial, days-and-nights.
@p 0312b012..0312b019
«Suivant les modernes», says Delambre, loc. cit., «l’*équation* du temps est l’excès de la longitude moyenne sur *l’ascension droite vraie*, et cet excès converti en temps à raison de 15° par heure, est l’*équation* du temps, qui sert à convertir le temps vrai en temps moyen. Ce procédé est au fond le même que celui de Ptolémée, à la réserve que le précepte de Ptolémée donne la différence de deux *équations* du temps, au lieu que la règle moderne ne donne qu’une seule *équation*. Avec nos tables d’*équation du temps* nous corrigerions séparément les deux temps donnés, et nous prendrions la différence des deux temps corrigés, qui seroit l’intervalle exprimé en temps moyens» («According to the moderns, the equation of time is the excess of the mean longitude over the true right ascension, and this excess, turned into time at 15° to the hour, is the equation of time, which serves to convert true time into mean time. This procedure is at bottom the same as Ptolemy's, except that Ptolemy's rule gives the difference of two equations of time, whereas the modern rule gives only one equation. With our tables of the equation of time we would correct the two given times separately and take the difference of the two corrected times, which would be the interval expressed in mean time»).
