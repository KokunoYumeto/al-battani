@p 0641b005..0641b012
I think that al-Battānī's tables of the kings (like the geographical ones) came from a Greek archetype through some *Syro-Christian* version. A Syriac origin is betrayed by «*Bukhtanaṣṣar the third*, who is the conqueror of Jerusalem», for Ναβοκολάσσαρος[^641.1]; by *Belshaṣṣar*[^641.2] for Νηρικασολάσσαρος[^641.3]; *Darius the Mede* for Ναβονάδιος; *Akhashwīros* for Ξέρξης; *Arnabā* for (Ptolemy) Λάγος; by the corruption of the name of *Alexander* [Severus] *son of Mammaea*, of which I shall speak on p. 194; and finally by the word *al-ḥanīf*, added to Julian, by which Παραβάτης, «apostate», is rendered. — That *Syriac* writer seems to have flourished under Leo the Isaurian (717–741), since the last of the kings is Theodosius III, who died in 717[^n].
@n Theodosius III abdicated in 717, when Leo the Isaurian took the throne; it was his reign, not his life, that ended then.

II, p. 652 (where the editor wrongly accepted بلتشصر); through the corruption of the copyists it is بلتنصر in Ibn Khaldūn, Būlāq edition 1284 H, vol. II, p. 117. In Ibn al-Athīr it is بلتاصر, in Eutychius (*Contextio gemmarum, sive Eutychii Patriarchae Alexandrini Annales ed. Edw. Pocockio*, Oxford 1659, vol. I, p. 257) بلتاسر; but perhaps in both ـتـ is corrupted from ـشـ. After the Syriac form, al-Bīrūnī, loc. cit., and Abū l-Faraj, ed. Ṣālḥānī, Beirut 1890, p. 78, have بلطشاصر.

@p 0641b013..0641b023
There are some small discrepancies from the Greek text. Ptolemy gives the reign of the first Artaxerxes 41 years, al-Battānī and al-Bīrūnī 43, so that the sum down to the death of Ochus is increased by two years. The reigns of Arogus, of Darius son of Arsakh and of Alexander of Macedon make 2 + 4 + 8 = 14 years in Ptolemy; 10 + 6 + 6 = 22 (that is, ى 10 for ب 2, و 6 for د 4, و 6 for ح 8) in al-Battānī; 2 + 6 + 8 = 16 in al-Bīrūnī; so the sum of the reigns down to the death of Alexander is 424 in Ptolemy's *Canon*, 434 in al-Battānī, 428 in al-Bīrūnī. Strangely, on p. 231 of the text (vol. II, p. 3 of the translation) and in the Spanish version the interval from Bukhtanaṣṣar to the death of Alexander is given as تكد, 424 years, which in the translation I thought should be read تلد, 434, on the authority of the table of the kings. — The error disappears in what follows, since a new count begins with Philip (Arrhidaeus), namely the era called after the death of Alexander (see vol. I, pp. 242–43).
@p 0641b024..0641b033
After that everything agrees well with *Ptolemy's Canon* down to the death of Antoninus; after him al-Battānī and al-Bīrūnī put Commodus with 32 years, putting together into one the reigns of Marcus Antoninus (19 years) and of Commodus (13). Likewise they fuse into one the reigns of Severus (18) and of Antoninus Caracalla (7); but the sums of the years are plainly not affected by any discrepancy. Again they differ from the *Canon* in the reigns of Philip, Decius and Gallus (6 + 1 + 3 instead of 7 + 1 + 2); but the sum is not changed. They give Diocletian 21 years instead of 20; and there are small discrepancies in the following years, which, however, gradually compensate one another, so that the sums of the years down to the death of Julian the pagan are equal in the Greek and in the Arabs. The Greek gives Constantine Pogonatus (Leontius in al-Battānī) 17 years instead of 16, but Philippicus 2 instead of 3; so the sum down to the death of Theodosius is 1040 in all of them[^641.4].
@f 641.4 0641nr011..0641nr013
Wrongly 1042 in Halma's edition; the error begins with the sum for Constantius, where the editor wrote 994 for 992.
@p 0642b001..0642b009
But although al-Battānī's order of the ***numbers*** agrees with the Greek and with al-Bīrūnī, there is a confusion in some ***names*** in the Escorial codex, which I did not wish to remove. For in al-Battānī the names (not the numbers) of the Second Interregnum and of Mesesimordacus, and of Theodosius the Great and Valens, are interchanged; moreover, after the two Constantines who follow Heraclius, the Greek and al-Bīrūnī give the names of the kings in this order: Constantine Pogonatus for Leontius, the later Justinian for Tiberius, Leontius for Justinian, Tiberius for Philippicus I, Justinian for Constantine, Philippicus, Anastasius, Theodosius. Besides, in the Escorial codex these names are so corrupt that doubt not seldom arises in reading them; so I would not dare to affirm that all these interchanges of names come from al-Battānī.

@p 0642b011..0642b025
*Darius the Mede*, i.e. Nabonadius, is in the text Darius *al-ādharī*, or «from the province of Ādharbayjān»; in the geographical table too, vol. II, p. 35, no. 69, this region is held to answer to Ptolemy's *Media*. In the Syriac archetype there was doubtless *mādāyā* ܡܕܝܐ, which al-Bīrūnī, *Chronology*, p. 89 of the text, 101 of the translation, has kept: «Darius (دريوس) *al-Mādāy* the first». In al-Ṭabarī, *Annales*, series I, vol. II, pp. 649 and 652, there occurs Darius (داريوش) *al-Mādhawī*, which in both places is said to be a relative name from an ancestor of his called Mādhī; in Eutychius, vol. I, p. 257, «Darius … *al-Mādī*, that is, of the progeny of Mādānī», داريوش بن امرير المادي اعني من ولد ماداني; in Abū l-Faraj, ed. Ṣālḥānī, pp. 78 and 80, Darius (داريوش) *al-Mādī*, and in his Syriac Chronicle *Mādāyā*. — From another source, a Persian one, al-Bīrūnī elsewhere, *Chronology*, p. 111 of the text, 115 of the translation, has *Dārā al-Māhī*, that is, from the region of Māh, which is the more recent Persian form of the very ancient name *Māda* (Media). — In the Arabic text I accepted داريوس, as the codex has it, which agrees very well with the Greek Δαρεῖος; but I hardly doubt that al-Battānī found داريوش *Dārīwash* in his source, as almost all the Arab historians have it, after the Syriac and Hebrew form *Dāryāwesh* (in the Persian cuneiform inscriptions *Dārayawaush*, in the Babylonian *Dāriyāmush*).

@p 0643b036..0643b042 + 0644b001..0644b006
*Alexander son of Mammaea*, Ἀλέξανδρος Μαμαίας, or Alexander Severus, is in the Escorial codex *Aleksandros Brnmā* (برنما), in the codices of al-Bīrūnī, *Chronology*, p. 94 of the text, *Aleksandros Bzymā* (بزيما), which shows a Syriac origin. For the first Arabic translator of this table took the Syriac ܒܰܪ ܡܳܡܳܐ *bar Māmā*, «son of Mammaea», for a single word, a surname of Alexander; so he wrote برمما *Barmamā*, which the copyists afterwards corrupted in various ways. — The genuine Syriac form of the name Μαμαία is ܡܡܐ *Māmā*, in Phoenician inscriptions ממה, in Palmyrene ממי (see Nöldeke, *Beiträge zur semitischen Sprachwissenschaft*, Strassburg 1904, p. 94); so Abū l-Faraj, ed. Ṣālḥānī, p. 126, very rightly took from Syriac sources «*Aleksandrūs*, whose mother's name was *Māmā*». — The Arab tradition that comes from the *Greek*, however, keeps the Greek genitive Μαμαίας, which it took for a surname of Alexander; so al-Masʿūdī, *Prairies* II, 306, *al-Iskandar Māmayās*; al-Masʿūdī, *al-Tanbīh*, p. 133, «*al-Ikṣandros* (الاكصندرس), and he is surnamed *Māmayās*», as Ibn al-Athīr too, Cairo edition, vol. I, p. 143 (wrongly printed 145), has in his second catalogue of the kings.[^n]
