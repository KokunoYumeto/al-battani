Nor are manuscript codices that give those small works infrequent, the author's name being corrupted in many ways: *Bereni*, *Bareni*, etc., as Steinschneider noted on Baldi, 456–57 (extr. 29–30); in a parchment codex of the National Library of Florence (*Conventi soppressi I, III, 28*), f. 103r–104r, «*Centiloquium Boetem*»[^37.2]. — According to a certain Paris codex, the small works were translated by John of Seville in the twelfth century.
@p 0037b023..0037b028
Baldi, Steinschneider (on Baldi, 458 = extr. 31) and Suter[^37.3] thought that *Bethem* or *Bethen* was corrupted from al-Battānī, and that the little book on the rising of the triplicities (Steinschneider), or all three (Suter), were probably excerpted from the book *on the ascensions of the signs*[^37.4]. To judge this conjecture we must examine those three small works at greater length. I give the text of the editions of 1493 and 1519; by the letter V I indicate the edition of 1507, by B both the Basel editions, by F the Florence codex (of the Centiloquium)[^37.5].
@p 0037b029..0037b031 + 0038b001..0038b005
*Centiloquium.* — It opens thus: «Incipit centiloquium Bethem[^37.6]. Hunc (V nunc) inchoabo librum de consuetudinibus in (F et) iudiciis (B iudicio) stellarum (V astrorum). 1. Scias quod quando planetae sunt retrogradi sunt ut vir infirmus, stupefactus, sollicitus. 2. Cadens vero est quasi mortuus cui non est motus» (here begins the centiloquium of Bethem. This book I shall begin on the customs in the judgements of the stars. 1. Know that when the planets are retrograde they are like a man who is sick, stunned, anxious. 2. And one that is cadent is like a dead man, who has no motion). There follow the other aphorisms of astrology, of which the last is: «100. Quando planeta infortuna erit (V infortunatus fuerit) in angulis receptus (V et receptus), dabit et applicabit quaerentis negocium: tamen accipiet ab eo quod dedit sibi: scientia receptionis est, quando fuerit planeta in domo sui (V sua), aut domo exaltationis suae salvus (F salutis) ab infortuna» (100. When a malefic planet is received in the angles, it will give and bring about the querent's business: yet it will take from him what it gave him: the knowledge of reception is when a planet is in its own house, or in the house of its exaltation, safe from misfortune).
@p 0038b006..0038b019
The *little book on the hours of the planets* begins thus: «De horis planetarum et primo de hora Saturni. Cum fuerit hora Saturni bonum est emere res gravis naturae. ut ferrum, stamnum, plumbum, et omnia metalla, et lapidem, et pannos nigros, et incipere hortos fodere, et aliquas fraudes excogitare contra inimicos.....» (on the hours of the planets, and first on the hour of Saturn. When it is the hour of Saturn it is good to buy things of a heavy nature, such as iron, tin, lead and all metals, and stone, and black cloths, and to begin digging gardens, and to devise some frauds against enemies.....). In the same way the hours of all seven planets, of which the last is the Moon, are reviewed; and when these are finished there follows: «*De ortu triplicitatum* et primo de ortu primae triplicitatis. Si fuerit in ortu Aries, Leo vel Sagittarius, quia ista signa sunt ignea, bonum est sub eorum ortu incipere ea quae pertinent ad ignem, sicut fornacem incendere, aurum, argentum, plumbum, stamnum, auricalcum colorare.....» (*on the rising of the triplicities*, and first on the rising of the first triplicity. If Aries, Leo or Sagittarius is rising, since those signs are fiery, it is good to begin under their rising the things that belong to fire, such as to light a furnace, to colour gold, silver, lead, tin, brass.....). When all four triplicities have been reviewed in this way, a very short appendix follows: «De hora Saturni et signo eius ascendente. Haec [sic] tantum diligentius est attendendum quod si fuerit hora Saturni, et signum Saturni ascendat, mirabile est facere omnia quae dicta sunt de horis Saturni: et idem intellige de omnibus aliis horis et signis, et si potes, concordes horas cum signis, quia optimum est. et quod diximus de Saturno, sic intellige de omnibus aliis planetis. Explicit de horis planetarum Bethem[^38.1]» (on the hour of Saturn and its sign ascending. This only must be attended to more carefully: that if it is the hour of Saturn and the sign of Saturn is ascending, it is wonderful to do all that has been said of the hours of Saturn; and understand the same of all the other hours and signs; and if you can, make the hours agree with the signs, for that is best. And what we have said of Saturn, so understand of all the other planets. Here ends Bethem's on the hours of the planets).
@p 0038b020..0038b028
There is no one who does not see that all this differs entirely from al-Battānī's other writings, those that are certain; nor can those propositions of judicial astrology have been excerpted from the book of the ascensions of the signs between the quarters of the heaven, which deals with directing the aphetae, nor can they be deemed consonant with al-Battānī's character and mind as it appears from elsewhere. Moreover Johannes Stöffler (d. 1531), a judge expert in these matters, thinks thus of the little book: «Many astrologers, most learned men, followed the Babylonians (*Babilonios* [sic]): and especially Bethen, who composed a treatise of his own on the hours of the planets, whose beginning is: *Cum fuerit hora Saturni* etc.»[^38.2]. So the little book does not seem to agree with the astrological principles of Ptolemy, which, however, we find that al-Battānī followed[^38.3].
@p 0038b029..0038b033
So there is no reason to attribute those small works to our astronomer except the likeness of the author's name. That this is a most deceptive argument no one who knows how altogether incredibly Arabic words were disfigured by our people of the Middle Ages will dare to deny. But I think the most certain indication that Bethem or Bethen has nothing to do with al-Battānī is to be taken from the name itself, as I shall show in what follows.
@p 0038b034..0038b035 + 0039b001..0039b025 + 0040b001..0040b003
There are two kinds of Arabic proper names: one always provided with the article *al*[^38.4], the other constantly without the article. The proper names of one kind can never be transferred to the other. And it was a most firm rule for those who in the Middle Ages translated Arabic books into Hebrew, Latin and Spanish, and therefore also for our John of Seville, to keep the article of proper names whenever it was found in the Arabic; most certain testimony to this are *Albategnius, Albenait, Alcabitius, Alchindus, Alcharsi, Alchasibi, Alchorismus, Alfraganus, Algazelus, Alhazen, Alkasem, Almansor, Almeon, Alpetragius, Alpharabius, Anaritius, Asaphar, Azarchel* or *Arzachel, Azophi*, and many other names of men[^39.1]. Indeed, names compounded with the word *Abū* (i.e. father) were sometimes wrongly provided with the article by the translators, such as *Albohali, Albohazen, Albubather, Albucasis, Albumasar*. Nor did they act otherwise in *geographical* names, of which very abundant examples are given by John of Seville in his translations of Alfraganus[^39.2] and of Alcabitius[^39.3], by Plato of Tivoli in his translations of the Tetrabiblos[^39.4] and of al-Battānī[^39.5], by the translators of the astrology of Ibn Abī l-Rijāl[^39.6], and by others. Likewise in the names of the *stars*, which it would take too long to list now[^39.7]. Lastly, in the very *technical terms* of the sciences the Latin and Spanish translators kept the article, although in Arabic it is put in or left out in them as the occasion requires, as happens in our languages: *Alchata, alcoranum, regula alchatain, alhigera, alkali, alkekengi, alchimia, alambicus, alchermes, alkohol, algebra*, etc., and, as regards astronomy and astrology, *almagesti* or *almagestum, almanach, almanar, almuri, alhidada, alcora, alchitoth, alferath, almehuar, asaphaea, almucantarat, azimuth*[^39.8], *almunchariff, alchinchirefet, attacium* (for *attacuim*), *athazir, alcobol, almenen, alichorad, alfazim, alhylech, alcochoden, algebugthar, azemena*, besides very many others scattered in the books of astrology and medicine. They translated so slavishly that they not rarely put in or left out the Arabic article according to the rules of Arabic grammar; besides what I said in n. 8, two examples from Alcabitius as translated by John of Seville may suffice: diff. IV, ch. 10 (ed. 1485, f. dd5v–dd6r): «et ex hoc *amanbaharat* [corr. *anaubaharat* = *an-nawbahārāt*] que sunt novenarie» (and from this the *amanbaharat* [read *anaubaharat* = *an-nawbahārāt*], which are the novenaries), but afterwards: «eritque *nauhahar* i. novenarium 19. gradus Aquarii Iovis» (and the *nauhahar*, i.e. the novenary, of the 19th degree of Aquarius will be Jupiter's); likewise diff. IV, ch. 11 (f. dd6r): «et ex hoc *adorogen* [*ad-darījān*].....» (and from this the *adorogen* [*ad-darījān*].....)[^n], but below: «erit eius *dorongen* Sol.....» (its *dorongen* will be the Sun.....)[^40.1]. For the same reason, in the translation of the *Commentary* of Anaritius *on Euclid*, ed. Curtze, p. 7: «sicut *meguar*» (i.e. like an axis, *miḥwar*), because it is indeterminate in the text.
@n The print has *ad-darījan*; Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part II, p. XVI) correct it to *ad-darījān*.
@p 0040b004..0040b007
Therefore I cannot believe that the translators of the Middle Ages, and especially John of Seville, turned al-Battānī into *Bethem* without the article; so the name itself confirms what I had deduced before from the very matter of the small works, namely that those three little books are by no means to be counted among al-Battānī's writings.
@p 0040b008..0040b010
That this *Bethem*, *Bethen*, *Bereni*, etc. is the same man as *Beleni*, *Belini* (i.e. Apollonius of Tyana), to whom astrological and magical little books translated from the Arabic into Latin are attributed[^40.2], I do not venture to affirm; indeed I think it hardly likely.[^n]

@f 40.1 0040nl001..0040nl016
M. Curtze, in the preface to Anaritii *in decem libros priores Elementorum Euclidis Commentarii, ex interpretatione Gherardi Cremonensis*, Leipzig 1899, pp. xiii–xiv, notes: «In his translation Gerard almost never uses the superlative, but only the comparative with the sense of the superlative. For example, on p. 5, l. 22 we read *brevior dimensio* for *brevissima dimensio*, and ibid. 1, 34 *cum breviori linea* for *cum brevissima linea*. Almost all the mathematical writers of the Middle Ages use the same manner, and whoever devotes himself to their study must be mindful of this usage». This perhaps comes from the fact that the Arabs denote both the comparative and the superlative by the same form of the adjective, *afʿal*; but with them the construction of the phrase does not produce the confusion that the Latin translators cannot avoid.
