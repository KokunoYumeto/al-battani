On pp. 304–305: the conversion of the eras into one another
@p 0765b026..0765b030
What these eras are is gathered with the greatest certainty from the first table. Let the epoch of the hijra be 15 July AD 622, according to the usage of the Arab astronomers and of the preceding tables; that is, the 196th day of the year 622 begun, or 621 complete Julian years and 196 days after the birth of Christ. The title of the Roman column[^765.1] gives for the beginning of the Roman era an interval of 932 Julian years, 9 months, 17 days before the hijra; therefore
@t spec=lrl 0765b031..0765b033
 | 932 | complete Julian years + 287 days
less | 621 | complete Julian years + 196 days
---
 | 311 | complete Julian years + 91 days before the birth of Christ.
@p 0765b034..0765b035
So the epoch of the era is 1 October 312 BC; it is the era of *Dhū l-Qarnayn* or *of the Seleucids*, according to the *common* usage.
@p 0766b001..0766b002
The title of the column puts between the era of the Copts and the era of the hijra an interval of 337 Julian years and 319 days. Therefore
@t spec=lrl 0766b003..0766b005
epoch of the hijra | 621 | complete years + 196 days after the birth of Christ
epoch of the era | 337 | complete years + 319 days before the hijra
---
 | 283 | complete years + 242 days after the birth of Christ.
@p 0766b006..0766b007
So the epoch of the Coptic era, since the year AD 284 is a leap year, falls on Friday, 29 August AD 284, which is the epoch of the era of the *Martyrs* or *of Diocletian*.
@p 0766b008..0766b010
Between the era of the hijra and the Persian era the table sets 9 *vague* years, 11 months, 9 days, or 3624 days, which are the known interval between the hijra of the Arab astronomers and the era of *Yazdajird* (whose epoch is Tuesday, 16 June AD 632). Cf. vol. I, p. 69.
@p 0766b011
The *fractions* are sixtieths of a day; therefore 45′ = ¾ of a day.[^n]
@n The print has 8/4.
@p 0766b012..0766b017
In this table the Roman, Coptic and Persian *months* are always taken to have *thirty* days. So the Roman and the Coptic year consist of 12 months, 5 days and ¼ (or 15′) of a day; the Persian year of 12 months and 5 days. Hence one understands the discrepancy of − 5 days or − 5¼ in the interval between two successive lines whenever one passes from a number of months 11 or 12 + *x* days to months 1 or 0 with the increase of a whole year; and one also understands why under 60 in the Roman column there stand (990ᵃ) 12ᵐ 4ᵈ 30′, and under 600 in the Coptic column (919ᵃ) 12ᵐ 4ᵈ 15′.
