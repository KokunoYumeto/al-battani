In Ptolemy's system the motions in longitude of the *superior planets* and of *Venus* are explained as follows. Each of these four planets has a circle *eccentric* (ὁ ἔκκεντρος) with respect to the centre of the earth or of the ecliptic, on whose periphery the centre of the *epicycle* moves; the Arabs, and those of our writers who followed them, mostly call it the *deferent* (*al-ḥāmil*)[^687.5]. The centre of the epicycle goes round the circumference of the deferent in the order of the signs (from west to east), but with a motion that is unequal both with respect to the centre M of the deferent and with respect to the centre E of the earth; it moves uniformly, however, about a point E′, which is as far from the centre M of the deferent as the centre E of the earth is on the opposite side. This imaginary circle, of the same diameter as the deferent, is called by Ptolemy ἔκκεντρος τῆς ὁμαλῆς κινήσεως (or παρόδου), «the eccentric of uniform motion», by the Arabs *al-muʿaddil li-l-masīr*, «that which equalizes the motion», and by our writers the *equant*; it is regarded as a mere circle, not as a sphere or orb. The centre of the epicycle of *Venus* completes the circuit of the deferent in a sidereal year of the sun; that of the *three superior planets* in other intervals of time. So *Venus* is always in mean conjunction with the sun. — The *superior planets* complete the circumference of the epicycle in one sidereal year[^688.1], and once a year (standing in the true apogee of the epicycle) they are in mean conjunction with the sun, once (in the true perigee of the epicycle) in mean opposition. *Venus*, however, completes its epicycle with its mean motion in 583 days, 22 hours and 15 minutes, or about 19 solar months.

All the planets move in the upper half of the epicycle in the order of the signs (from west to east), in the lower half the contrary way. The motion of the three *superior planets* on the epicycle equals the excess of the mean motion of the sun in longitude over their own mean motions of the centre of the epicycle.
@p 0688b007..0688b008 + 0688b009..0688b016
The line KG of the apsides of the eccentric keeps a constant direction with respect to the fixed stars; in *Venus* and *Mars* it coincides with the line of the greatest inclination of the plane of the eccentric to the plane of the ecliptic, in *Jupiter* it deviates from this line by 20° to the west, in *Saturn* by 50° to the east. The apogees of the eccentrics of *Saturn*, *Jupiter* and *Mars* always incline to the north of the ecliptic, the perigees always to the south; but the apogee and the perigee of *Venus* undergo every change, taking now a northern, now a southern latitude, and sometimes lying in the plane of the ecliptic. — The same line KG moves about the centre E of the earth with the motion of the precession, or of the fixed stars, carrying the points E′ and M with it.

Let ABD be the ecliptic, GHK the deferent, H the centre of the epicycle at a given moment, and G the apogee of the eccentric (whose longitude on the ecliptic is A). According to its mean motion about the centre E′ of the equant, the centre of the epicycle will hold the longitude Z on the ecliptic; but according to its true motion, as seen from the centre E of the earth, it will be at the longitude X. The arc ZX, the difference between the mean and the true place, is called by Ptolemy ἡ ζωδιακὴ ἀνωμαλία, «the zodiacal inequality», and by al-Battānī and the Arabs «*the equation of the anomaly and the centre*»[^689.1]; hence the name *equation of the centre*, which astronomers use even today. One column would suffice to give the equation of the anomaly if the centre of the epicycle always kept the same distance from the centre of the earth. Since this does not happen, to equate the motions without too great a number of tables one will have to give the variations that fall to the equations because of the different distances by proportional minutes. Since in these five planets the variation from the perigee to the apogee is so great that proportional minutes, if set simply as was done for the moon, would produce sensible errors, Ptolemy, whom al-Battānī follows, put in col. VI the equation of the anomaly for the mean distance of the centre of the epicycle from the earth; in V the amount to be taken from the sixth so that the equation for the greatest distance of the epicycle results; in VII the excess of the equation for the least distance over the equation for the mean distance; and finally in VIII the proportional minutes for finding the equations at the intermediate distances. These proportional minutes have been computed for the greatest equations, namely those that occur at the tangent drawn from the centre of the earth to the circumference of the epicycle; for the corrections of the equations falling at the other points of the epicycle keep sensibly the same proportion (see *Almag.* XI, 10, ed. The matter is more involved in *Mercury*, in which the centre M of the deferent[^692.1] does not hold the middle place between the centre E′ of the equant and the centre E of the earth, but goes round a small circle (called by the Arabs *al-mudīr*, «the revolving one»), whose centre F is as far from M and from E′ as the centre of the equant is from the centre of the earth. So at times the centre of the deferent falls on the centre of the equant; then the point of the apogee of the deferent holds the perigee of the equant, and both circles coincide[^692.2]. The line of the apsides of the equant moves about E with the motion of the precession, as in the other planets. The centre of the epicycle completes the circumference of the deferent in the order of the signs in one sidereal year of the sun; and in the same time, against the order of the signs (from east to west), the centre of the deferent travels round the revolving small circle, and the line of the apsides of the deferent describes a whole circle; so that, because of the sum of these equal but contrary motions, the centre of the epicycle holds the apogee of the eccentric (the deferent) twice in a sidereal year, and twice the perigee. For the same reason the angular distance of the apogee of the equant from the apogee of the deferent is always as great as its distance, on the opposite side, from the centre of the epicycle. — The mean motion of the centre of the epicycle equals the mean motion of the sun in longitude; Mercury, however, completes its epicycle with its mean motion in about 115 days, 21 hours and 5 minutes.
