@p 0046b009..0046b015 + 0047b001..0047b015
But at the beginning, among us, of that very great renewal of the arts and of studies which closes the Middle Ages and is the beginning of the modern age, the great weight of al-Battānī's book became apparent, perhaps even more than it had before. Regiomontanus (6 June 1436 – 6 July 1476), the prince of the mathematicians and astronomers of that age, gave the greatest care to gaining an exact knowledge of al-Battānī's work, of which several traces survive. Plato of Tivoli's translation first came out in print at Nuremberg in 1537, from a copy that Regiomontanus had furnished here and there with notes in his own hand[^46.5]. In his letters, to refute the doctrines of the Alphonsine Tables on the motion of the eighth sphere, or the trepidation, he relies on his own observations and on those of Ptolemy and al-Battānī[^47.1]; and in the *Epitome of the Almagest*[^47.2] he often adduces the authority of our astronomer, as in book III, prop. 2, pp. 47–48, on the length of the tropical year; III, 13, pp. 56–57, on the length of the four parts of the year, the eccentricity of the solar orbit and the greatest equation of the Sun; IV, 12, p. 78, on the corrections that al-Battānī had made to Ptolemy's mean lunar motions; V, 21, pp. 100–103, on the eclipses, the distances and the diameters of the Sun; V, 35, p. 107, reporting al-Battānī's precepts (p. 80, l. 37 to 81, l. 3) for deriving the parallaxes of altitude from the tables[^47.3]; VI, 7, p. 118, on the limits of the solar eclipses, and VI, 8, p. 119, on the limits of the eclipses of the Moon (passages that you may see in my notes on vol. II, p. 32 of the tables); VII, 6, p. 145, on the motion of the fixed stars and the doctrine of trepidation; and lastly IX, 1, 163. — But what profit Regiomontanus took from our astronomer appears also from other places in his works in which the name of al-Battānī is not mentioned. I have reported two examples in my notes, pp. 193 and 322; but of far greater weight is what von Braunmühl, *Vorlesungen über die Gesch. der Trigonometrie*, vol. I, pp. 130–31, was the first to notice, namely that Regiomontanus[^47.4] learned from al-Battānī the use of *versed sines* and of the *orthographic projection* in the problems of spherical trigonometry[^47.5].
@p 0047b016..0047b020
The other astronomers of the same and of a later age valued al-Battānī's observations highly for correcting the tables of the celestial motions, and very often reported them in their writings. Nicholas of Cusa (1401–64) adduces them in the *Reparatio calendarii* and in the *Correctio tabularum Alphonsi*[^47.6]; and so do Copernicus (1473–1543), who calls our author «Mahometus Aractensis», Tycho Brahe[^47.7] and Riccioli (1598–1671).

C. de Simoni, *Intorno ad Andalò di Negro*, p. 322, thinks that the table of the culminations and declinations of the chief fixed stars placed by Andalò (fourteenth century) at the end of his treatise on the astrolabe was taken from al-Battānī, for the reason that, together with other chief stars (such as α Cor. borealis), α Hydrae or Alphard is left out, «della quale ultima omissione in specie Albategnio viene accusato dall'Arabo es-Sufi» (for which last omission in particular Albategnius is accused by the Arab es-Sufi). But al-Ṣūfī (whose words I adduced on p. 295) by no means says that al-Battānī left out that star; in fact α Hydrae is given in al-Battānī's table, vol. II, p. 185, no. 66.
