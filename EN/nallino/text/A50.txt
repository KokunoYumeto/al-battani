*The distances of the planets from the earth* «according to what the more recent wise men established with long labour *after* Ptolemy». The codex, as I noted in its place, has «before», which goes badly with the words «more recent wise men». The doubt is easily removed, however, if we consider that the foundations of all these computations are the eccentricities and the sizes of the epicycles determined by Ptolemy, as will be plain from note *b*.
@p 0375b017..0375b023
This theory of the distances rests on the principle that the spheres of the seven planets are contiguous and separated from one another by no interval; so the least distance of a superior planet equals the greatest distance of the planet immediately below it. This principle was accepted by all the Arab astronomers known to me; in the fifteenth century it is still found in the Persian writer ʿAlī Qūshjī (in Delambre p. 198). A philosophical discussion is in the epistles on the various sciences that men called *Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*, «the faithful brethren», wrote in the tenth century in the city of al-Baṣra, whose doctrine came as much from Aristotle as from the Neopythagoreans and the Neoplatonists[^375.2].

@p 0375b024..0375b032
Before al-Battānī, al-Farghānī already relates this, ch. XXI, p. 80 of Golius's edition: «Ptolemy indeed set out in his book how great the distance of the sun and the moon alone is; nor have we found anything handed down by him about the distances of the other stars, except that he taught how far the centres of the orbs are from the centre of the earth, and how great the orbs of the epicycles are. — If, then, we suppose that the farthest distance of the moon, with respect to both orbs together (the eccentric and the epicycle), is the same as the nearest distance of Mercury, and use the same method as we have said, proceeding similarly in Mercury and Venus, we shall find that the greatest distance of Venus in both orbs together equals the least distance of the sun, which Ptolemy set out. @p 0376b017..0376b025
The principle on which this theory of the distances rests some later Arabs attribute to Ptolemy himself. So in the thirteenth century al-Qazwīnī, *Cosmography*, Cairo 1311 H (in the margin of al-Damīrī), vol. I, p. 26, treatise I: «Ptolemy recorded the thickness[^376.10] of the celestial spheres, and the size of the body, of the circumference and of the diameters of each planet. Do not think this very difficult; for it is difficult only for those who do not know the science of geometry. Whoever has finished the second book of Euclid[^n] will, if he is perspicacious, find this easily». And in the following chapters (II–IX) of the same treatise I he gives how many miles the thickness of the sphere of each of the seven planets comprises, «according to the opinion of Ptolemy, the author of the observations, who derived it by geometrical demonstrations».
@n Nallino's Addenda et emendanda (Part II, p. XXV) add that the passage stands with the same readings in Wüstenfeld's edition, vol. Now let us question the Greeks. Schiaparelli supplies me with a passage of Proclus' *Hypotyposes*[^378.2], where this method of computing the distances is mentioned as regards the moon, Mercury, Venus and the sun. For he says that «some (τίνες), supposing that there is no empty space in the world and that all the intervals are filled with intermediate spheres», computed the distances of those planets. Taking the greatest distance of the moon as 64ʳ 10′ earth radii and the least of the sun as 1160ʳ [from the Almagest], «they found that the distance of the apogee of the epicycle of Mercury from the centre of the zodiac [or of the earth], as demonstrated [by Ptolemy], is to the distance of the perigee as 91ʳ 30′ to 34ʳ 4′. Then, making 34ʳ 4′ to 91ʳ 30′ as the greatest distance of the moon (64ʳ 10′) to a certain fourth term, they found from this proportion 277ʳ 33′, which is the apogee distance of Mercury … Likewise, taking the perigee distance of Venus from the centre of the zodiac to be to the apogee distance as 15ʳ 35′ to 204ʳ 25′, and the same ratio to hold between the apogee distance of Mercury (277ʳ 33′) and another fourth term, they found this to be 1150ʳ, which is nearly the perigee distance of the sun (1160)»[^n].
@n With these numbers 64ʳ 10′ × 91ʳ 30′ : 34ʳ 4′ is 172ʳ 21′, not 277ʳ 33′; and Ptolemy's greatest distance of Venus is 104ᵖ 25′ (60ᵖ + 1ᵖ 15′ + 43ᵖ 10′), not 204ᵖ 25′. With 172ʳ 21′ and 104ʳ 25′ the last term comes out 1154ʳ 49′, the «1150ʳ» given.

These computations, whose elements are taken from the *Almagest*, could easily be attributed by the Arabs to Ptolemy himself. But Schiaparelli points out to me that the Greeks had already done this. For Simplicius[^378.3], who is later than Proclus, relates: «That Mercury stands below Venus is shown by the observations in which Mercury is found running below Venus. It is also demonstrated by the ratio of the distances of their apogees and perigees; for the greatest distance of Venus is shown to be nearly the distance of the sun, so that Venus is next to the sun; likewise the greatest of Mercury nearly equal to the least of Venus, and the greatest of the moon nearly equal to the least of Mercury. For these things are demonstrated in Ptolemy's *Syntaxis* [= the Almagest], the ratio being derived from the eccentricity of these planets with respect to the centre of the earth»[^378.4].

Simplicius had devoted the greatest effort to the study of the books of Proclus, whom he calls «the master of my master». Perhaps, as Schiaparelli rightly conjectures, he knew the doctrines of the Almagest not from the original text, which is very hard to understand, but from some compendium, such as the Hypotyposes («first lines, or rudiments» of the motions of the planets) are; so he could easily think that everything Proclus relates in them, including the computation of the distances of the planets, came from Ptolemy. — Things being so, I think it not improbable that the *Kitāb al-manshūrāt*, which I find mentioned only in those two places of al-Bīrūnī, means «the Book of things made public [on astronomy]», which would be a version of the Greek name Ὑποτυπώσεις; that it is therefore not Ptolemy's but Proclus'; and that some of the Arabs, lastly, fell into the same error into which Simplicius had already fallen[^n].

[I. *The diameters of the planets with respect to the earth.* They are computed in this way. Let Δ be the apparent angular diameter of the sun, *R* its mean distance; the linear diameter of the sun will be *R*Δ. Likewise let δ be the apparent angular diameter of the planet, *r* its mean distance; *r*δ will be the linear diameter of the planet. So
\[ \frac{\text{diameter of the planet}}{\text{diameter of the sun}} = \frac{r\delta}{R\Delta} = \frac{r}{R} \times \frac{\delta}{\Delta}\,. \]
The diameter of the sun comprises 5 1/2 diameters of the earth; so
\[ \frac{\text{diameter of the planet}}{\text{diameter of the earth}} = 5\tfrac{1}{2} \times \frac{r}{R} \times \frac{\delta}{\Delta} = r \times \frac{\delta}{\Delta} \times \frac{1}{\left(\dfrac{R}{5\frac{1}{2}}\right)}\,. \]
But $R = 1108$, $\frac{R}{5\frac{1}{2}} = 201\tfrac{5}{11}$, so
\[ \frac{\text{diameter of the planet}}{\text{diameter of the earth}} = r \times \frac{\delta}{\Delta} \times \frac{1}{201\frac{5}{11}}\,, \]
which is the rule of al-Battānī, or rather of those who had made these computations before him. Plainly, when he speaks of the observed ratio between the diameters of the planets and the diameter of the sun, he always means the ratio of the *apparent diameters*, not of the true ones.

To these very strong arguments, which most certainly show that this passage (ll. 20–30) is interpolated, another sign can be added. In this one passage of the whole of al-Battānī's work Saturn is called not *Zuḥal* but *al-Muqātil*, «the fighter»; and Mercury not *ʿUṭārid* but *al-Kātib*, «the scribe» or «the writer», a name that occurs only in appendix D (p. 221, l. 15 of the text, p. 147, l. 30 of the translation), which we shall show to be beyond doubt spurious. They were not unknown, on the other hand, to the Arabs who lived in north-west Africa and in Spain; and in fact Dozy, *Supplément aux dictionnaires arabes*, Leiden 1881, vol. So the man who used these names must have been an Arab of the West; and of the West likewise was the man who made the copy from which the Escorial codex was later transcribed, as plainly appears from the additions at the end of the geographical tables and in the headings of the tables of the ascensions of the signs in the city of al-Raqqa. The same African or Spanish glossator also added at the end of the work (ff. 241v–244r) tables taken from the book of Maslama al-Majrīṭī, who had lived in Cordoba in the second half of the tenth century and had made an abridgement of al-Battānī's tables.
