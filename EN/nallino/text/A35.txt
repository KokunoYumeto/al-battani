@p 0335b032
*The computation of the twelve astrological houses.*
@p 0335b033..0335b038
What Delambre has on pp. 45–48 does not serve to explain this chapter, for the division of the houses that Delambre chose is not the one that al-Battānī means. I shall first set out briefly what these houses are, following above all V. Nabod, *Enarratio elementorum astrologiae, in qua praeter Alcabicii, qui Arabum doctrinam in compendio prodidit, expositionem atque cum Ptolemaei principiis collationem, de verae artis praeceptorum origine et usu satis disseritur*, Cologne 1560, pp. 116–122[^335.1].
@f 335.1 0335nl001..0335nr002
It is a long commentary, but without mathematical proofs, on the very well-known little book of Alcabitius (ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Qabīṣī), who flourished in the tenth century; Suter, pp. 60–61, no. 132.
@p 0336b001..0336b015
Astrologers have various methods by which the whole heaven is divided in various ways into twelve parts, called *houses*[^336.1]; all, however, agree in this, that six great circles, cutting one another in two opposite places and having a common diameter that runs from south to north, are thought to divide the whole heaven into twelve parts. But the ends of the diameter, at which those six circles cut one another, different astrologers place differently. Alcabitius and those who wrote on the division of the celestial houses before Campanus (thirteenth century) held, as Nabod relates, that these two points are the poles of the equator. Let there be two great circles, the one the meridian, the other the circle that passes through the point of the ecliptic lying on the horizon and cuts the equator at right angles. The *equinoctial circle* then appears divided into four parts, of which each two opposite ones are equal. Let all these four parts be cut into three equal parts, and through the points of section and the poles of the equator let four circles be drawn (thus four *circles of declination*), which with the two former will make the twelve houses[^336.2]. In this way the ecliptic is divided into twelve parts, of which only the opposite ones are equal; only once a day will the ecliptic appear divided into four equal parts, namely at the moment of time at which the beginnings of Aries and Libra fall on the horizon.

Others put the ends of the common diameter at the points of intersection of the horizon and the meridian; the other four great circles are therefore those that are called *circles of position*. But on the method of drawing these circles they disagree, as Nabod relates[^336.3]:
@f 336.3 0336nl006..0336nl009
Regiomontanus sets out the first three methods at length (Iohannes de Monteregio, *Tabulae directionum profectionumque*, Venice 1485, problems XIV–XVI).
@p 0336b019..0336b022
*a*) Campanus and Gazulus[^336.4] take the *vertical* circle that passes through the points of rising and setting and is divided by the meridian and the horizon into four equal quadrants; they divide each quadrant of the vertical into three equal sections and draw the *circles of position* through the points of section. So the twelve houses are twelve equal lunes.
@f 336.4 0336nl010..0336nl011
Iohannes Gazulus of Ragusa lived at the beginning of the fifteenth century.
@p 0336b023..0336b026
*b*) Regiomontanus, in problem XIV of the *Tabulae directionum*, supposes the *equinoctial circle* divided into equal quadrants by the meridian and the oblique horizon; the quadrants are then cut into three equal parts of 30 degrees, and the *circles of position* are drawn through the points of section.
@p 0336b027..0336b034 + 0337b001..0337b002
Others, lastly, such as Johannes Schöner[^336.5], think that the *ecliptic* is divided into four quadrants by the horizon and by the great circle passing through both poles of the ecliptic and through the zenith; that the quadrants are cut into three equal parts; that through the points of section and the poles of the ecliptic four other *circles of latitude* are drawn; and that in this way the ecliptic and the celestial sphere are divided into twelve equal houses. «That all astrologers have adapted their judgements to these», says Nabod, «has been handed down by many. And that this is true cannot be denied, if it is understood of Firmicus, Albohali[^336.6], Albumasar[^336.7], Albubater[^336.8], Bonatus[^336.9] and the like». But that Ptolemy divided the *equator* into 12 equal parts (of 30 degrees, that is, of two equinoctial hours each), Nabod tries to deduce from various passages of the Tetrabiblos, against the opinion of other authors.

@p 0337b003..0337b013
These twelve points of the *ecliptic*, however they are determined, are called *cusps* or *beginnings of the houses*. Of them four are of the greatest moment; they have the names κέντρα, γωνίαι, *cardines geniturae*, *anguli* (the cardines of the nativity, the angles), and in Arabic *pegs* (*watad*, pl. *awtād*): they are 1) the *ascendant*, *the horoscoping part* (ἀνατολή, ἀνατέλλων, μοῖρα ὡροσκοποῦσα, ὡροσκόπος, *ṭāliʿ*), namely the point of the ecliptic on the eastern horizon at a given moment of time; 2) the *lower heaven* (ὑπόγειον, ἀντιμεσουράνημα, ἀντιμεσουρανῶν, ὑποχθόνιον κέντρον, *watad al-arḍ*, «the peg of the earth», *al-rābiʿ*, «the fourth»), namely the point of the ecliptic on the lower meridian; 3) the *setting* (δύσις, δύνων, *al-ghārib*, «the setting one»), or the point of the ecliptic on the western horizon; 4) the *middle heaven* (μεσουράνημα, μεσουρανῶν, *watad wasaṭ al-samāʾ*, «the peg of the middle of heaven», *al-ʿāshir*, «the tenth»), which is the place of intersection of the ecliptic and the upper meridian. In every division the order of the pegs and of the houses is always the same, namely in the succession of the signs.

@p 0337b015..0337b020
As appears from the very rules of the computation, al-Battānī's division of the houses is beyond doubt the one that Alcabitius and the Jew Ibn ʿEzrā (Avenezra, in Nabod, loc. cit.) teach; I find the same in Ḥabash ff. 102r and 108v, in Abū l-Wafāʾ[^337.1], in Ulugh Beg pp. 145–146 of the text (195–196 of the translation), and here and there in the *Libros del saber de astronomia* that King Alfonso X had compiled from Arabic sources. Ptolemy seems to have had it in mind in the *Tetrabiblos*; see Nabod, loc. cit., and what I shall cite below from the *Libros del saber*.

Let PSP′N be the meridian, NES the horizon (where N is the north point, S the south, E the east), BB′ the celestial equator, A♈X the ecliptic, ♈ the beginning of Aries, P and P′ the poles of the equator, A the point of the ecliptic that is then on the eastern horizon, namely the ascendant. — Before all else al-Battānī bids us take the temporal hours of the point A[^337.2] («*the hour-times of the degree of the ascendant in that clime*») and double them; which is the same as dividing the arc *a*B′ of the equator (the semidiurnal arc of the point A), the arc *a*B (the seminocturnal arc) and their opposites in the western hemisphere into three equal parts each. When circles of declination are drawn through the points of section, two unequal series of lunes arise; in one of them each lune comprises two diurnal temporal hours of the ascendant, in the other two nocturnal hours. The equator appears cut at *a*, 2, 3, 4 … 10, 11, 12; the ecliptic at A (the ascendant), II, III, IV (the peg of the earth) … X (the middle heaven), XI, XII, which are the beginnings or cusps of the houses. Once the arcs of the equator are known, the tables of right ascensions will at once give the corresponding arcs of the ecliptic.

@p 0338b006..0338b018
So in this partition of the houses not only do two unequal series of lunes arise, but an absurdity is also found, which Delambre, p. 502, speaking of Alcabitius, notes in these words[^338.1]: «Les six maisons dernières sont toujours diamétralement opposées aux six premières; il en résulte cependant une espèce d’absurdité. Le quart de l’équateur, entre le méridien et l’horizon occidental, se trouve divisé suivant les arcs nocturnes, quoiqu’il appartienne au jour; le quart entre l’horizon occidental et le méridien inférieur, est divisé suivant les heures du jour, quoiqu’il appartienne à la nuit. Au reste, le calcul de cette méthode est extrêmement simple, et c’est peut-être ce qui a fait passer sur l’absurdité que nous venons de remarquer» («The last six houses are always diametrically opposite the first six; a kind of absurdity results from it, however. The quarter of the equator between the meridian and the western horizon is divided according to the nocturnal arcs, although it belongs to the day; the quarter between the western horizon and the lower meridian is divided according to the hours of the day, although it belongs to the night. For the rest, the computation of this method is extremely simple, and that is perhaps what has made people pass over the absurdity we have just noted»).
