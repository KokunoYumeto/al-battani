@p 0395b012..0395b016 + 0395b017..0395b024
[«*The quadrature, which for all the stars …*». Let a planet P look at another planet in *quartile* aspect, that is, be 90 degrees of a great circle away from it. The two rays that P will cast on the ecliptic will then be the two arcs AP, PB of a great circle, each of which contains 90°. Let the planet be projected on the ecliptic at Q by the perpendicular arc PQ (which will plainly be the latitude of the planet); then the points A, B will be the poles of the arc PQ, so that also AQ = QB = 90°. The same would happen if the planet were at P′, and the arc P′A = 90°. Hence it is clear that «*the quadrature … is always the same, and never differs from 90°, whether the latitude of the planet is small or great*». Al-Battānī explains this further by comparing the great circle whose poles are A and B with the equator of the sphere, which always divides into equal quadrants all the circles passing through both poles; «*and this is plainly seen on the sphere, on whose poles the circles fall*».
@fig AB01-PDF0395-F01 AB01-PDF0395-body-F01
@p 0395b025..0395b029
Now let the planet R be in *sextile aspect* with another heavenly body; it will cast on the ecliptic the ray RA = 60°. Let the arc RS be drawn perpendicular to the ecliptic, which will be the latitude of the planet; the projection of the *sextile* aspect, or radiation, on the ecliptic will be the arc AS, plainly *less* than 60°. But if the planet R were in *trine* aspect, the projection of the arc RB = 120° would be the arc SB, always *greater* than 120°, since AS + SB must always be taken equal to 180°. — Schiaparelli.]
@p 0395b030..0395b032 + 0395b033..0395b037 + 0396b001..0396b003
Ibn Yūnus writes in his introduction (in Caussin, p. 73): «Likewise false is what Muḥammad ibn Jābir ibn Sinān al-Battānī says of the place on which the rays of the stars fall when the stars have latitude; for according to his principles a star with a latitude of 60 degrees has no sextile. But to avoid this the radiation must be understood in a way different from al-Battānī's; what Ibn Yūnus thought of the projection of the rays we do not know. Moreover it must be noticed that the astrologers use the radiation of the planets only, not that of the fixed stars (p. 131, l. 4); so the latitudes are always much smaller than 60°.

@p 0396b006..0396b008
I. — *Al-Battānī's computation.* The projection of the quartile radiation on the ecliptic is always 90°, as the author said before and as has been explained in note *c*. The projection of the trine radiation = 180° − the projection of the sextile; so it is enough to know the projection of the sextile ray.

The problem is like the first of chapter XXVI: given a star of latitude β and a star standing on the ecliptic, to compute the distance of the one from the other on the great circle passing through both. @p 0396b018..0396b036
According to the names set there (p. 39), we shall now have \[ \text{I side} = x,\qquad \text{II side} = 2\,\text{arc}\left(\sin\frac{x}{2}\times\frac{\cos\beta}{R}\right),\qquad \text{III side} = \text{crd}\,\beta, \] and the rule of that chapter will become \[ \text{crd I side}\times\text{crd II side} = \text{square of crd } 60^\circ - \text{square of III side}, \] that is[^396.2] \[ \text{crd}\,x\times\text{crd}\,x\,\frac{\cos\beta}{R} = \text{crd}^2\,60^\circ - \text{crd}^2\,\beta,\qquad \text{crd}^2\,x = R\,\frac{\text{crd}^2\,60^\circ - \text{crd}^2\,\beta}{\cos\beta}, \] whence finally \[ [[(α)]] \text{crd}\,x = \sqrt{R\,\frac{\text{crd}^2\,60 - \text{crd}^2\,\beta}{\cos\beta}}, \] which I think was al-Battānī's formula. And indeed, to restore this rule it is enough to leave out a little of al-Battānī's text, to add far less, and to keep *the rest untouched*; so, marking by some dots what is to be left out and is now left out, I do not doubt that al-Battānī wrote in this way: «Find in the tables of sines the cosine of the latitude [and divide it by the radius; what comes out will now be] the whole chord of the second side, which we have mentioned only in the chapter on the diagonals of quadrilaterals and in this very chapter in which we are. Keep this chord of the second side. Then find the chord of the latitude, which is the doubled sine of half the latitude. … Take the square of the chord of the latitude from 3600ᵖ, that is, from the square of the chord of the sextile aspect …; divide the remainder by [the kept chord] of the second side [and take the square root of the quotient]; … the chord sought will come out, which you convert into an arc as whole chords are converted into arcs, etc.».

@p 0398b009
II. — *On the projection of the rays before al-Battānī.*
@p 0398b010..0398b015
On the ἀκτινοβολία, or projection of the rays, among the Greeks Cl. Salmasius, *De annis climactericis et antiqua astrologia diatribae*, Leiden 1648, pp. 398–409, and A. Bouché-Leclercq, *L'astrologie grecque*, Paris 1899, pp. 247–51, have written. But both considered only its philosophical principles and its use in predictions; by what mathematical method, and on what circle of the heavens, the Greeks computed the rays they neglected to investigate.
@p 0398b016..0398b018
Many probably computed the radiations neglecting the latitude of the planet, simply adding 60°, 90°, 120° to the longitude of the planet, or subtracting them, for the different aspects; which we know al-Qabīṣī, or Alcabitius[^398.1], and the Jew Abraham ibn ʿEzra (Avenezra) also did.
@f 398.1 0398nl001..0398nl008
*Libellus ysagogicus Abdilazi, qui dicitur Alchabitius, ad magisterium iudiciorum astrorum, interpretatus a Ioanne Hispalensi*, Venice 1485, f. aa4, in the chapter on the aspects of the planets = *Praeclarum Alchabitii opus ad scrutanda stellarum magisteria isagogicum*, Venice 1521, f. 4r. — He lived in the fourth century H, the tenth AD; Suter, pp. 60–61, no. 132.
@p 0398b019..0398b029
Other Greek methods can be gathered from Arabic books. Ibn Hibintā, f. 33v, says that Hermes, in his book entitled العرض[^398.2], takes the radiations by «equal degrees» (on which see p. 28, note 3); but Dorotheus, as the same Ibn Hibintā and Muḥammad ibn ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān[^398.3] attest, counted the radiations not on the ecliptic but on the equator, taking the sextile ray to be the arc of the equator contained between the right ascension of the degree of the planet's longitude and the right ascension corresponding to (the longitude of the planet ± 60°). Hermes' method is also set out in the *Libros del saber de astronomia*[^398.4], from which these precepts are gathered: let the degree of the equator be found that falls on the circle of position (see p. 311, note 3) drawn through the given planet; let 60° (or 90°, or 120°) be added to it or subtracted from it; the degree of the ecliptic that falls on the circle of position drawn through that sum or remainder of the equatorial arc will be the degree of the left and of the right sextile (or quartile, or trine).

\text{arc of the ecliptic answering to } (\text{RA of the degree of the star} + 60^\circ) = \text{first radiation},
\text{arc of the ecliptic answering to } (\text{obl. asc. of the degree of the star} + 60^\circ) = \text{second radiation},
\text{radiation I} \pm \dfrac{(\text{radiation I} - \text{radiation II})}{6} \times \text{hours of distance} = \text{corrected radiation},

that is, the longitude of the place of the ecliptic on which the left sextile radiation falls. If the right one is sought, −60° is to be put for +60°; if the quartile or trine radiations are to be found, 90° or 120° are to be put for 60.

Plainly this «Ptolemaic» method is the same by which the astrologers determine the *directions* or *profections*, as I shall show in the notes on ch. LV; so they take the amount of the radiation on either side of the star's longitude to be the arc of the ecliptic contained between the degree of the planet's longitude and the circle of position[^400.3] drawn through the point of the equator that is 60° or 90° or 120° before or behind the right ascension of the planet. But in this way (which Ḥabash also has, f. 114r–v, though he does not mention Ptolemy) the star is supposed to have no latitude and to stand on the ecliptic. If the latitude is not thought negligible, the computation must be changed a little, as in the direction of stars that have latitude; that is, instead of the degree of the ecliptic that numbers the longitude of the star, the degree culminating together with the given star is to be used when right ascensions are computed, or the degree rising with the star when oblique ascensions are to be taken. But the astrologers seem mostly to have neglected this in the radiations; the error arising from it is often very small, since only planets, not fixed stars, are considered in this problem.
@f 400.3 0400nr008..0400nr010
The circles of position are the great circles that pass through the common intersections of the meridian and the horizon; their poles are on the prime vertical.

Not without reason do the Arabs call this computation of the radiations «the method of Ptolemy»; for in the *Tetrabiblos* III, 10 (= III, 15 of the Frankfurt version) I find this written on the directions: «When the significator has been found, two ways of taking [the directions] are to be used: the one in the order of the signs, *which is called the projection of the rays* (ἀκτινοβολία), namely when the significator is in the eastern places, from the midheaven down to the ascendant; the other both in the order of the signs and against it, according to the horary reckoning, namely when the significator is in the places declining from the midheaven». So I think that Bouché-Leclercq, p. 248, note 1, wrongly wrote: «La théorie de l’ἀκτινοβολία est probablement postérieure à Ptolémée» («The theory of the ἀκτινοβολία is probably later than Ptolemy»).
@p 0401b017..0401b027
Al-Zarqālī, in the passage already mentioned on p. 308, says: «The [ancient] Persians are said to make the projections of the rays only in the right sphere; which serves only those who live under the equinoctial circle. So we make no mention of it». These words probably mean that the ancient Persians added 60°, 90°, 120° to the right ascension of the star, or subtracted them, and took the arc of the ecliptic answering to the right ascension so increased or diminished to be the degree of longitude at which the left or right radiation falls on the ecliptic. This method[^401.3] is also mentioned, with others, by Johannes de Monteregio, or Regiomontanus, in Problem XXXI of the *Tabulae directionum profectionumque* (of 1475); and on the same, if I rightly understand what Ibn Hibintā says, f. 34r–v, Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī[^401.4] relied in constructing the tables of radiations that Ibn Hibintā gives, ff. 35r–37v. It is known that al-Khwārizmī followed chiefly the methods of the Indians in his astronomical work.
