@p 0413b003..0413b014
[No one will ever believe that this whole chapter, a monument of gross ignorance, is al-Battānī's work, especially since it conflicts with the true precepts of chapter XLVI (p. 114). The interpolator foolishly misinterpreted the meaning and the use of the tables of the stations of the planets, which are in the *Almagest* XII, 7 (ed. Halma vol. II, pp. 354–55) and, with a slight change in the numbers of Mars, in al-Battānī, vol. II, pp. 138–39. The foundation of the tables is this: since the centre of the epicycle moves on the circumference of a circle eccentric with respect to the earth, the ratio of the radius of the epicycle to its geocentric distance varies at the various places of the eccentric; it is greatest at the perigee, least at the apogee. So the places and the epochs of the stations cannot be computed on one and the same numerical basis at different places of the eccentric, but particular elements must be taken for each amount of longitude on the eccentric; the stations answer to points of the epicycle that differ from one another, and to different values of the anomaly. These values Ptolemy gives in the tables for all the positions of the centre of the epicycle on the eccentric.
@p 0413b015..0413b026
The interpolator, who perhaps did not know all this, interpreted the tables absurdly. Ptolemy says that Saturn, when the centre of the epicycle is at the apogee of the eccentric, makes its first station at 112° 45′ of anomaly, the second at 247° 15′; but when the centre of the epicycle stands at the perigee, the first at 115° 29′, the second at 244° 31′. By dividing these numbers by the daily motion of the anomaly, the numbers of days are found that pass from the epoch of the apogee on the epicycle to the epoch of the first and of the second station. — Our interpolator, on the contrary, says that Saturn is direct from the apogee of the epicycle up to the anomaly 112° 45′, whatever the position of the planet in the zodiac; then that it stays from the anomaly 112° 45′ to 115° 29′; thence retrogrades up to the anomaly 244° 31′; again stays up to the anomaly 247° 15′; and finally advances with direct motion up to 360°, that is, until it returns to the apogee of the epicycle. Nor did he notice how many absurdities arise from this interpretation, especially for Mars, to which he dares to give a station of 25 1/3 days! — Schiaparelli.]
