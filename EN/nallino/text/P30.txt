In the same codex B, f. 54vb–56va, there is also another little book: «*Incipit liber Thebith de quantitatibus stellarum et planetarum et proportio terre.* Ptholomeus et alij sapientes posuerunt corpus terre communem mensuram qua metiebantur stellarum corpora, et posuerunt medietatem diametri terre communem mensuram qua stellarum a centro terre longitudines mensurabant.....» (here begins the book of Thebith on the sizes of the stars and the planets and the proportion of the earth. Ptolemy and other sages made the body of the earth the common measure by which they measured the bodies of the stars, and made half the diameter of the earth the common measure by which they measured the distances of the stars from the centre of the earth.....). It sets out briefly the distances of the Sun and the Moon found by Ptolemy from the eclipses; after which it adds (f. 55ra and b): «Dicit ergo Geber ostensum est per hoc proportio [sic] cuiuscumque 2. diametrorum luminarium ad diametrum terre» (Geber therefore says: by this has been shown the proportion [sic] of each of the two diameters of the luminaries to the diameter of the earth). There follow the diameters of the heavenly bodies, their volumes («bodies»), and also their distances from the centre of the Earth in terrestrial radii and in miles (a degree of the equator being taken as $56\tfrac{2}{3}$ miles). The method of the computation does not differ from the one that Albatenius reports, but the numbers agree with al-Farghānī. — Lastly it sets out the latitudes of the seven climates of the Earth on the authority of al-Farghānī («Alfragano», f. 56ra); the last words are: «Latitudo 7.$^{\text{i}}$ climatis est trium graduum et 4.$^{\text{e}}$ unius in celo, qui valent in terra 184. sed iste non est in terra, sed proximus ei maior scilicet ille 185. quia quoddam modicum remansit. Explicit tebith de quantitatibus stellarum» (the latitude of the seventh climate is three degrees and a quarter of one in the heaven, which are worth 184 on earth; but this is not on earth, but the next greater, namely 185, because a certain small amount remained. Here ends Thebith on the sizes of the stars). Because of the names of Alfraganus and above all of Geber (Jābir ibn Aflaḥ), and because of the discrepancies from the numbers of the other small work, a very strong suspicion arises that the little book is spurious. Other codices are indicated by Steinschneider, *Thabit ben Korra*, in Zeitschrift für Mathematik und Physik, XVIII, 1873, p. 337.

291, n. 3: Relying on the Paris codex, I had attributed the little book to al-Zarqālī. But afterwards I found some excerpts from the same codex (formerly cod. Sorb. 980) in Steinschneider, *Zum Speculum astronomicum*, in Zeitschr. f. Mathem. XVI, 1871, p. 390, who shows that Arçachel is corrupted from Zahel, i.e. Sahl ibn Bishr[^85.1]. Some things that do not belong to this small work at all have crept into the Paris copy; I therefore think it worth while to add to Steinschneider's information[^85.2] some notes from an excellent codex of the Laurentian Library (Florence), marked «S. Marco 194» (f. 69r–87v). The beginning is: «Incipit *liber arçachelis introductorium ad librum iudiciorum arabum.* [Omnibus] planetis erraticis qui feruntur in signis. non quod sint in eis. sed quia feruntur sub eis. hoc est in directo eorum. altior et propior circulo signorum et cursu tardior est almicatil [*al-muqātil*] .i. saturnus. Deinde almustaim [*al-mushtarī*] .i. iupiter. Deinde almereg [*al-mirrīkh*] .i. mars. Deinde semuz [*ash-shams*] .i. sol. Deinde azara [*az-zuhara*] .i. venus. Deinde aiotarit [*ʿuṭārid*] .i. mercurius. quem sequitur alamar [*al-qamar*] .i. luna. que est terre propior et cursu omnibus velocior. — Significantur etiam per caput et caudam draconis quod arabes dicunt ieuzahar [*jawzahar*].....» (here begins the book of Arçachel, an introduction to the book of the judgements of the Arabs. Of all the wandering planets, which are carried in the signs, not because they are in them but because they are carried beneath them, that is, in a line with them, the highest and nearest to the circle of the signs and the slowest in its course is almicatil, i.e. Saturn. Then almustaim, i.e. Jupiter. Then almereg, i.e. Mars. Then semuz, i.e. the Sun. Then azara, i.e. Venus. Then aiotarit, i.e. Mercury, which is followed by alamar, i.e. the Moon, which is nearest to the earth and the swiftest of all in its course. — There are also signified, by the head and the tail of the dragon, what the Arabs call ieuzahar.....). Then come these chapters: *De domibus; De gaudiis planetarum; De exaltationibus et casibus eorumdem; De distinctione signorum fixorum, mobilium et communium*[^86.1][^n]; *De faciebus signorum* (with a table below it); *De signorum complexionibus; De ascensionibus* (whose table is left empty); *De planetarum aspectibus; De planetarum fortitudine; De planetarum debilitate*[^86.2].

When these are finished, the codex gives on f. 73r–v the *significations* of each house and the *loca digniora circuli et laudabiliora* (the worthier and more praiseworthy places of the circle), which are set out in the edition, f. 111vb–112rb.
