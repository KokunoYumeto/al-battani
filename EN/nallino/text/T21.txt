On pp. 88–91: the tables for computing eclipses
@p 0680b002..0680b004
**P. 88**, the small table of the *unequal motions*. It is mentioned in ch. XLII, vol. I, p. 94; it is missing in Ptolemy. — On the smaller table on the right side of the page, which is mentioned in vol. I, pp. 94 and 150, see what Schiaparelli set out in vol. I, p. 273, note *a*.

The *limits of the eclipses* of the sun should be corrected as I showed on p. 209, namely: 159° 44′ to *190° 56′*, 0° 0′ to 20° 16′, *349° 4′* to 360°.
@p 0680b007..0680b022
**P. 89**, the table of *correction*. It is mentioned in chs. XXXIX, XLI, XLIII and XLIV (vol. I, pp. 80, 83, 87, 88, 97, 101, 106, 110–11). It is the κανόνιον διορϑώσεως of Ptolemy, VI, 8 (ed. Halma, vol. I, p. 430), in which, however, only the διαφόρων ἑξηκοστά, «the sixtieths of the differences», are given. Theon, *Handy Tables*, vol. I, p. 146, gives all the columns. Under the number 18, 1′ 42″ should be restored for 1′ 22″. — The «portions of the distances of the moon» are the proportional parts to be taken from the variation of the diameter of the moon (or of the sun) between the apogee and the perigee distance, so as to know the diameter answering to the corrected anomaly of the moon with which one must enter the table. Moreover, in vol. I, p. 80, with the anomaly of the sun taken as the argument, the portions serve to determine the variation of the parallax of the sun. — The two remaining columns, wrongly merged into one by the scribe of the codex, give the correction to be applied to the lunar parallax in latitude and in longitude on account of the epicycle and of the eccentric; the argument of the column of the epicycle is the corrected anomaly of the moon, that of the eccentric the mean double elongation. — All the columns occur in the *Alfonsine Tables*, p. 116r of the edition mentioned in vol. I, p. XXXVI, note 7; the heading there is: «Table of the equation of the diversity of aspect [i.e. of the parallax], or table *Attacium*». «*Attacuim*» should be restored, that is, *at-taqwīm*, the name by which al-Battānī also designates this correction[^680.1].

**P. 89**, the *eclipsed digits*. The three columns that serve to compute them are mentioned in chs. XLIII and XLIV (vol. I, pp. 101 and 111–12); they occur in Ptolemy, VI, 8 (ed. Halma, vol. I, p. 430), and in Theon, II, 95. In the third column, l. 3, Ptolemy has 2 1/15 (= 2$^{\mathrm{dig}}$ 4′), Theon 2 1/5; in l. 9 Halma's edition wrongly has 9$^{\mathrm{dig}}$ 10′ (ϛ″) for 9$^{\mathrm{dig}}$ 1/2 (ϛ″).

**P. 89**, the *inclinations of the eclipsed parts* with respect to the ecliptic, given in the last four columns. They are mentioned in chs. XLIII and XLIV (vol. I, pp. 101 and 112); the tables are also in Ptolemy, VI, 11 (ed. Halma, vol. I, p. 449), who gives the seconds too, and in Theon, I, 146 and II, 95 (ἡλίου καὶ σελήνης προσνεύσεις), with whose help it was easy to heal the small errors of the codex. — Delambre, *Astr. ancienne*, vol. II, p. 238: «Ptolémée passe ensuite[^680.2] à une recherche moins importante et négligée depuis par les modernes; c'est celle de la direction de la partie éclipsée soit par rapport à l'écliptique, soit par rapport aux différens points de l'horizon[^680.3]. Cette direction change à chaque instant, il se borne à la déterminer pour les points les plus importans, comme le commencement, la fin, le milieu, l'immersion et l'émersion. Il fait passer un grand cercle par les deux centres et prolonge cet arc jusqu'à l'écliptique et jusqu'à l'horizon. Le problème est donc purement trigonométrique. Il y néglige l'inclinaison de l'orbite» (Ptolemy then passes on to a less important inquiry, since neglected by the moderns: the direction of the eclipsed part, either with respect to the ecliptic or with respect to the different points of the horizon. This direction changes at every instant; he confines himself to determining it for the most important points, such as the beginning, the end, the middle, the immersion and the emersion. He passes a great circle through the two centres and prolongs this arc to the ecliptic and to the horizon. So the problem is purely trigonometric. He neglects the inclination of the orbit in it). Delambre gives a clear exposition of these directions, following Theon's commentary, in another place, II, pp. 597–605. — The numbers are clearly computed with two formulas (see the figure in vol. @p 0681b006..0681b012
**P. 90**, *eclipses of the moon*. The tables are mentioned in ch. XLIII, vol. I, p. 101. Ptolemy, VI, 7 (ed. Halma, vol. I, pp. 428–29), gives similar ones; but he takes as the argument of the table the true distance of the moon from the northern limit, at intervals of 30′ at the greatest distance from the earth and of 34′ at the least; all the numbers differ. In Theon, II, 90, the argument is the true latitude of the moon, as in al-Battānī's tables, with which Theon also shares the name «*duration*» (μονή) for «*half-duration*» (ἡμίσου μονῆς) in the third columns (see vol. I, p. 275). But Theon leaves out the seconds and uses a rougher computation than al-Battānī.
@p 0681b013..0681b016
**P. 91**, *eclipses of the sun*. The argument of the tables is the apparent latitude of the moon (that is, the true latitude ± the parallax), as in Theon's similar table, II, 90. Ptolemy, VI, 7 (ed. Halma, I, pp. 426–27), takes the apparent distance from the northern limit, at intervals of 30 minutes; the other numbers differ from al-Battānī's.
