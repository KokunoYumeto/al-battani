161, n. 3: What is told of Euclid and the *obliquity of the ecliptic* goes back in its first origin to the commentary of Heron of Alexandria (first century BC) on Euclid. For I find this in al-Nayrīzī[^438.1]: «In figura sextadecima [libri IV] dixit Yrinus [= Īron, Heron], quod ipsa est, sicut dixit Euclides, que ubilibet est necessaria superioribus spheris. In his enim spheris necessarium est, ut sit in arcu, qui est inter circulum equinoctialem et inter unumquemque duorum circulorum solstitialium, figura habens duodecim [sic! leg.: quindecim] bases, et astrologi dixerunt, scilicet quod arcus, qui est inter circulum equinoctii et inter unum duorum circulorum solstitialium, quod scilicet est arcus unius circulorum, qui transeunt per polos sphere, scilicet polos tocius, *recipit figuram duodecim* [sic!] *basium equalium, et ideo Euclides apposuit hanc figuram, ut nihil praetermittatur non probatum*» (on the sixteenth figure [of book IV] Yrinus [= Īron, Heron] said that it is, as Euclid said, the one that is needed everywhere for the higher spheres. For in these spheres it is necessary that in the arc between the equinoctial circle and each of the two solstitial circles there be a figure of twelve [sic! read: fifteen] sides; and the astrologers have said that the arc between the circle of the equinox and one of the two solstitial circles, which is the arc of one of the circles that pass through the poles of the sphere, namely the poles of the whole, *takes a figure of twelve* [sic!] *equal sides, and therefore Euclid added this figure, so that nothing should be left unproved*).
@p 0438b031..0438b040 + 0439b001..0439b009
Proclus Diadochus (d. AD 485) relates similar things[^438.2] on the eighth proposition of the first book of Euclid[^438.3]. For after noting that the eighth can be demonstrated even without the help of the seventh, he defends Euclid thus: «Not very excellently (περιττῶς), they say, was it [the seventh proposition] inserted by the author of the Elements (στοιχειωτής); for if we have taken it up only for the sake of the eighth, and the eighth can be demonstrated even without it, how will the seventh not appear wholly useless? To these [reproaches] must be answered what our predecessors (οἱ πρὸ ἡμῶν) also said: that the demonstration of the seventh is most useful to those who are versed in astronomy (τοῖς τὰ ἀστρονομικὰ δεινοῖς), in questions about eclipses. For using it, they say that three successive eclipses cannot occur equally distant from one another, that is, so that the second is as far in time from the first as the third from the second ..... That this is so is demonstrated by the seventh proposition. And it is not this one alone, superfluous indeed for the [geometrical] work undertaken, but as it were making us ready for astronomy, that the author of the Elements demonstrates[^439.1], but many other theorems and problems too. Will anyone say, then, that the author brings forward the last proposition of the fourth book, in which he inscribes the side of the fifteen-sided polygon in a circle, for any reason other than the relation of this problem to astronomy? *For when they inscribe the fifteen-sided polygon in the circle passing through the poles, they have the distance between the poles of the equator and the poles of the zodiac; for these are distant from each other by the side of the fifteen-sided polygon*[^439.2]. The author of the Elements, therefore, with astronomy too in view, thought that many things should be demonstrated beforehand, to prepare us for that discipline as well».
@p 0439b010..0439b019
This comparison of the side of the inscribed fifteen-sided polygon with the amount of the obliquity of the ecliptic is old. For on the authority of Eudemus (fourth century BC) Theon of Smyrna says[^439.3][^n] that the astronomers who flourished after Anaximander found «that the fixed stars move about the axis passing through the poles [of the equinoctial circle], but the planets about the axis of the zodiac, perpendicular to the zodiac; and that the axis of the fixed stars is distant from the axis of the planets by the side of the fifteen-sided polygon, that is, by 24 degrees» (ἀπέχουσι δ’ἀλλήλων ὅ τε τῶν ἀπλανῶν καὶ τῶν πλανωμένων ἄξων πεντεκαιδεκαγώνου πλευρὰν ὅ ἐστι μοῖραι κδʹ). — Likewise p. 203 (ed. Martin, p. 334): «24 degrees are the fifteenth part of the 360 degrees of the whole; for fifteen times 24 make 360. For this reason we say that those two axes, the axis of the fixed stars and the axis of the planets, are distant from each other by the side of the fifteen-sided polygon inscribed in the sphere».
