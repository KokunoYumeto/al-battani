@p 0284b034
To compute *the hours of the night that have elapsed*, by any star.
@fig AB01-PDF0285-F01 AB01-PDF0285-body-F01
@p 0284b035..0284b037 + 0285b001..0285b005
First let the altitude of the star on the meridian circle be found, by adding the declination δ of the star to the [meridian] altitude of the beginning of Aries, or subtracting it, as the case requires. This rule of the author is the same as the rule set out in ch. XV for computing the meridian altitude of the sun:
\[ [[(α)]] \text{merid. alt.} = \text{complement of the latitude} \pm \text{declin.}; \]
for, as appears at once from the figure, the meridian altitude of the beginning of Aries (and of any other point of the equinoctial circle) is always the complement of the latitude φ of the given city, that is, the complement of the altitude of the pole of the equator above the horizon of the city[^285.1].
@f 285.1 0285n001
See ch. VII (p. 20, ll. 1–2), XIV (p. 29, last line), XV (p. 30, l. 15).
@p 0285b006..0285b014
This done, al-Battānī repeats the rule of chapter XVI:
\[ [[(β)]] \operatorname{vers} t = \operatorname{vers} P - \frac{\sin h\,\operatorname{vers} P}{\sin(\text{merid. alt.})}. \]
By adding the arc *t* to the half arc of the day of the star, or subtracting it, according as the star is west or east of the meridian circle, the arc of the revolution of the sphere since the rising of the star will be found. Let this arc be increased (or, if the star rises from the horizon before sunset, diminished) by the difference between the oblique ascension of the star and the oblique descension (or the ascension of the nadir) of the sun; there will result the arc of the celestial sphere revolved since sunset, whose degrees are very easily turned into seasonal or equal hours.
@p 0285b015..0285b016
If the computation is made by the moon, whose parallax is perceptible, not its true but its apparent coordinates must be used.
