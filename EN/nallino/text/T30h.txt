@p 0735b025..0735b045
A few things must be said of the word الخوصى, which denotes the colour towards which the stars tend in the version of the Almagest made by al-Ḥajjāj and in those who mostly follow this version (al-Ṣūfī, Naṣīr al-dīn); Ulugh Beg rendered it in Persian سُرْخى, «redness», and Gerard of Cremona (relying on faulty readings) rendered it in Latin twice by «air» and twice by «rapine». The word is corrupted in many ways in the codices: الخوصى, الحوصى, الحوض, الحرض; but the more frequent and more probable reading is the one that Schjellerup and I have accepted. The Arabic dictionaries do not know it at all, and no one answered Knobel (*Note on an obscure arabic word* etc.) when he publicly asked scholars about its meaning. Schjellerup translated «rouge» by mere conjecture, which he afterwards set out in his very short paper *On Al-Sūfi and Ptolemy's Catalogues*, Monthly Notices of the R. Astron. Society, vol. XLV, 1885, pp. 465–66; but Knobel showed well enough, in a short note added to that paper on p. 466, that it is idle and to be rejected. If in so great a perplexity one may put forward a new conjecture, I think it not impossible that الخوصي (*al-khūṣī*) should be taken as a relative adjective, derived from خوص (*khūṣ*), «the branches of the date palm»; it would then denote the colour of these branches, above all in their lower part near the trunk, when they begin to dry, namely a yellow tending to red (and often nearly red), which fits the Greek ὑπόκιῤῥος very well. Such relative adjectives, derived from plants or from other things, are not infrequent for denoting colours, as أُرْجُوَانِيّ, بَنَفْسَجِيّ, دُرِّيّ, زَعْفَرَانِيّ, زَيْتُونِيّ, سَمَاوِيّ, سِنْجَابِيّ, شَمَعِيّ (which occurs in the other version of the Almagest), فُسْتُقِيّ, قِرْمِزيّ, لَازُوَرْدِيّ, وَرْدِيّ, etc., some of which are missing in the *dictionaries* but have been collected by Dozy and by Fleischer in their Supplements. — The other version of the Almagest, preserved in no. 390 of the British Museum, renders ὑπόκιῤῥος by شمعي (*shamaʿī*), «waxen [in colour]», that is, like wax not yet purified, whose colour is yellow.

From what has just been said it is clear that only *four stars* are marked with the epithet ὑπόκιῤῥος in the *Arabic* tradition of the Almagest, that is, in the version of al-Ḥajjāj (and in Gerard of Cremona's Latin), in the other version, no. 390 of the British Museum, in al-Ṣūfī's catalogue and in Ulugh Beg's Persian catalogue; that this epithet is rendered *shamaʿī*, «waxen», in no. 390 of the British Museum, «inclining to redness (*surkhī*)» by Ulugh Beg, and «tending to الخوصى» by the rest; and that these four stars are *Aldebaran, Pollux, Antares* and *Betelgeuze*. — Naṣīr al-dīn alone adds *Sirius* to them. — *Arcturus* is counted among the red stars by al-Ṣūfī alone, not, however, in the catalogue, p. 69, but in his general description of the constellation of Boötes (p. 65), in which he explains and corrects Ptolemy on the strength of his own observations[^736.1]. In the copies of the Almagest from which the Arabic versions flowed the epithet ὑπόκιῤῥος does not seem to have been missing at Arcturus, but to have been so corrupted that it was wrongly explained by the Arabs; see p. 284, note 3, and what I shall say below of Barhebraeus.

Barhebraeus, writing in 1279, knew very little Greek or none at all[^736.5], but had at hand books translated from the Greek into Syriac and Arabic that have not come down to us; in the eighth chapter of the first part of his Syriac Astronomy he describes summarily the constellations and their principal stars. To *Aldebaran* (p. 115 of the text, 100 of the translation) and to *Pollux* (ibid.) he gives no colour; but he calls *Antares* (p. 116 of the text, 102 of the translation) and *Betelgeuze* (p. 118 of the text, 103 of the translation) *sūmāqā* ܣܘܡܩܐ, «red»[^736.6][^n]. Strangely, however, he describes the other two thus: «That bright one which is of the first magnitude and is called *arqṭūrūs ūpīqūrūs*, that is, the guardian of the north» ܢܨܝܚܐ ܗܰܘ ܕܡܢ ܪܒܘܬܐ ܩܕܡܝܬܐ ܡܬܩܕܐ ܐܪܩܛܘܪܘܣ ܐܘܦܝܩܘܪܘܣ ܐܘܟܝܬ ܢܳܛܪ ܓܪܒܝܐ (p. 111 of the text, 96 of the translation); — «That bright one which is in its mouth, which is *sīryūn*, of the first magnitude, specially surnamed *dog* (*kalbā*) and *ūpqrūs*[^736.7], because it rises from the east after Orion and is thought of as barking after him[^736.8]; it is also called *sīryūn arabīqūn* (σείριος ἀραβικός)[^737.1], because it sets in the region of Arabia» ܘܗܰܘ ܢܨܝܚܐ ܕܒܦܘܡܗ ܕܗܘܝܘ ܣܝܪܝܘܢ ܡܢ ܪܒܘܬܐ ܩܕܡܝܬܐ ܕܝܠܢܐܝܬ ܟܠܒܐ ܘܐܘܦܩܪܘܣ ܡܬܟܰܢܐ. ܒܗܳܝ ܕܒܬܪ ܓܢܒܪܐ ܣܳܠܩ ܡܢ ܡܕܢܚܐ. ܘܐܝܟ ܗܰܘ ܕܢܳܒܰܚ ܒܬܪܗ ܡܬܗܓܓ. ܘܣܝܪܝܘܢ ܐܪܒܝܩܘܢ ܬܘܒ ܡܬܩܪܐ. ܒܗܳܝ ܕܒܦܢܝܬ ܐܪܒܝܐ ܥܳܪܒ (p. 118 of the text, 103–104 of the translation). In describing the constellations Barhebraeus seems to have used *celestial globes* and the *Almagest*; the latter he probably had at hand translated into Arabic or Syriac[^737.2], and, unless I am mistaken, in a copy transcribed from another in whose margin some reader had noted the Greek names of the stars, following Greek or Syriac texts. If so, it is very easy to understand how that reader, deceived by Ptolemy's ambiguous description of *Arcturus* (ὁ καλούμενος ἀρκτοῦρος ὑπόκιῤῥος) and of *Sirius* (καλούμενος κύων καὶ ὑπόκιῤῥος), could have taken the word ὑπόκιῤῥος, which is left out in the Arabic Almagests[^737.3], for a proper name of these two stars.

@p 0737b012..0737b019
The question of the ancient colour of *Sirius*, which is now white, is of great importance in celestial physics. The Arabic tradition of the Almagest is by no means to be despised here. In the preface to al-Ṣūfī, p. 25, Schjellerup, comparing some passages of Greek and Latin writers and relying above all on al-Ṣūfī's silence, conjectured that the older Arab translators found nothing in the catalogue of the Almagest about the colour of Sirius[^737.4], and concluded: «il faut vraisemblablement lire dans l'Almageste Ὁ ἐν τῷ στόματι λαμπρότατος καλούμενος κύων καὶ σείριος (au lieu καὶ ὑπόκιῤῥος d'après Halma [et Heiberg]), conformément aux désignations que donne Ptolémée aux autres étoiles brillantes qui portent un nom propre, comme α Bootis (ἀρκτοῦρος), α Leonis (βασιλίσκος), α Scorpii (ἀντάρης), etc.» (one should probably read in the Almagest Ὁ ἐν τῷ στόματι λαμπρότατος καλούμενος κύων καὶ σείριος (instead of καὶ ὑπόκιῤῥος, according to Halma [and Heiberg]), in accordance with the designations that Ptolemy gives to the other bright stars that bear a proper name, such as α Bootis (ἀρκτοῦρος), α Leonis (βασιλίσκος), α Scorpii (ἀντάρης), etc.).
@f 737.4 0737nl012..0737nr008
But he is in error when he states: «Al-Battani .... ne rapporte, dans son ouvrage: De numero stellarum, que cinq étoiles rougeâtres, tandis que l'Almageste grec nous en présente six» (al-Battānī .... reports in his work De numero stellarum only five reddish stars, whereas the Greek Almagest gives us six). No doubt he had in mind the passage of vol. I, p. 126, l. 5, where, as already in the Almagest, five *nebulous* stars are said to be in the sky (Plato: «and five cloudy ones, which are likened to clouds»); he confused the nebulous with the reddish ones.
@p 0737b020..0737b035
Schiaparelli treated the matter with admirable learning in *Rubra Canicula: considerazioni sulla mutazione di colore che si dice avvenuta in Sirio* (37 pp., from the Atti dell'I. R. Accademia di Scienze, Lettere ed Arti degli Agiati, series III, vol. II, fasc. 2, Rovereto 1896), and again in *Rubra Canicula: nuove considerazioni circa la mutazione di colore* etc. (24 pp.; from the Atti dell'I. R. Accademia, series III, vol. III, fasc. 1, Rovereto 1897), in which second paper he strongly attacks and refutes the contrary conclusions of the astronomer T. J. J. See[^737.5]. He shows most fully that the names of Sirius which at first sight may seem to denote a red colour of the star in some ancient writers are to be interpreted otherwise; as for Ptolemy's catalogue, he points out that the authority of the Greek editions is somewhat weakened by the old Arabic tradition of the Almagest preserved in al-Ṣūfī and by the Latin version of Gerard of Cremona, so that it becomes certain that several Greek codices of the Almagest known to the Arabs did not give the name ὑπόκιῤῥος to Sirius. So of Schjellerup's conjecture (καὶ σείριος for ὑπόκιῤῥος) he judges thus, on p. 13 of the first paper: «Simili correzioni ipotetiche sono sempre pericolose: nel presente caso tuttavia, dopo vedute le ragioni sopra addotte, la proposta dello Schjellerup potrà sembrare non solo ingegnosa ma anche abbastanza probabile» (such hypothetical corrections are always dangerous; in the present case, however, after the reasons given above, Schjellerup's proposal may seem not only ingenious but also fairly probable). The Oriental texts I cited above confirm very well what Schjellerup and Schiaparelli said of the older Arabic tradition. —

Moreover, Schiaparelli cites two passages of the *Tetrabiblos*, pp. 10–12 of the first paper, which also seem to show that Ptolemy did not ascribe a red colour to Sirius. The first passage is of such a kind that a comparison of many codices would be useful to remove all doubt; it is the ninth chapter (the seventh in the Latin edition of Frankfurt) of book I, which deals with the powers of the fixed stars and mentions the colours of some stars. According to both Greek editions, that of Nuremberg of 1535 and that of Basel of 1553, and also according to Hephaestion of Thebes, who about AD 380 copied this chapter of the Tetrabiblos into his astrology (περὶ καταρχῶν), *Aldebaran, Antares* and *Arcturus* are marked with the word ὑπόκιῤῥος, but nothing is said of the colour of Sirius.
