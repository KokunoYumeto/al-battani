The year 1191 is the fundamental epoch to which the author also refers the roots of the mean motions; reducing the catalogue to the year 299 H (which began on 28 Āb 1222 Dhū l-qarnayn, that is, 28 Aug. 911 AD) would not only have brought no advantage, but would at times have produced a very awkward disagreement with the other tables.
@p 0718b013..0718b025
Ptolemy, VII, 5 and VIII, 1 (ed. Halma, vol. II, pp. 32–83), lists 1025 stars[^718.2], of which 360 belong to the northern constellations, 349 to the zodiac and 316 to the southern ones. This is not the place to discuss his sources; yet I think I should point out that the opinion of the nineteenth-century writers, that Ptolemy's catalogue is nothing but the catalogue of Hipparchus with the longitudes increased by 2° 40′ for the precession that Ptolemy accepted for that interval of time, is now held to be wrong. For Fr. Boll, *Die Sternkataloge des Hipparch und des Ptolemaios* (Bibliotheca Mathematica, ed. G. Eneström, 3rd series, vol. 2, 1901, pp. 185–95), has shown from new Greek sources that Hipparchus described only 850 stars and that Ptolemy added 175 to them; moved by this information, A. A. Björnbo, *Hat Menelaos aus Alexandria einen Fixsternkatalog verfasst?* (Bibl. Mathem., 3rd series, vol. 2, 1901, pp. 196–212), weighed the matter further, stating on p. 206 that Ptolemy drew his additions of stars to Hipparchus's catalogue from writers of different ages without suitable reductions, so that the longitudes of the catalogue of the Almagest can in part be referred to no definite epoch.

@p 0718b026..0718b034 + 0719b001..0719b023
Björnbo, however, is in grave error when, from the testimony of the Arabs and above all of al-Battānī[^718.3], he wishes to conclude that the Arabs knew some description of the stars made by Menelaus; he even states on p. 207 «dass Al-Battānī in der That entweder ein astronomisches Werk von Menelaos, oder wenigstens Nachrichten, die wir nicht kennen, über ein solches besass» (that al-Battānī did in fact possess either an astronomical work of Menelaus or at least information, unknown to us, about one). The arguments on which he relies are now easily refuted with the help of the translation and my notes in vol. I. First, al-Battānī does not at all say that he added 11° 50′ to Ptolemy's longitudes (Björnbo, p. 205); for, as I pointed out in vol. III, p. 188, note 3, and vol. I, p. 124, note 9, Plato's «eleven degrees and a half and a third», or the والنصف والثلث of the Escorial codex, is a small error of the copyists for ونصف الثلث, «and half of a third», i.e. 10′; the wrong writing arose all the more easily because 11° 50′ had been mentioned a very little before. How al-Battānī chose this amount of 11° 10′ to be added to Ptolemy I have set out in vol. I, pp. 293–94. Thirdly, Björnbo says that al-Battānī knew observations of Regulus (α Leonis) and of Sirius (α Canis maioris) by Menelaus that are not mentioned in the Almagest; but here he was deceived by Plato's ambiguous version: «Among those observations by which Ptolemy worked there was an observation of Menelaus, which he made in the year 842 from King Nebuchadnezzar, and he said that the northern star that is placed between the two eyes of the Scorpion, as tested by means of the moon with the sphere of circles, stood in that year at 2 degrees and 22 minutes of Scorpio, and that, according to what *he had written in his book*, the Heart of the Lion in that same year *ought to have been* at 2 degrees and a sixth of Leo, Scehere Leumia[^719.1] at the 17th degree of Gemini». The Arabic text, vol. III, p. 187, ll. 15–20, is to be translated word for word thus: «One of the observations of stars that Ptolemy used was the observation that Menelaus reported, from the year 845 of the said King Nabonassar; and he reported that the northern one of the stars that are between the two eyes of the Scorpion was in that year, when he measured it with the armillary sphere by means of the moon, at 5° 55′ of Scorpio. And *it was necessary, according to what he described*[^719.2] *in his book*, that the Heart of the Lion should be in that said year at 2⅙° of Leo, and Sirius at 17° of Gemini». The subject of that «described in his book» is Ptolemy, as I accepted in vol. I, p. 124, l. 23, not Menelaus; so, comparing the longitudes of the catalogue of the Almagest with what Ptolemy reports «in his book» about the precession between himself and Menelaus, al-Battānī *conjectured* that in that year 845 of Nabonassar Regulus occupied 2° 10′ of Leo and Sirius 17° of Gemini[^719.3]. — But, even leaving all these considerations aside, anyone who looks at al-Battānī's tables and my short notes will see that the Arab astronomer used no Greek catalogue of stars other than Ptolemy's; so they will not be useless for *finally establishing* a *critical edition* of this part of the *Almagest*.

@p 0719b024..0719b037
With the gap of the Escorial codex filled from the Spanish version, al-Battānī's catalogue comprises 533 stars, namely 149 of the northern constellations, 198 of the zodiacal and 186 of the southern; how they were chosen is not certainly established, but see vol. I, pp. 297–98. — Corrections of Ptolemy's numbers seem to have been made only in the first star of Pegasus, the sixth of Leo, the first of Scorpio and the eleventh of Capricorn; it is very uncertain whether the discrepancies mentioned in the notes to the ninth, tenth and twelfth of Sagittarius are to be regarded as corrections by the author. On the discrepancies in the first of Cygnus, the first and third of Libra and the sixteenth of Sagittarius, see what I have pointed out in their places. The magnitudes of the 7th of Hercules, the 1st of Cassiopeia and the 4th of Perseus seem to have been changed by the author on purpose; those of the 3rd of Cassiopeia, the 1st and 8th of Ophiuchus and the 9th and 12th of Sagittarius are very well corrected; but these apparent corrections may be lucky errors of the copyists. — In describing the stars he followed a mediocre Arabic version of the *Almagest*, from which come the errors pointed out at the second star of Pegasus, the third of Andromeda, the eighth of Taurus and the twenty-fifth of Centaurus. How I was able to heal the frequent errors of the Escorial codex in the numbers is shown well enough by the short notes added to each page of the catalogue and by the supplement given on p. 278 ff.

A few general considerations will now suffice. Wherever the codex agrees with Ptolemy the reading is to be held good; so first I used the edition of Ptolemy's catalogue prepared by Baily, which we may almost call critical[^719.4][^n]; it rests on two Greek codices already collated by Halma, on the Latin version from the Arabic published at Venice in 1515, on the Latin version from the Greek made by George of Trebizond and printed in 1528 under the care of Lucas Gauricus, and finally on the Greek text that appeared at Basel in 1538 through the work of Grynaeus[^720.1].

2° when, as not seldom happens, Liechtenstein and al-Ṣūfī agreed with al-Battānī against Baily or Halma, I kept the readings of the codex, as answering to the Arabic tradition of the Almagest and sometimes better than the Greek ones;
@p 0720b015..0720b016
3° I also judged that the numbers of the codex should not be changed when Liechtenstein alone or al-Ṣūfī alone agreed with the Escorial codex; which indeed happened rarely;
@p 0720b017..0720b020
4° when all the sources disagreed with the Escorial codex but partly also differed among themselves, I accepted the numbers of two that agreed (such as Liechtenstein and al-Ṣūfī, much more rarely the Greek and Liechtenstein, very rarely the Greek and al-Ṣūfī); otherwise I did not dare to change the numbers of the codex, which are printed in *italic*, or sloping, letters. This was extremely rare.

Likewise, after all the tables had been printed, I received photographic sheets of the *Spanish version* of al-Battānī's catalogue. It teems with more frequent errors than the Escorial codex, mostly because of the confusion of Arabic letters. For the Spanish translator often interchanged ف 100 and ڢ 80, ن 50 and ي 10; and, since he had at hand a copy written in Maghribi, that is, western Arabic, letters, he often read ز 7 for ن 50, د 4 or ه 5 for ط 9, and ط 9 for ك 20. Moreover, he took the negation لا, which he sometimes found in his copy to denote the zero (0), at the numerical value of its letters, and wrote 31 instead of 0. Only in three or four places does the Spanish translation give better readings, which are no doubt to be accepted; but very happily it fills the gap that, because of a leaf lost from the Escorial codex, I had indicated in this very vol. II, p. 172.
