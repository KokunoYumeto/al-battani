Chapter 42. On knowing the computation of the conjunctions and the oppositions of the sun and the moon by the era of the Romans and the era of the Copts, and on knowing their times in every locality
@p AB01-PDF1014-L21..AB01-PDF1013-L20
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the computation of the conjunctions or the oppositions in any month you wish of the months of the Romans, take the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, and do not count the year that you are in until Subāṭ has run out. Look for the number of years that you have in the column of the collected years of the tables of the conjunction or of the opposition, whichever you want. Wherever you find the same — I mean the same number — or the nearest one below it, take what stands opposite it in the four columns that are for the days, the mean position of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the motion in latitude. Then see what remains with you of the years in excess of those that you found in the table, enter with it in the column of the single years, and take what stands opposite it in those four columns; write down what you find in each of them with its counterpart, I mean each kind under its kind. Then take what stands opposite the complete month before the month in which you wish to compute, of the days of the lunar months set down in the first of the four columns, and add it to the sum of the days that came from the collected and the single years that you wrote down. If the sum of all that is more than the number of the days of the Roman months set down under that complete month, and less than the days under the month that you are in, which is the month in which you wish to compute, write down those days that you found opposite the complete month and what is under them in the three remaining columns. If the sum of the days is more than the Roman days set down under the month in which you wish to compute, take the days that stand opposite the month before the complete month, and what is under them in the three remaining columns, and write it down with what you wrote from the tables of the collected and the single years. What each of the four columns comes to after you have added it up, write down in the manner given before. Then subtract the Roman days that stand opposite the complete month before the month in which you wish to compute from the days that came from the sum of what is in the three tables of the collected years, the single years and the months: the days and minutes that remain are days that have passed of the month in which you wished to compute, and equal hours after noon of the last day that has passed of that month up to the time of the conjunction or the opposition of the sun and the moon at that time by their mean course. [From the three remaining columns come the positions of the sun and the moon at that time by their mean course,] the anomaly of the moon and the motion in latitude. [The mean position of the moon] is the mean position of the sun at the time of [the conjunction; at the time of] the opposition the mean position of the moon is necessarily opposite the mean position of the sun.
@n The years of the tables begin on 1 Ādhār, March, six months before the year of Dhū l-Qarnayn with the same number, which begins on 1 Aylūl, September; so the year that you are in is counted only when Subāṭ, February, is over (Nallino; see chapter 33). The first bracketed words are supplied by Nallino after Plato of Tivoli. The printed text then reads «and it is the mean position of the sun at the time of the opposition, and the mean position of the moon is necessarily opposite the mean position of the sun»; the other bracketed words follow the correction that Nallino proposes and translates.
@p AB01-PDF1013-L20..AB01-PDF1012-L22
If you wish to compute the conjunction and the opposition by the era of the Copts, take the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn together with the year that you are in, even if only one day of Aylūl has entered. Then cast 287 from the years, and take a quarter of what remains: the result is the days of the quarters. If a fraction falls in it, do not count it; if no fraction falls, that year is a leap year. When the year is a leap year, drop one day from the days of the quarters, unless Subāṭ has run out and come out at 29 days; when Subāṭ has run out, add the day that you had taken away back to the days. To the days of the quarters that result always add the three days by which the Copts precede the Greeks in Tūt, and to what the days come to after that add the days from the beginning of Aylūl to the end of the Roman month before the month that you want, the month in which you compute. If the sum of the days is more than 365 days, cast 365 from it and add a year to the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn from which you subtracted nothing; if the year is a leap year and Subāṭ has run out, you count the year as 366 days. What results of the days after casting off the year, if they were more than a year, or the days themselves if they were less than a year, are the days of the Copts; write them down apart. Then enter with what you have of the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, with the year added on account of the days if there is one, into the table of the collected Egyptian years, which go in steps of 25, in the column of the collected years in the tables of the conjunction or of the opposition, whichever you want. Wherever you find the same years, or the nearest number below them, take what stands opposite them in the four columns in that way. Look for the years that remain in the column of the single years, and take again what stands opposite them in the four columns. Then take the days of the Copts and cast them out by thirties; enter with the number of complete months that results into the column of numbers of the tables of the Coptic months, take what stands opposite it in the column of the days, and add it to the days that came to you from the two tables of the collected and the single years. If the sum is equal to the number of the days of the Copts, or more than it by less than a lunar month, write down those days and what is under them in the three remaining columns. If the sum of the days is more than the days of the Copts by more than a lunar month, take one month from the number of the complete Coptic months with which you entered the table; take what stands opposite the number of complete Coptic months that remains in the four columns and write it down. Then add it up in the usual manner, and subtract the days of the Copts from the days that result: the days and minutes that remain are the days and the hours of the conjunction or the opposition that have passed of the month in which you wished to compute, and what results from the three columns is the mean position of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the motion in latitude.
@n The days of the Copts, the days that have passed of the Coptic year, are found as in chapter 32.
@p AB01-PDF1012-L22..AB01-PDF1011-L03
When you know that by whichever of the two eras you wish, look at the minutes that are added up with the days, and count each 2½ minutes of them as an equal hour; what does not make up 2½ minutes is parts of an hour. The days and hours that result are the days of the mean conjunction or opposition and its hours after noon at the city of al-Raqqa; keep them.
@n A day is 60 minutes, so that 2½ minutes are an hour. The translation reads «الأيّام», «the days», for «الأبّام» (p. 141, line 2), a misprint that the corrigenda do not list.
@p AB01-PDF1011-L03..AB01-PDF1011-L08
Then write down the mean position of the sun and the moon in another place, making one of the two places for the sun and the other for the moon, and compute the true positions of the sun and the moon as usual, except that for the moon you then need only the single equation, for the distance between the sun and the moon is not such that a sensible error enters from the second equation. If the sun and the moon are equal to the minute, that is the time of the true conjunction or opposition. Then do with the motion in latitude what you did with the mean position of the moon: add the single equation to the motion in latitude if you added it to the mean position of the moon, and subtract it from it if you subtracted it from the mean position of the moon.
@p AB01-PDF1011-L08..AB01-PDF1011-L15
If the positions of the sun and the moon differ, take the excess between them in degrees and minutes, and find its sixth and its eighth. If the excess is the sun's, add that sixth and eighth to the anomaly of the moon, and if the excess is the moon's, subtract them from it; what the anomaly comes to after the addition or the subtraction is the corrected anomaly. Enter with it into the tables of the equation of the moon, in the two columns of numbers, and take what stands opposite it of the single equation, set down in the second column. If this anomaly is less than 180, subtract this equation from the mean position of the moon itself and from the motion in latitude itself; if the number of the anomaly is more than 180, add the equation to the mean position of the moon and to the motion in latitude. What the mean position of the moon comes to after the addition to it or the subtraction from it is the true position of the moon.
@n The excess is the sun's when the true sun is ahead of the true moon, so that the true conjunction comes after the mean one. The reason for adding a sixth and an eighth, 7/24, of the excess to the anomaly is given below. The second column is counted without the two columns of numbers (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1011-L15..AB01-PDF1010-L09
Then take again the excess between the sun and the moon and know it. Then take the motion of the sun and of the moon in an hour: enter with the corrected anomaly of the moon, with which you found the equation of the moon, and the anomaly of the sun, with which you found the equation of the sun, into the tables of the unequal course of the sun and the moon, in the two columns of numbers, which go in steps of 6 parts, and take what is under them in the column of the course of each of them, by interpolation, after adding to the course of the moon, or subtracting from it, the seconds that you find set down under the excess between the sun and the moon, in the way that we have explained in that chapter, at those tables. Then subtract the motion of the sun from the motion of the moon: the remainder is the unequal gain of the moon on the sun in an hour. Divide the excess between the sun and the moon by the gain of the moon: the hour and part of an hour that result are the hours of the excess; keep them. If the excess is the sun's, add the hours of the excess to the hours of the mean conjunction that came from the tables, and if the excess is the moon's, subtract them from them. What the hours of the mean conjunction come to after the addition or the subtraction are the plain hours of the true conjunction. If they are more than 24 hours, subtract 24 hours from them and add one day to the days that have passed of the month. If you need to subtract the hours of the excess from the hours of the mean conjunction, and the hours of the excess are more than the hours of the mean conjunction, take one day from the days that have passed of the month, count it as 24 hours, and add them to the hours of the mean conjunction; then take the hours of the excess from the sum. What results of the hours of the mean conjunction after the addition or the subtraction is the equal hours that have passed after noon at the city of al-Raqqa of the day that you obtained from the days that have passed of the month.
@n That chapter is a short unnumbered chapter on the hourly motion of the moon, which stands with the tables in the Escorial codex alone and which Nallino prints after chapter 57 as chapter H. The seconds correct the hourly motion of the moon for its elongation from the sun: about one second an hour for each degree of elongation, taken from it in the upper part of the epicycle and added in the lower (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). The plain hours are mean hours; they are converted into hours of the irregular days below.
@p AB01-PDF1010-L09..AB01-PDF1010-L12
Multiply the hours of the excess by the course of the sun and the course of the moon in an hour, and keep the products. If the excess is the sun's, add what results for the sun to the position of the sun, and what results for the moon to the position of the moon and to the motion in latitude; to the motion in latitude add besides the motion of the northern node in the amount of those hours. If the excess is the moon's, subtract in all of this instead of adding, so that you obtain the correct positions of the sun and the moon at that time.
@n The motion in latitude is counted from the northern node, which moves backwards; so the node's motion in those hours is added to it as well.
@p AB01-PDF1010-L12..AB01-PDF1010-L19
If you wish to work approximately in another way, find half a sixth of the excess between the sun and the moon. If the excess is the sun's, add half a sixth of the excess to the sun, and the whole excess with half its sixth to the moon and to the motion in latitude; if the excess is the moon's, subtract half a sixth of the excess from the sun, and the whole excess with half its sixth from the moon [and from the motion in latitude]: then the two are equal to the minute. Then divide [the whole excess] and half its sixth by the unequal motion of the moon in an hour: the result is the hours of the excess. Subtract them from the hours of the mean conjunction if the excess is the moon's, and add them to them if the excess is the sun's, in that manner. But the first procedure is more correct.
@n The bracketed words are supplied by Nallino after Plato of Tivoli. Half a sixth, a twelfth, of the excess is the motion of the sun while the moon gains the excess on it: the moon moves about 13 times as fast as the sun, so that in that time the sun moves about a twelfth of the excess (Nallino). This is Ptolemy's method in *Almagest* VI 4 (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1010-L19..AB01-PDF1009-L02
You should also know the anomaly of the moon at the time [of the conjunction and the opposition]: enter with the hours of the excess into the table of the hours, take the course of the anomaly of the moon in them, and add it to the corrected anomaly of the moon if the excess is the sun's, and subtract it from it if the excess is the moon's. What it comes to after that is the corrected anomaly of the moon at the time of the conjunction, if your computation is for the conjunction; if your computation is for the opposition, it is the corrected anomaly of the moon at the time of the opposition. Then add 180 degrees to the position of the moon that you had made the same as the mean position of the sun, so that the true position of the moon is opposite the true position of the sun, where it is seen.
@n The bracketed words are supplied by Nallino after Plato of Tivoli.
@p AB01-PDF1009-L02..AB01-PDF1009-L11
When you know the plain equal hours of the conjunction, which are the mean hours, convert them into hours of the irregular days. Enter with the degree of the sun into the tables of the right sphere and take the degrees and minutes that stand opposite it in the column of the equation of the days, set down in the sign of the sun; divide what there is by 15: what results is hours, and what remains is part of an hour. Always add it to the mean hours of the true conjunction: what they come to after that is the equal hours of the true conjunction converted into the irregular days, which are found by measurement, after noon at the city of al-Raqqa. Then convert them into hours of the locality that you want. You do this by taking the excess between the longitude of the city that you want and the longitude of the city of al-Raqqa, which is 73;15, and dividing it by 15. Add the hour or part of an hour that results to the hours of the true conjunction that you have obtained if the longitude of the city is greater than the longitude of al-Raqqa, and subtract it from them if the longitude of the city is less than the longitude of al-Raqqa. What the hours come to after the addition or the subtraction is the true equal hours after noon in that city.
@n The equation of the days with their nights is that of chapter 29. Here it is added, since the hours are converted from mean days into irregular days; chapter 34 subtracts it for the opposite conversion.

If you wish to know the ascendant of the conjunction, multiply all these hours by 15, add the product to the ascensions of the degree of the sun in the right sphere, and with what results you know the ascendant and the middle of the sky as usual.
@n At noon the sun is in the middle of the sky, and 15 times of the right sphere pass the meridian in each equal hour; the ascendant and the middle of the sky are then found as in chapters 16 and 35.
@p AB01-PDF1009-L13..AB01-PDF1009-L21
If you wish to convert these equal hours into seasonal hours, multiply them by 15 degrees and keep the product. Then find the hours of the night and of the daytime from the degree of the sun in that clime. From what you kept of the product take out the times of the hours of the daytime, up to a total of six hours; if it is less than six, add to the hours that come from it six seasonal hours, those from sunrise to noon: the sum is what has passed of the daytime from sunrise to the time of the conjunction, in seasonal hours. If what you took out is six complete hours and a remainder is left with you, take it out by the times of the hours of the night, up to a total of 12 hours; if yet another remainder is left, take it out by the times of the hours of the daytime again: what comes from it is what has passed of the hours of the daytime from sunrise of the morrow. If you wish to know the ascendant from these hours, you know it in the usual way.
@p AB01-PDF1009-L21..AB01-PDF1008-L07
It is clear that if the hours of the conjunction are more than half the equal hours of the daytime of that day, and you subtract them from 12 hours if they are less than 12, the remainder is the amount in equal hours by which the conjunction precedes the time of midnight. If the hours are more than 12, up to the completion of half the equal hours of that night, and you cast 12 hours from them, the remainder is the amount in equal hours by which the conjunction comes after the time of midnight. If they are more than 12 together with the half of the hours of the night that is added to them, subtract them from 24 hours: the remainder is the amount in equal hours by which the conjunction precedes the time of noon of the morrow. By what we have described you know the time of the conjunction or the opposition, and the positions of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the motion in latitude at those times.
@p AB01-PDF1008-L07..AB01-PDF1008-L16
What compels us to correct the anomaly of the moon by a sixth and an eighth of the excess is that the time of the mean conjunction cannot be, and need not be, at every time the time of the true conjunction. If we neglect what falls in the anomaly on account of the doubled distance between the sun and the moon, a variation can fall in the time of the conjunction or of the opposition whose greatest amount can reach about a quarter of an hour. For if the equation of the sun amounts to 2 parts and the equation of the moon to 3 parts, then, when one of the two equations is added to the mean course and the other subtracted from it, they make together 5 parts, and twice that is 10 parts, which is the doubled distance; and you find the equation of the anomaly at such a distance, by addition or by subtraction, about a degree and a half, which is about a sixth and an eighth of the excess. When the moon is at the place on the epicycle where its corrected equation must be 3 parts, the share of the degree and a half in it is about an eighth of a part, and that comes, by the gain of the moon, to about a quarter of an hour.
@n The doubled distance is twice the elongation of the moon from the sun; away from the mean conjunctions the anomaly needs a correction that depends on it. Schiaparelli (Nallino's commentary) derives the correction from Ptolemy's lunar model, with the distance 10;19 between the earth and the centre of the eccentric in parts of which the centre of the epicycle is 60 from the earth at the conjunctions: it is 20;38/70;19 = 0.2934 of the elongation, against al-Battānī's 1/6 + 1/8 = 0.2917, and for an elongation of 5 parts it is 1;28. At the mean hourly gain of the moon, 0;30,28, an eighth of a part takes about a quarter of an hour.
@p AB01-PDF1008-L16..AB01-PDF1008-L22
Ptolemy, on the other hand, based the reckoning in this on the greater of the two equations, where the equation of the moon is 5 parts and that of the sun 2 parts and 23 minutes, according to the computation on which Ptolemy worked; the excess between the sun and the moon then comes to 7 parts and 23 minutes, and twice that is about 14 parts 46 minutes. On this reckoning no more than an eighth of an hour need arise from it, as was mentioned. But when the equation of the moon is 5 parts, the share of the one or two parts that is then added to the anomaly of the moon or subtracted from it is only a small thing of no account; it varies more at 3 parts than at 5 parts. For this reason the matter is as we have said.
@n Ptolemy's values are those of *Almagest* VI 5, on the limits of the eclipses, which take 5;1 for the moon, so that the excess is 7;24 (Nallino). Schiaparelli remarks that that problem has nothing to do with this correction, and would read «an eighth of a degree» for «an eighth of an hour», as in the preceding paragraph (Nallino's commentary). The neglected correction matters most where the equation of the moon is about 3 parts: at the greatest equation the equation hardly changes, and near the farthest and the nearest distance on the epicycle the elongation is only the equation of the sun (Schiaparelli). With a greatest equation of 5;1 and an equation of the sun of 2 parts, it changes the equation of the moon by at most about 0;7, where the equation is about 3 parts.
@p AB01-PDF1008-L22..AB01-PDF1007-L05
It is clear also that if we divide the excess between the sun and the moon by the gain of the moon taken with the anomaly that holds between the mean and the true conjunction, that is more correct and more exact. You know it by taking half the excess between the sun and the moon and adding to it half its sixth; then subtract that from the corrected anomaly if the excess is the moon's, and add it to it if the excess is the sun's: so we obtain correctly the anomaly of the moon for the middle between the true and the mean conjunction. With it take the course of the moon in an hour, subtract from it the course of the sun, and use the remaining gain of the moon only in dividing the excess.
@n Half the excess with a twelfth of that half is the motion of the moon, and so nearly of its anomaly, in half the time between the mean and the true conjunction; the twelfth comes from the motion of the sun (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1007-L05..AB01-PDF1007-L18
If you wish to compute the times in another way, by the method of minutes, which is the method by which the day and night are 60 minutes, take the true equal hours of the conjunction after noon in the city and multiply them by 2½ minutes. If the product is 30 minutes, the conjunction is at midnight; if it is less than that, it is before midnight; and if more, after midnight. Put the minutes that you obtain in the place of degrees, so that each minute of them becomes a degree and each second a minute. Then find the times of the hours of the daytime and of the night. If those degrees are less than the times of the hours of the daytime, the conjunction is in the daytime; divide them by a sixth of the times of the hours of the daytime: the result is seasonal hours after noon. If those degrees are more than the times of the hours of the daytime, up to 30, subtract the times of the hours of the daytime from them and divide the remainder by a sixth of the times of the hours of the night: the result is the seasonal hours that pass from the beginning of the night up to midnight. If they are more than 30, cast 30 from them; if the remainder is less than the times of the hours of the night, divide it by a sixth of the times of the hours of the night: the result is seasonal hours after midnight. If the degrees are more than the times of the hours of the night, subtract the times of the hours of the night from them and divide the remainder by a sixth of the times of the hours of the daytime: the result is seasonal hours that have passed from sunrise of the morrow, if God wills.
@n Taken as degrees, the minutes from noon to sunset equal the times of a seasonal hour of the daytime, each being 1¼ times the length of the daytime in equal hours; so the degrees can be compared with those times directly, and a sixth of them is one seasonal hour. The same holds for the night.
