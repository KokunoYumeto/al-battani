Chapter 36. On knowing the true position of the moon on the ecliptic
@p AB01-PDF1041-L19..AB01-PDF1040-L17
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the true position of the moon, in which it is seen on the ecliptic, at any time you wish, find the mean position of the moon and its anomaly for the day that you want and the hour sought, in the way that I showed you, from the four entries and the hours of the computation, and find also the mean position of the sun for that time. Then subtract the mean position of the sun from the mean position of the moon and double the remainder: the result is the doubled distance; if it is more than a circle, cast off a circle from it. Enter with the remainder in the two columns of numbers of the tables of the equation of the moon, and take what stands opposite it in the third column, and also the minutes that stand opposite it in the fourth column; keep each of the two apart, under its own name. That is: what results from the third column is the equation of the anomaly, so write it down under the anomaly; what results from the fourth column is the proportional parts of the difference, so write them down apart. Then look: if the doubled distance with which you found the equation is less than 180 degrees, add what you obtained of the equation of the anomaly to the anomaly, and if it is more than 180 degrees, subtract it from the anomaly. What the anomaly of the moon comes to after the addition or the subtraction is the corrected anomaly. Look for the same number in the columns of numbers of the tables of the equation of the moon again, take what stands opposite it in the fifth column, headed «nearest distance», and write it down under the minutes of the fourth column. Then take also what stands opposite that corrected anomaly in the second column, headed «single equation»: what results is its single equation, so write it down apart. Then find what part of 60 the minutes of the fourth column are, and name it by its ratio, whether a half, a quarter or a third, or less or more than that, and take as much of what you wrote down from the fifth column; always add the result to the single equation that you wrote down, and the result is the compound equation. Add this compound equation to the mean position of the moon if its corrected anomaly exceeds 180 degrees, even by a minute, and subtract it from it if it is less than 180 degrees. What the mean position of the moon comes to after the addition to it or the subtraction from it is the true position of the moon, in which it is seen on the ecliptic. Cast it from the beginning of Aries in the way given before, and you come to the degree and the minute of the sign in which it is, if God wills.
@n The rules are Ptolemy's (*Almagest* V 8). The translation follows Nallino's emendanda (Part II, p. XXXI) in reading «حِدَته», «apart», at p. 112, line 4. The four entries are those of chapter 33, the hours of the computation those of chapter 34. The columns are counted without the columns of numbers (Nallino). The third column holds the arc between the true and the mean farthest distance of the epicycle, the second the single equation, the fifth the increase of the equation at the nearest distance of the eccentric, and the fourth the sixtieths of that increase that apply at a given doubled distance (chapter 30).

Then look: if the corrected anomaly of the moon is less than 180, the moon is increasing in its course, and if it is more, it is decreasing in it. When it is from 0 to 95, its course is less than the mean course; when it is from 95 to 180, its course is more than the mean course; likewise from 180 to 265 its course is more than its mean course, and from 265 to the end of the circle less than the mean course. So too with the sun, in the same way.
@n The true motion equals the mean motion where the moon is on a line from the earth that touches the epicycle, at the anomaly of 90 degrees and the greatest equation, here 5 degrees: hence 95 and 265. For the sun, whose greatest equation is about 2 degrees, the numbers are 92 and 268; and at the quadratures, where the greatest equation of the moon reaches 7;40, they are 97;40 and 262;20 (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
