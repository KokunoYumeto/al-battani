Chapter 49. On knowing the nine configurations that the fixed stars, and some of the wandering ones, have with the sun
@p AB01-PDF0973-L03..AB01-PDF0973-L11
He said: Since the stars, fixed and wandering, revolve in their course about the two poles of the ecliptic [ever since their motion in longitude and latitude has been known], while the right sphere revolves about the two poles of the equator, their rising and their setting on the two sides of the meridian line are always of equal amount at the place of the equator, and no change in their motion has been found. In the spheres inclined to the equator, however, their rising and their setting on the two sides of the meridian line are not equal but differ: the southern ones are later in rising than the northern ones, and likewise they precede them in setting. For this reason none of them that is not on the belt of the signs rises, sets and culminates with one and the same part of the signs, but with different parts; and so their configurations differ, as do those of some of the running stars, which are the moving wandering ones, in the regions of the ecliptic and of the horizon, and with the sun and the moon too. The most evident in force are those that they have with the sun, and they come in nine kinds.
@n Nallino brackets these words as inept; Plato of Tivoli lacks them. The nine configurations are Ptolemy's (*Almagest* VIII 4; Nallino's commentary). *Tashrīq* and *taghrīb*, rendered «easting» and «westing», commonly mean the heliacal rising and setting, the appearance of a star in the east before sunrise and its disappearance in the west after sunset; al-Battānī uses them more widely in this chapter (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0973-L11..AB01-PDF0973-L15
(1) The first kind is the morning easting, when the star is with the sun on the eastern horizon. It is of two sorts: one is called the morning one, when the star is not visible in the east and then rises afterwards, after sunrise; the other is called the morning-eastern, when it rises with the sun in the east. One also speaks of easting when the star rises before the sun.
@p AB01-PDF0973-L15..AB01-PDF0973-L18
(2) The second kind is called the culmination of the easting, when the star, at sunrise, is on the middle of the sky above the earth or below it. This too is of two sorts: one is called the morning one, when it culminates after sunrise; the other is called the morning culminating one, when the star is in the middle of the sky while the sun is in the east.
@p AB01-PDF0973-L18..AB01-PDF0972-L02
(3) The third kind is called the morning westing, when the sun is at the place of rising and the star near the western horizon. This too is of various sorts: the star may be in the west at sunrise, which is called the morning westing, when it sets after the sun rises; the setting of the star may come with the rising of the sun; or it may precede the sun in setting.
@p AB01-PDF0972-L02..AB01-PDF0972-L06
(4) The fourth kind is called the southern easting, when the sun is on the meridian line and the star in the east. It is of various sorts: it may be in the daytime, with the sun in the middle of the sky [so the star is not seen]; or at night, with the sun at the cardine of the earth, and the star is seen on the eastern horizon in those ways, either preceding the sun, or level with it, or behind it, so that it rises after the sun's culmination.
@n The bracketed words are supplied by Nallino after Plato of Tivoli. The cardine of the earth is the lowest point of the ecliptic, below the earth (chapter 35).
@p AB01-PDF0972-L06..AB01-PDF0972-L09
(5) The fifth kind is called the southern culmination, when the sun and the star are in the middle of the sky. It is of two sorts. In one it is daytime and the sun is above the earth, so the star is not seen, or it is night and the sun is with it at the cardine of the earth, so it is not seen either. In the other the sun is at the cardine of the earth and the star in the middle of the sky above the earth, so that it is seen in those ways of preceding, being level and being behind.
@p AB01-PDF0972-L10..AB01-PDF0972-L13
(6) The sixth kind is called the southern west, when the sun is in the middle of the sky and the star in the west. This too is of several sorts: it may be daytime, with the sun in the middle of the sky, so the star is not seen; or night, with the sun at the cardine of the earth, so that it is seen in the ways mentioned of preceding, being level and being behind.
@p AB01-PDF0972-L13..AB01-PDF0972-L16
(7) The seventh kind is called the evening rising, when the sun is in the west and the star on the eastern horizon. It is of several sorts: the sun may set while the star is seen in the east, having risen before it; the star may rise with the setting of the sun, which is called the evening one; or it may be at the point of rising when the sun sets, so that it is not seen until it rises after the sun has set.
@n Nallino's translation adds «not» to the first sort, which Ptolemy calls the earlier evening rising, not visible.
@p AB01-PDF0972-L16..AB01-PDF0972-L19
(8) The eighth kind is called the evening culmination, when the sun is on the western horizon and the star in the middle of the sky above the earth or below it. It is of several sorts: at the setting of the sun it may be above the earth, and so seen, or below the earth, and so not seen; and it may precede in its culmination, or be level, or be behind.
@p AB01-PDF0972-L19..AB01-PDF0972-L22
(9) The ninth kind is called the evening westing, when the star is with the sun on the western horizon. It is of several sorts: the star may be seen in the west before it enters the rays, and set after the sun sets; the star and the sun may be together on the horizon and set together; or the star may precede the sun in its setting, and so move away from it in setting until it appears in the east before the sun.
@p AB01-PDF0972-L22..AB01-PDF0971-L02
We have explained earlier in this book how to know the distances between the stars in their arrangement on the sphere, and shown how to know the parts of the ecliptic with which they rise, set and culminate.
@n Chapters 18, 20 and 26 (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0971-L02..AB01-PDF0971-L09
As for the seeing of the stars that are called fixed, and their disappearing on account of the sun: when the amount at which each of the six kinds of their magnitudes mentioned appears and disappears is known, your computation for it is as we described for the seeing. It is said that the amount of the arc at which the stars of the first magnitude appear and disappear — such as the Yemeni Shiʿrā and the Syrian one, the Heart of the Lion, the two Simāks, the end of the River, and those like them in magnitude — is 15 parts, I mean the arc at which each appears and disappears; these parts are of the times of the equator. For the remaining kinds, below this magnitude, the arc exceeds 15 parts in the order of the magnitude, until it comes to the smallest of them in magnitude, which is seen and disappears at the amount of a sign, approximately.
@n The stars are Sirius, Procyon, Regulus, Spica and Arcturus, and Achernar (Nallino). Plato of Tivoli has 12. Ptolemy gives no such arcs. The 15 parts may come from Autolycus, who gives 15 parts of the ecliptic for all the fixed stars, a book known to the Arabs in Qusṭā ibn Lūqā's translation, corrected by Thābit ibn Qurra (Nallino's commentary). Schiaparelli supposes that earlier Arab astronomers had found by rough observation that stars of the first magnitude appear an hour from the sun, 15 times of the equator, and those of the sixth two hours, 30 times, and set the other magnitudes between (Nallino's commentary). A sign is 30 parts.
