Chapter 25. On finding the part in which a star is, and the latitude of the star, from its distance from the equator and the degree of the signs that culminates with it
@p AB01-PDF1098-L19..AB01-PDF1097-L08
Al-Battānī said: Suppose you wish to find the part in which a star is, and the latitude of the star, from its distance from the equator and what culminates with it, when these are known from measuring the altitude of the star in the middle of the sky and the degree that culminates with it, or from measuring it at the horizon. When the altitude of a star in the middle of the sky has been taken and the difference between it and the altitude of the beginning of Aries in that locality is known, this is its distance from the equator, on the side on which it falls: if the altitude is greater than the altitude of the beginning of Aries, the distance is to the north, and if it is less than the altitude of the beginning of Aries, the distance is to the south. The part that culminates with it at that time is found from the part of the signs that culminates at that hour, by measuring another star whose position is known, or by other measurements with which the part of the middle of the sky can be found. If it can be known only from the rising or setting of the star on the horizon circle and the part that rises or sets with it, [it is found] as we have explained in the preceding chapter, from whatever allows the rising or setting part of the ecliptic and its azimuth on the horizon to be known.
@p AB01-PDF1097-L08..AB01-PDF1096-L01
When you know this, in whichever way you found it, take the declination of the part that culminates with the star and the distance of the star from the equator. If both are on the same side, subtract the smaller from the greater; the remainder is the adjusted distance. Keep it, and find its sine and the sine of what this distance falls short of 90. Then find the sine of the whole inclination and the sine of what the whole inclination falls short of 90. Then subtract the sine of the complement of the whole inclination from 120; the remainder is the longest chord. Then subtract the declination of the part that culminates with the star from 90, find the sine of the remainder, and subtract that too from 120; the remainder is the exceeding chord. Keep all these by their names. Then multiply the sine of the whole inclination by the radius, and divide the product by the sine of what the declination of the part that culminates with the star falls short of 90: the result is the sine of the adjusted inclination; keep it with its name and its direction. Then find its arc, subtract the arc from 90 and find the sine of the remainder: this is the sine of the complement of the adjusted inclination. Then multiply the sine of the adjusted inclination that you kept by the sine of the adjusted distance mentioned earlier in this chapter, and divide the product by the sine of the complement of the adjusted distance. Multiply the quotient by the exceeding chord and divide the result by the longest chord. Multiply the quotient by the sine of the complement of the whole inclination and divide it by the sine of the complement of the adjusted inclination. Multiply the quotient by the sine of the distance of the degree that culminates with the star from the beginning of Cancer or the beginning of Capricorn, whichever it is nearer to on either side, I mean behind it or in front of it, in ascensions of the right sphere. Divide the result by the radius and find the arc of the quotient: the arc you obtain is the variation of the star. Keep it.
@p AB01-PDF1096-L01..AB01-PDF1096-L07
Then look: if the part that culminates with the star is between the beginning of Cancer and the end of Sagittarius, and the distance of the star from the equator is northern, subtract the variation of the star from the times of ascension in the right sphere of the part that culminates with the star; if it is southern, add it to them. If the star is between the beginning of Capricorn and the end of Gemini, and its distance from the equator is northern, reverse this and add the variation of the star to those times; if it is southern, subtract it from them. Find the degree of the signs opposite the times obtained after the addition or the subtraction, in the ascensions of the right sphere: the result is the degree and minute of the signs in which the star is, if God wills.
@p AB01-PDF1096-L08..AB01-PDF1096-L19
If the distance of the star from the equator and the declination of the part that culminates with the star are on different sides, the way to work is to add them together: the sum is the adjusted distance. Multiply the sine of the whole inclination by the sine of the distance of the star from the equator, divide the product by the sine of what the adjusted distance falls short of 90, multiply the quotient by the sine of what the declination of the part that culminates with the star falls short of 90, and divide the product by the radius: the result is the sine of the adjusted inclination. Multiply it by the sine of the distance of the star from the equator as well, divide the product by the sine of the complement of the distance of the star from the equator, multiply the quotient by the exceeding chord, and divide the result by the longest chord: the result is the dividend; keep it with its name. Then find the arc of the sine of the adjusted inclination that you obtained, subtract the arc from 90, take the sine of the remainder and multiply it by the dividend that you kept. Divide the product by the sine of the complement of the whole inclination, and multiply the quotient by the sine of the distance of the part that culminates with the star from the beginning of Cancer or the beginning of Capricorn, whichever it is nearer to in front of it or behind it, in the right sphere. Divide the product by the radius and find the arc of the quotient: the arc you obtain is the variation of the star. Follow the first rule with it, until you know the part of the ecliptic in which the star is.
@n Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary, judges these rules for the longitude false. Schiaparelli suggests that they are not al-Battānī's, but were written to fill a leaf lost from the exemplar from which the Escorial codex and Plato of Tivoli's version both derive; al-Battānī could have found the longitude by inverting the rules of chapter 18. A numerical check bears out the judgement. When the star and its culminating degree are on the same side of the equator, the first rule comes close to the true variation without being exact: for a star at longitude 60° and latitude 10° north it gives 2.48°, against the true 2.45°, and for one at 150° and 25° north 10.63°, against 10.47°. When they are on opposite sides, the second rule is far off: for a star at longitude 200° and latitude 12° north it gives 0.07°, against the true 4.53°.
@p AB01-PDF1096-L19..AB01-PDF1095-L04
If you wish to find the latitude of the star and the direction of its latitude, multiply the sine of the distance of the star from the equator by the sine of what the declination of the degree in which you have found the star falls short of 90, divide the product by the sine of what the whole inclination falls short of 90, and find the arc of the quotient. Then look: if the arc is greater than the declination of the degree in which you found the star, subtract the declination of that degree from it; if the arc is the smaller, subtract it from the declination of that degree. The result after the addition or the subtraction is the latitude of the star. If you wish to know the direction of the latitude, look: if the arc is greater than the declination of the degree in which the star is, the latitude is on the side of that declination; if it is less, the latitude is on the side opposite to the declination of the degree of the star.
@n The «declination» of the degree is here again its second declination β′ (chapter 18). With it the rule is exact (Nallino, after Schiaparelli): sin x = sin δ · cos β′ / cos ε, and the latitude is the difference between x and β′.
