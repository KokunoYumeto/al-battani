Chapter 47. On knowing the latitudes of the five wandering stars
@p AB01-PDF0978-L03..AB01-PDF0978-L13
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the latitudes of the five wandering stars and their sides: if your computation is for Saturn, Jupiter or Mars, enter with the centre of one of them corrected by the third column — for Saturn with 50 added, for Jupiter with 20 subtracted, and for Mars as it is, without addition or subtraction — in the two columns of numbers of the tables of the latitudes of the five wandering stars, which go in steps of six parts, and take what stands opposite it of the minutes of the shares of the latitude, common to all of them, set down at the end of those tables, which is the ninth column of them; write down what results. If the number with which you entered falls in the upper half, which is half of the numbers, from 0 to 90 and from 270 to 360, enter with the corrected anomaly of the star in the two columns of numbers of those tables, take what stands opposite it in the column of the northern latitude of the star, called *afījiyūn* of the north, and take of it the part that the minutes of the shares of the latitude are of sixty. If it falls in the lower half, which is from 90 to 180 and from 180 to 270, take what stands opposite that corrected anomaly in the column of the southern latitude of the star, called *farījiyūn* of the south, and take of it the part that the minutes of the shares are of sixty. What results, whichever of the two sides it is, is the latitude of the star on the side on which you find it.
@n The rules of this chapter are Ptolemy's (*Almagest* XIII 6; Nallino). Adding 50 for Saturn and subtracting 20 for Jupiter counts their centres from the northern limits of their latitudes instead of from their farthest distances. In the codex the column of the shares is the seventh; it would be the ninth in the table of the three upper planets (Nallino). *Afījiyūn* and *farījiyūn* are the Greek *apogeion* and *perigeion*, which al-Battānī took from the Arabic copies of the *Almagest*: in Ptolemy's account the latitude of the three upper planets is northern while the centre of the epicycle is in the half of the eccentric that holds its farthest distance, and southern in the other half (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0978-L13..AB01-PDF0977-L07
If your computation is for Venus or Mercury, enter with their corrected anomaly into those tables, take what stands opposite it in the column of the inclination and the column of the slant, and write down each of them as it is. If your computation is for Venus, leave it as it is. If it is for Mercury alone, and the centre corrected by the third column falls in the upper half mentioned, subtract from the slant alone a tenth of it, I mean one part in ten of it; if it falls in the lower half, add to the slant alone one part in ten of it. What it comes to after the addition or the subtraction is the corrected slant of Mercury; write it down in place of what you obtained from the table, and cancel the first. Then add to the centre corrected by the third column 90 for Venus and 270 for Mercury, and enter with what you have, after casting off the circle if it is more than the circle, into those tables; take what stands opposite it in the ninth column, headed «the shares of the latitude for all». Find what part of sixty the minutes that you obtain are, and take that part of the inclination that you wrote down: the result is the first latitude; keep it. If the number of the centre corrected with the addition for the star, with which you found the minutes of the ninth column, and the corrected anomaly of the star both fall in one half, whichever half it is, upper or lower, the latitude that you wrote down is southern; if they differ, one in one half and the other in the other half, that latitude is northern. Know it with its side.
@n The inclination and the slant are Ptolemy's *enklisis* and *loxōsis*, the two tilts of the epicycles of Venus and Mercury (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0977-L07..AB01-PDF0977-L17
Then take the centre corrected by the third column, for Venus as it is and for Mercury with 180 added, and enter with what you have of the centre, after casting off the circle if it exceeds it, into those tables as well; take what stands opposite it of the minutes of the shares set down in the ninth column, and write them down in two places. Find what part of sixty one of the two places is, and take that part of the slant: the result is the second latitude; write it down. If the number with which you found these minutes fell in the upper half and the corrected anomaly is less than 180, what you obtained of the second latitude is northern, and if the anomaly is more than 180, the latitude is southern; if it fell in the lower half and the anomaly is less than 180, the latitude is southern, and if it is more than 180, it is northern. Then take of the minutes that you wrote down in the other place their sixth, if your computation is for Venus, which is always northern, and their half and quarter, if your computation is for Mercury, which is always southern. If these three latitudes fall on one side, add them together; if they differ, cast the smaller from the greater and find the side of the remainder: that is the latitude that results for the star, on the side on which it falls.
@n The four sides of the second latitude are Nallino's correction; the codex has them the other way round (*Almagest* XIII 6). A sixth and three quarters of the sixty minutes are 0;10 and 0;45, Ptolemy's greatest deviations of Venus to the north and of Mercury to the south. Ibn Yūnus counts an error in the side of the latitude of Mercury among al-Battānī's mistakes; Nallino finds none in the rules or the tables, which agree with Ptolemy's, and suggests that Ibn Yūnus had a faulty copy, like the codex here (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0977-L17..AB01-PDF0977-L20
Know that the whole latitude of Saturn to the north, as Ptolemy found it, is 3;2, and to the south 3;5; the whole latitude of Jupiter to the north is 2;4 and to the south 2;8; the whole latitude of Mars to the north is 4;21 and to the south 7;7; the whole latitude of Venus, to the north and to the south equally, is 8;56, and the whole latitude of Mercury, to the north and to the south equally, is 4;18 as well.
@n Nallino restores these values from Ptolemy's tables (*Almagest* XIII 5) and from Theon's canon; the codex has several of them wrong, among them 8;2 and 33;5 for Saturn and 6 for both latitudes of Mars, and writes the last two without dots.
@p AB01-PDF0977-L20..AB01-PDF0976-L04
If you wish to know whether the star is ascending or descending on the side on which it is, compute its latitude after ten days. If it is in the north and you find that its latitude has increased, it is ascending, and if you find that it has decreased, it is descending; if its latitude is in the south and you find it increased, it is descending, and if you find it decreased, it is ascending. If it was in the north and you find that it has passed to the south, you know that it was descending in the north; if it was in the south and you find that it has passed to the north, you know that it was ascending in the south.
@n Theon's canon has similar rules (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0976-L04..AB01-PDF0976-L08
Saturn, Jupiter and Mars have another way by which this is known for them: if the latitude of one of them is in the north and its anomaly is less than 180, it is ascending, and if it is more than 180, it is descending; if its latitude is in the south and its anomaly is less than 180, it is descending, and if it is more than 180, it is ascending. As for Venus and Mercury, because of the speed of their motion around the sun, although their greatest latitude comes at their conjunction with it, it is difficult to know their state except in the way given before.
@n «Although» follows Nallino's translation; the text is faulty here (Nallino). «Around the sun» describes the apparent motion of Venus and Mercury to either side of the sun, as in the like phrases of the Greek and Latin writers (Nallino's commentary, after Schiaparelli). Their greatest latitudes come at their conjunctions below the sun (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's note).
