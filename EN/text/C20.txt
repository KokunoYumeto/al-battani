Chapter 20. On finding the degree of the ecliptic with which a star rises and the one with which it sets, in every locality
@n The print has «الّني» for «الّتي» four times on p. 50 (lines 1, 6, 10 and 12), a dot missing.
@p AB01-PDF1103-L13..AB01-PDF1103-L21
He said: If you wish to find the degree of the signs with which a star rises and the one with which it sets, take half the arc of the day of the star and half the arc of the day of the degree that culminates with it, and find the difference between them: this is half the difference of the two days; keep it. Then look: if half the arc of the day of the star is greater than half the arc of the day of the degree that culminates with it, subtract half the difference of the two days from the times of ascension opposite the part that culminates with the star in the given climate; if half the arc of the day of the star is the smaller, add half the difference of the two days to those times. The times of the part that culminates with the star in the climate, after the addition or the subtraction, are the times of ascension of the degree with which the star rises in that locality. Find the degree of the signs opposite them in the ascensions of the climate: it is the part with which the star rises in that locality, of the sign in which the number of times fell.
@p AB01-PDF1103-L21..AB01-PDF1102-L09
If you wish to find the degree with which that star sets, take the times opposite the degree that is the counterpart of the degree culminating with the star, in that climate. Then look: if half the arc of the day of the star is greater than half the arc of the day of the degree that culminates with it, add half the difference of the two days to those times; if half the arc of the day of the star is the smaller, subtract half the difference of the two days from those times. The times after the addition or the subtraction are the times of ascension of the degree opposite to the degree with which the star sets. Take the degree of the signs that stands opposite them in the ascensions of the climate: it is the degree opposite to the degree with which the star sets. Find the counterpart of that degree: it is the degree of the ecliptic that sets with the setting of the star. It is known that if the star has no latitude, its passage through the middle of the sky does not differ [from that of its degree], and it rises and sets with the part of the ecliptic in which it is.
@p AB01-PDF1102-L09..AB01-PDF1102-L15
Or, if you wish, add the whole arc of the day of the star to the times of ascension, in the climate, of the degree with which it rises; the sum is the ascension of the degree that is the counterpart of the degree with which it sets. Find the degree of the signs that corresponds to it: the star sets with the counterpart of that degree. You can also find the degree with which the star rises and the one with which it sets in another way. Take the times of ascension in the right sphere of the part that culminates with the star, and both add half the arc of the day of the star to them and subtract half its arc of the day from them. The one added to is the ascension of the counterpart of the degree with which it sets, and the one subtracted from is the ascension of the degree with which it rises, in the climate. Take the degrees of the signs opposite them according to the rule.
@n The ascensions in the right sphere are counted from the beginning of Capricorn (chapter 5), so that they can be entered directly in the table of the climate.
