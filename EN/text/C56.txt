Chapter 56. On making a horizontal and a vertical instrument, with each of which the seasonal hours that have passed of the day are known in every town; it is also called the sundial
@p AB01-PDF0949-L07..AB01-PDF0949-L16
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the seasonal hours that have passed of the day, from sunrise to sunset, with the horizontal instrument, from the sun's shadow on its surface, take a slab of marble or a plate of copper with a level surface and a smooth face, of any size you like; it is best that its width be two thirds of its length. Mark a point at two thirds of the width, on the middle of the length; take it as a centre and draw about it a circle of any size you like. Then divide the circle into quarters by two lines that cut each other at its centre at right angles and divide the circle into equal quarters, and divide each quarter into 90 parts, exactly, at intervals of a degree or more, as the size of the circle allows. Then find the shadow of the beginning of Cancer and of the head of Capricorn for 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 seasonal hours, and the azimuth of the shadow at each of these hours on the circle of the horizon, by the methods given to you at the beginning of the book, in the chapter on knowing the azimuth of the shadow and of the altitude for the parts of the signs in every town: that is, find the altitude of each of these hours, and from it find its shadow and its azimuth in the manner given before, in any town you like.
@n «Sundial» renders «al-rukhāma», literally «the marble slab». The chapter on the azimuth of the shadow and of the altitude is chapter 11 (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0949-L16..AB01-PDF0948-L09
Then take a ruler with straight edges, one of whose faces is divided into as many equal parts as you like, provided they are as many as the number of the shadow of the head of Capricorn or more. Put the first point, from which you began, on the edge of the ruler, on the point of the centre of the circle, and lay the edge of the ruler along the azimuth of the shadow of one hour of the hours of Capricorn, towards the wide side of the slab, beginning the count of the azimuth from the point of the east on the circumference of the circle. Then count off on the ruler, from the point of the centre, as many parts as the shadow of the first hour, and mark there, at the edge of the ruler, a point, which is the mark of the shadow of one hour. Do the same for the shadow of two hours and the azimuth of two hours, and of three, four and five, until you come to six hours, when you mark the place of the shadow on the line that cuts between the north and the south, on the wide side, which is the meridian line. Then turn the ruler to the other quarter, next to the meridian line, and do in it as you did in the quarter before, so that the shadows of one hour, two, three, four and five fall on both sides of the meridian line, on the wide side of the slab, towards the east and the west, for the beginning of Capricorn; mark a point at the shadow of each hour. Then do the same with the azimuths of the hours of the head of Cancer, putting their shadows on the other, narrow, side of the slab, as you did with the hours of Capricorn, on both sides of the meridian line, until the shadow of the end of the sixth hour falls on the meridian line.
@p AB01-PDF0948-L09..AB01-PDF0948-L16
It is known that when the azimuth is northern, it lies towards the narrow side of the slab from the line between the east and the west, and when it is southern, towards the wide side of this line. Then join the points marked for the hours of the head of Cancer and of the head of Capricorn with straight lines, running from the point of the first hour of the hours of Cancer to the point of the first hour of the hours of Capricorn, and likewise from the point of two hours to the point of two hours, up to the end of the five hours on both sides of the meridian line. Likewise join all the points of the hours of Capricorn to one another, and the points of the hours of Cancer, by lines across the slab, which end on both sides, from the point of the first hour to the sixth, marked on the meridian line; so that the place of the shadow is a known passage on the slab, which it does not go beyond.
@n «Narrow» is Nallino's reading here and below; the codex has «الصيفية», «summer», for «الضيّقة» in several places.
@p AB01-PDF0948-L16..AB01-PDF0947-L07
Then set up at the centre of the circle on the slab a style of copper or iron, round, turned on the lathe, with a pointed head, and let what shows of it above the surface of the slab be 12 parts of the parts of your ruler with which you took the amounts of the shadow. Check this style with the compasses, from the sides of the circle to its pointed end, so that you know that it stands truly upright on the centre. Make the hole that you bore for the style, at the place of the centre, go through to the other side of the slab, so that the end of the style that enters the hole is fastened from the other side firmly, so that it neither shakes nor moves. Then make the wide side of the slab its northern side and the narrow side its southern side, so that the point of the north falls on the meridian line on the wide side and the point of the south on the meridian line on the narrow side, and the point of the east and the point of the west fall in their places on the line at right angles to the meridian line, which is the line between the east and the west. Begin the hours from the side of the west, in the wide part of the slab, and write the first hour, the second and the third under each of the points of the hours of Capricorn, up to the end of the eleventh. With the dial one cannot know more than the time from the end of the first hour of the day to the end of the eleventh, because of the length and spread of the shadow at the two ends of the day, for which a very large instrument would be needed to receive the surface of the shadow. If you wish to divide between the hours into halves and thirds, more or less, find the azimuth and the shadow of each fraction that falls between those hours and mark it as you wish; that is not difficult.
@p AB01-PDF0947-L07..AB01-PDF0947-L15
When you have finished making the dial, go to a place where the horizon lies open from the first hour of the day to the end of the eleventh; draw a circle there and find in it the meridian line in the way described at the beginning of this book. Then lay the meridian line drawn on the dial on the meridian line that you found with the circle, fitting it exactly, neither inclined nor turned aside, so that the southern direction of the dial, which is the narrow side, faces the south of the line along its direction, and the wide, northern, side accordingly lies along the meridian line towards the north. Let the upper surface of the dial be parallel to the plane of the horizon, levelled with the plumb line and inclined to no side. Then from the place where the end of the style's shadow falls on the lines of the hours, the seasonal hours that have passed of the day are known in every town whose latitude is the same as the latitude for which you made the dial.
@n The meridian line is found in chapter 12 (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0947-L15..AB01-PDF0946-L04
The setting of the dial can also be adjusted another way. Find the altitude whose azimuth has no inclination, in the way that I explained to you at the beginning of the book; then watch the altitude, and when it has come to the amount of the altitude you worked out [for it], turn the dial until the shadow of the style falls on the line between the east and the west. When that is right, the setting of the dial is right, provided its face is levelled and not inclined. If you like, find the altitude of one hour, or of two or three: when you know it by computation, watch the shadow, and when the altitude has come to the one you want, turn the dial until the shadow of the style falls on the line of the hour for which you found the altitude. You can also find the azimuth of the altitude you want; then watch the altitude, and when it is equal to the altitude whose azimuth you found, turn the dial until the shadow of the style falls at the amount of the azimuth of that altitude on the circle drawn. If the shadow does not reach the circumference of the circle, tie a thin thread to the foot of the style and stretch it out to the amount of the azimuth from the limit of the east or of the west, on the side on which it is at the time of the observation; then turn the dial until the middle of the shadow of the style falls on that thread. Then the dial is set right, and the line of the sixth hour falls in line with the meridian line, along its direction, if God wills.
@n The altitude whose azimuth has no inclination is the altitude of the sun when it stands due east or due west, where its azimuth, counted from the east and west points, is nothing (chapter 12; Nallino). The bracketed words are Nallino's conjecture.
@p AB01-PDF0946-L04..AB01-PDF0946-L19
If you wish to find from this chapter the azimuth of Mecca, which is the azimuth of the qibla for prayer, so as to draw a line along it from the centre of the circle, the line being the azimuth of the qibla in that town, find the latitude of the town in which you are and the latitude of Mecca, and find the direction of Mecca, God guard it, from that town, whether it is north of it or south. Find the longitude of Mecca and the longitude of the town and subtract the smaller from the larger, so that you know the amount between them in longitude, and where Mecca lies from that town, towards the east or towards the west: if the longitude of Mecca is greater than the longitude of the town set down in the tables of the latitudes and longitudes of the towns, Mecca is east of the town, and if it is less, Mecca is west of the town. Then put the end of the ruler at the number of the latitude between them, counting it from the line of the east towards the side on which Mecca lies in latitude, and likewise from the line of the west towards that side, on the circumference of the circle, so that the edge of the ruler passes at the amount of the latitude between them; and draw along the edge of the ruler a line joining the eastern mark and the western. Take also the difference between them in longitude and count as much on the circumference of the circle from the meridian line towards the side on which Mecca lies in longitude, on the southern part of the circumference, and count as much again on its northern part; put the edge of the ruler on the two marks and draw along its edge a straight line. Where these two lines cut each other is the place of Mecca in its azimuth from that town. Put the edge of the ruler on the centre of the circle and on the place of the intersection, and draw along it a straight line, carrying it out on the dial to the southern circumference of the circle: that line is the azimuth of the qibla in that town.
@n The qibla is the direction of the Kaʿba at Mecca, towards which Muslims turn in prayer (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0946-L19..AB01-PDF0945-L03
If you wish to know the amount of the azimuth of the qibla by computation, take the chord of what lies between the two towns in longitude and the chord of what lies between them in latitude; multiply each of them by itself, add the two, and take the root of the sum: the result is the hypotenuse of the triangle, which subtends the right angle, and it is the distance between the centre of the circle and the place of the intersection made by the lines of the longitude and the latitude in the circle. Keep it. Then go back to the chord of what lies between the two towns in latitude, multiply it by the half diameter and divide by the hypotenuse of the triangle; turn the result into an arc: the arc that results is the azimuth of Mecca. Count as much on the circumference of the circle from the point of the azimuth of the east or of the west, according to the place of Mecca from that town in longitude, towards the side on which Mecca lies in latitude; where it ends, make a mark on the circumference of the circle, and draw a straight line from the centre of the circle to that mark: that line is the azimuth of Mecca from that town.
@n The chords here must be half chords, sines, as in the construction, where the two lines stand at the sines of the two differences from the line of east and west and from the meridian line; Nallino's translation has sines. With a the azimuth from the east or the west point, the rule is sin a = sin Δφ / √(sin² Δφ + sin² Δλ), Δφ and Δλ being the differences of latitude and longitude; the construction gives the same line. This is not the azimuth of the great circle to Mecca. Nallino explains the simplification as meant for the builders of mosques, for whom an error of a few degrees did not matter (Nallino's commentary). With al-Battānī's coordinates, al-Raqqa at 73;15 of longitude and 36;0 of latitude and Mecca at 77;53 and 21;40, the rule gives 71;56 from the east towards the south and the great circle 73;1. Nallino puts the error at 3° or 4° in Syria and Mesopotamia; with the positions of the towns as now known it is about 1° there, and less than 1½° at every place between 30° and 37° north within 12° of longitude of Mecca.

@p AB01-PDF0945-L05..AB01-PDF0944-L14
Al-Battānī said: Take a wide, oblong, four-sided marble slab and mark its corners A B G D; take a centre at two thirds of its width and the middle of its length, marked H, draw a circle about it and divide it into quarters by two lines that cut each other at right angles, carried out to the edges of the slab. Make the one, longer, line, which runs along the length of the slab, the line between the east and the west, and the shorter line, which runs across the width of the slab, the line between the north and the south, and write on it «meridian line»; write the directions of the horizon at the ends of the lines. Make the beginning of the azimuth on the circumference of the circle at the two points of the east and the west on the longer line: what of it is southern we count towards the north, and what is northern we count towards the south, after dividing each quarter of the circle into 90 parts in black or in red, so that it leaves no lasting trace on the face of the slab; and the circle likewise. As for the two diameters of the circle, which are the two lines mentioned, we draw them by cutting, so that their trace remains on the surface of the slab. On each of the azimuths of the hours of Cancer we write the mark M, and on the azimuth of each of the hours of Capricorn the mark L, and at the place of the shadow of each hour something by which its number is known, beginning from the side of the west; and we join the points along the length and the width of the slab with the lines between the figure of the hours and their shadows on it. We put Mecca in the region of the east and the south, and mark on the arc between them in latitude M K; we take as much on the side of the west and draw through the two marks K a line parallel to the line of the east and the west. We mark on the arc between them N Ḍ, and at the place where the two lines cut each other LA, and draw the line H LA ʿ, which is the azimuth of Mecca. We make the length of the style from the mark H — it is the line H S that shows — and set it upright on the centre H. That is what we wished to show. We have made tables of the azimuths of the hours of Capricorn and of Cancer, of their shadows and of their altitudes, for a latitude of 36 degrees.
@n The text is defective after «N Ḍ»: the codex and Plato of Tivoli lack the marking of the difference in longitude and the line through it parallel to the meridian line, which Nallino's translation supplies from the rule above (Nallino). The tables are on f. 240r of the codex; the tables for latitudes of 36° and 38° that Plato of Tivoli has here seem to be the work of a reader of that version, and do not agree with al-Battānī's (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0944-L14..AB01-PDF0944-L22
The making of the vertical dial, whose upright face faces the south, is by this same method in the azimuth; only the shadows change, in the way that we have described in the knowledge of the vertical shadow. When you have finished the dial with the amounts of the vertical shadow and set its face upright on the line of the east and the west, the face of the dial looks to the south, lying across between the east and the west, with the wide side towards the earth and the narrow side upwards. It is known that the longest shadow on this dial is at the head of Cancer and the shortest at the head of Capricorn. Let the style here too be 12 parts of the parts of the ruler by which the shadow is measured; then from the place where the end of the shadow falls on the lines of the hours you know all the seasonal hours that have passed of the day. The hours are known in many ways and with various instruments, but these two instruments are the most correct of those that I have worked with, and the safest for this knowledge, if God wills.
@n The vertical shadow is that of chapter 10. Taken as written, the rule for the vertical dial is not right. On a wall facing south, a style 12 parts long at right angles to the wall casts the end of its shadow 12 tan A parts sideways and 12 tan h / cos A parts down from its foot, for the sun at altitude h and at azimuth A from the south; the rule puts it 12 tan h parts from the foot, in the direction of the azimuth. The two agree only when the sun is on the meridian: with the sun at 30° of altitude and 45° from the south, the rule gives a shadow of 6.9 parts at 45° below the level, the wall one of 15.5 parts at 39° below it. «The safest» renders Nallino's reading «أسلمه»; Nallino also considers «أسهله», «the easiest», for the codex's «اسلهه». Plato of Tivoli lacks the passage (Nallino).
