Chapter 52. On knowing what the astrologers have reported in saying that the sphere has a motion of displacement, advancing and retreating, and on the evident falsity of what they say
@p AB01-PDF0962-L11..AB01-PDF0962-L17
Al-Battānī said: It is mentioned in Ptolemy's book that the astrologers claimed that the sphere has a motion of displacement, slow in time, one degree in every 80 years, and they said that this motion goes as far as 8 parts, advancing and then retreating. What they mean is that the sphere of the signs moves 8 parts from west to east, in the same direction as the motion of the sphere of the fixed stars, and then moves 8 parts from east to west, contrary to the first motion. Yet at the same time it must move with the motion of the fixed stars, the first motion, which is from west to east; and this cannot be, nor can it come about, unless something other than it moves it, or else the fixed stars are what move upon it; for one body cannot move with two motions in two different directions at once.
@n Not Ptolemy but Theon of Alexandria, in the fourth century, reports this doctrine of «some of the ancient astrologers» in a work on Ptolemy's Handy Tables, adding that Ptolemy does not hold it, since without it the equinoxes computed from the tables agree with those found with instruments. Theon's remark probably means that Ptolemy passed over the doctrine in silence; al-Battānī took it to mean that Ptolemy had mentioned and refuted it (Nallino's commentary). «The astrologers» renders «aṣḥāb al-ṭilsamāt», literally «the people of the talismans», which here stands for the Greek apotelesmatikoi, the astrologers (Nallino). The doctrine is known as the trepidation of the fixed stars.
@p AB01-PDF0962-L17..AB01-PDF0961-L02
They stated that the end of the advance was 128 Egyptian years before the reign of Augustus, which is the year 166 of Alexander the Macedonian, and that one must take the years after that and reckon one degree for every 80 years of them; what results from that [is subtracted] until it reaches 8 degrees, and what remains is added to the motions of the stars computed before. When the 8 are complete, they are dropped, and what exceeds 8 is taken as it stands and added to the positions of the stars until 8 are complete; then the first procedure is repeated.
@n The era of Alexander the Macedonian is the era from Alexander's death, the era of Philip (chapter 32). In Ptolemy's canon of kings the reign of Augustus begins when 294 years of this era are complete, and 294 − 128 = 166. The bracketed words are supplied by Nallino. In Theon's account the degrees found are subtracted from 8, and the remainder is added to the positions of the sun, the moon and the five planets computed from the tables (Nallino's commentary). «Computed before» renders «al-mutaqaddima», Nallino's reading for the codex's «al-muqaddama»; Nallino's translation renders the words as «the direct motion of the stars».
@p AB01-PDF0961-L02..AB01-PDF0961-L10
The length of the year on which those who held this view worked was greater than 365 days and a quarter of a day by a fifth of an hour or about that; for that reason the mean motion of the sun in the Egyptian year comes to 359;44,43. Hipparchus, who came after them, worked with a year of 365 days and a quarter of a day only, so that the motion of the sun in the time of the Egyptian year comes to 359;45,13; and Hipparchus claimed to have established that it is less than the quarter of a day. Then Ptolemy observed 285 years after Hipparchus and found the length of the year, on which Ptolemy worked, to be 365 days and less than a quarter of a day by one part in 300, so that the motion of the sun in the Egyptian year came to 359;45,25. We observed 743 years after Ptolemy and found the length of the year to be 365 days and less than a quarter of a day by 3 parts and two fifths of a part of 360, so that the motion of the sun in the Egyptian year became 359;45,46.
@n The translation follows Nallino's emendanda (Part II, p. XXXI) in reading «او نحوه», «or about that», for «ونحوه» (p. 191, line 3). The year of the astrologers, 365 days 6 hours 12 minutes, is the year that chapter 27 ascribes to some of the ancient people of Egypt and Babylon; Letronne and Martin show that these astrologers lived after Hipparchus, perhaps before Ptolemy (Nallino's commentary). Ptolemy's year is 365 days 5 hours 55 minutes 12 seconds, al-Battānī's 365 days 5 hours 46 minutes 24 seconds (Nallino). The 285 and the 743 years are the intervals between the autumnal equinoxes observed by Hipparchus, Ptolemy and al-Battānī (chapter 27; the codex has 943 for 743). The four motions are 360 parts multiplied by 365 days and divided by the four years; for the first three the codex has 359;44,48, 359;41,53 and 359;44,25, which Nallino corrects.
@p AB01-PDF0961-L10..AB01-PDF0961-L15
All these motions have been increasing since the time of Nabonassar or thereabouts. So it is refuted that what has been described agrees with anything in their statement: not in the number of the parts, not in the amount of the motion, and not in the increase and the decrease. Rather, we see this increase come without order in its slowness and speed: Ptolemy corrected Hipparchus by nearly a day in about 300 years, and we have corrected Ptolemy by 4 days and a quarter in about 750 years, besides the day by which Ptolemy had corrected Hipparchus.
@n «What has been described» renders «what they described»; Nallino's translation takes the sentence to say that what the astrologers held about the number of degrees, the amount of the motion and the increase and decrease is void. Nallino's translation has «about 4½ days»; neither Nallino's notes nor the corrigenda mention the difference from the Arabic. By chapter 27, al-Battānī's year is shorter than Ptolemy's by 2;12 parts of a day of 360 parts, which in 743 years make 4.54 days; Ptolemy's year is shorter than 365 days and a quarter by 1/300 of a day, which in 285 years make 0.95 of a day.
@p AB01-PDF0961-L15..AB01-PDF0961-L19
If this excess has come about only from an error in the instruments with which the observations were made, from their division or from their changing over a long time, then it must of necessity occur in our observations after a period of time, since in our observations we measure only against those observations. And if it comes from a motion in the sphere whose true nature has not become clear to us, and which neither we nor any of those before us have grasped, then seeking the truth and following it means observing at every time, so that whatever is found and caught at that time is set right, as it was set right in the time before.
@n Nallino suggests that «خطأٌ», «an error», may be missing after «occur»; the Latin translation has «errors will of necessity befall our observations».
@p AB01-PDF0961-L19..AB01-PDF0960-L05
As for what may be conjectured, and what the measurements require: since this excess is common to all the motions of the stars, it has come about only from the motion of the sphere of the fixed stars. For Ptolemy stated that this motion, by what was found from Ptolemy's own observations and also by what was worked with before, is one degree in every 100 years; but between Ptolemy's observations and the observations they were compared with there was not a period long enough for an evident change in such a motion to appear, for between the observation that Ptolemy made and the observation it was compared with there are only about 200 years. As the time between us and Ptolemy has grown long, the increase in this motion has become evident, so that it has been found to be one degree in every 66 solar years; and it is from the difference between these two motions that the excess in all of them has come.
@n «Require» follows Nallino's reading «يوجبه»; the codex has «يوحيه», «suggests». Nallino suggests reading 280, the interval between Hipparchus and Ptolemy, for the 200 years of the codex and of Plato of Tivoli. The rates of one degree in 100 years and one degree in 66 years differ by 18.5″ a year, against the 21.7″ by which al-Battānī's motion of the sun in an Egyptian year exceeds Ptolemy's (chapter 27).
