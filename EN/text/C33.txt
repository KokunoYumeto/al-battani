Chapter 33. On knowing the mean position of the sun and its true position, in which it is seen on the ecliptic, by the eras of the Romans and the Arabs
@p AB01-PDF1046-L03..AB01-PDF1045-L04
He said: If you wish to know the position of the sun on the ecliptic by the era of the Romans, take the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, and do not count the broken year that you are in until the last day of Subāṭ has run out, at noon on it; then count it. Look for the same number of years as you have in the column of numbers of the tables of the collected Roman years, which go in steps of twenty years. Wherever you find the same number, or the nearest one below it, take what stands opposite it of the degrees, minutes and seconds set down in the column of the mean motion of the sun, and write them down. Then subtract the years that you found in the table from the years that you had: what remains is single years. Look for the same number in the column of numbers of the tables of the single Roman years, take again what stands opposite it in the column of the mean motion of the sun, of degrees, minutes and seconds, and write each kind under its kind, under what you wrote first. Then look in the table of the Roman months for the name of the complete Roman month before the month that you are in, take what stands opposite it in the column of the mean motion of the sun, and write those degrees, minutes and seconds under what you wrote before, each kind under its kind. Then enter with the number of the days that have passed of the Roman month that you are in into the table of the days of the column of the mean motion of the sun, take again what stands opposite it of degrees, minutes and seconds, and write them with what you wrote from the three places mentioned before. Then begin by collecting the seconds and add them up; take 60 from them, reckoning a minute for each time you take it, and carry that to the minutes, and write what remains below 60 under the seconds. Then add up the minutes, with what has been carried to them from the division of the seconds; take 60 from the sum, reckoning a degree for each time you take it, and carry that to the degrees, and write the minutes that remain below 60 under the minutes. Then add up the degrees, with what has been carried to them from the division of the minutes. If the sum is more than one circle, or several circles, the amount of a circle being 360 degrees, cast off the circles from it and write what remains below 360 under the degrees. The degrees, minutes and seconds that result are the mean motion of the sun collected from the four entries; it is the position of the sun by its mean course on the ecliptic, from the beginning of Aries. Cast off 30 from the degrees for each sign; what remains below that is the degrees, minutes and seconds that the sun has traversed of the sign that it has not completed, the one that does not make up 30.
@n The collected years (*al-sinūn al-majmūʿa*) are tabulated in steps of twenty years, the Arab ones in steps of thirty; the single years (*al-mabsūṭa*, «spread out») run from one upwards. The years of the tables begin on 1 Ādhār, March, so that the leap day is the last day of the year; the mean motions at the epochs are for noon on 1 Ādhār (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF1045-L04..AB01-PDF1045-L10
If you wish to know the mean position of the sun by the years of the Arabs, take the years of the Hijra together with the year that you are in and enter them in the tables of the motion of the sun, in the column of the collected years of the Arabs, which go in steps of thirty; look for the same number or the nearest one below it, with the number of the single years, and follow the procedure given before, which I set out with the years of the Romans and their months. What is collected of the motion of the sun from the four entries, after the circles are cast off, is the mean position of the sun; it comes to the same whichever of the two eras you have worked with. Let this description be a model for you in finding the mean positions of the other stars and their arguments.
@p AB01-PDF1045-L10..AB01-PDF1045-L17
When you know the mean position of the sun, subtract its farthest distance from its mean position: its anomaly remains, so write it down under the mean position of the sun. Then enter with the anomaly of the sun into the tables of the equation of the sun, in the two columns of numbers, take the degrees, minutes and seconds that stand opposite it in the first column after the two columns of numbers, headed «equation of the sun», and write them under the anomaly. Then look: if the anomaly of the sun with which you took the equation is less than 180 degrees, subtract the equation from the mean position itself, and if this anomaly is more than 180, add the equation to the mean position. What results after the addition or the subtraction is the true position of the sun, in which it is seen on the ecliptic. Cast it from the beginning of Aries, giving each sign 30 degrees, and the count stops at the second of the minute of the degree of the sign in which it is.
@p AB01-PDF1045-L17..AB01-PDF1045-L23
This equation is for the time of noon at the city of al-Raqqa on the day for which you have computed. If there are minutes with the anomaly, take the equation that stands opposite the complete degree and keep it, and find the difference between it and the equation that stands opposite the degree one above it. Take of the result in the proportion of those minutes to 60, and subtract what you obtain from the equation that you kept if that one is the greater, or add it to it if it is the less. The equation opposite the complete degrees that you kept, after the addition or the subtraction, is the exact equation for that anomaly. Let this be a model for you to work with for all the minutes that you find with the anomaly of the moon and of the stars, if God wills.
@n Al-Battānī begins the day at noon, as the astronomers do (chapter 29), not at sunset (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1045-L23..AB01-PDF1044-L07
The farthest distance of the sun was, in the year 1191 of Dhū l-Qarnayn, on the first day of Ādhār, at about Gemini 22;15, which is 82;15 from the beginning of Aries. If you wish to compute the position of the sun for before this year or after it, find the difference between this year and the year for which you wish to compute, and take one degree for each 66 Roman years. Subtract the degrees and minutes that result from 82;15 if the time of the computation is before the year mentioned, and add them to it if it is after it; the distance of the sun that results after the addition or the subtraction is its farthest distance in the year for which you compute. For its farthest distance moves with the motion of the sphere of the fixed stars, which is, as we found by observation, one degree in each 66 Roman years; that is also about one degree in each 68 lunar years. If your computation is by the era of the Arabs, carry it out as I have described for you.
@n 1 March 880 (Nallino). In chapter 28 al-Battānī found the farthest distance at Gemini 22;17 from his observations of the year 1194. One degree in 66 years is 54 seconds 33 thirds a year (chapter 51; the modern value is about 50 seconds), and 66 Roman years of 365¼ days are 68 lunar years of 354 11/30 days.
