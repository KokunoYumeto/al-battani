Chapter 39. On knowing the parallax that occurs in the moon in longitude and latitude, and the cause from which it occurs, and on knowing it by computation and by the table
@p AB01-PDF1038-L11..AB01-PDF1037-L07
Al-Battānī said: The parallax of the moon is the amount by which the position in which it is seen by measurement differs from the position in which it really is, which is given by computation. For the size of the earth relative to the sphere of the moon is greater than relative to the spheres of the other stars, because the moon is near it, and [it grows less] until the ecliptic is reached, relative to which the size of the earth is like a point. Since the centre of the earth is the centre of the ecliptic, which is the place of true sight, the sight differs [only] by the difference between the centre of the earth and its surface, which is the place from which the eyes see, and the amount of that is the radius of the earth. For this reason the parallax is greater in the moon than in the others and more evident to the senses. It occurs, and varies as it occurs, from two different causes: one is the variation of the distance of the moon from the earth, and the second the variation of its distance from the point of the zenith in the regions of the sphere, on the circle of altitude, which passes through the pole of the horizon, that is, the zenith, and through the moon and the horizon. When the place where this circle cuts [the ecliptic] is half way between the setting degree of the ecliptic and [the ascendant], at a distance of 90 from the ascendant, which is half the semicircle, half of the 180, and the amount of a right angle of the four angles of the sphere, the parallax then falls in latitude alone, not in longitude. This cannot happen on the meridian line except at two points of the ecliptic, the two solstitial points, I mean the beginning of Cancer and the beginning of Capricorn. For when either of them is on the meridian line, one of the two equinoctial points, I mean the beginning of Aries and the beginning of Libra, is on the eastern horizon and the other on the western horizon, everywhere on the earth, and so each of the four angles is a right angle; the amount of a right angle is 90 parts.
@n The text has «where the point of this circle» and, before it, «until it passes» for «which passes»; the translation follows the readings that Nallino proposes.
@p AB01-PDF1037-L07..AB01-PDF1037-L14
As for the other parts of the sphere, when they are on the meridian line their distances from the horizon differ, being more or less than 90, and the angles differ, growing smaller or larger. When one of the parts of the ecliptic between the beginning of Cancer and the end of Sagittarius is on the meridian line, the place half way between the rising and the setting degree of the ecliptic appears in all the climes inclined from the meridian line towards the west; when one of them there is between the beginning of Capricorn and the end of Gemini, the place half way between the ascendant and the setting point inclines towards the east. In these two inclinations the parallax on the meridian line falls in longitude and latitude together. So too in the other regions of the sphere, when the distance of the degree in question from the ascendant is more or less than 90, since the angle is then less than a right angle.
@n The translation reads «appears» (يظهر) with Nallino for «rises» (يطلع); «all the climes» means our climes, north of the tropic (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1037-L14..AB01-PDF1037-L20
The ratio of the parallax in latitude to the parallax in longitude is as the ratio of the sine of the angle to the sine of what remains of it to a right angle, and the squares of the two parallaxes added together are equal to the square of the parallax between the point of the zenith and the moon on the circle of altitude. The inclination of the parallax in latitude is always towards the side of the degree that this circle cuts, when it is the circle of the middle of the sky, from the point of the zenith; and the inclination of the parallax in longitude is towards the side of the horizon to which the degree of the moon inclines, I mean that the moon is inclined, being nearer, towards that horizon, the eastern or the western, rather than towards the other.
@n With A the angle between the ecliptic and the circle of altitude through the moon and p the parallax in altitude, the parallax in latitude is p sin A and that in longitude p cos A (Nallino, after *Almagest* V 19). The parallax in latitude falls on the side to which the arc from the zenith through the moon points when it is continued beyond the ecliptic; the «middle of the sky» here is the zenith (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF1037-L20..AB01-PDF1036-L08
What compels us most of all to the knowledge of this anomaly is the cause of the solar eclipses, for they cannot be grasped without grasping the amount of this anomaly at all places of the horizon. In the lunar eclipses we have no need of it, since the moon is not the cause of its own eclipse, as it is the cause of the eclipse of the sun: the cause of its eclipse is something else. This anomaly is also perceptible in Venus and Mercury, but more in Mercury, because it is nearer the moon, above all when Mercury is at its nearest distance, for its parallax is then like the parallax of the moon at its farthest distance. In the sun it is not perceptible as it is in the other three mentioned; it is, as Ptolemy set it down, in the ratio of one to the 1210 that Ptolemy made the distance of the sun from the centre of the earth, without distinction. We find the visible position of the sun agreeing with its true position, because the parallax of the sun entered into the computation of the sun at the time of the observation, since the limit of the ecliptic and its distance from the equator were known only by observation of the sun.
@n «Compels»: so Nallino's translation; the printed word, «يقطم», is obscure. Ptolemy (*Almagest* IX 1) holds the parallaxes of the five stars imperceptible. Al-Battānī, like al-Farghānī, follows those who make the spheres touch, so that the nearest distance of Mercury is the farthest distance of the moon (chapter 50); hence their parallaxes there are equal (Nallino).
@n The ratio of 1 to 1210 makes the greatest parallax of the sun 2′ 50½″, about 3 minutes, the value of Ptolemy, Theon and the Arabs (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1036-L08..AB01-PDF1036-L18
It appeared before that the point of the farthest distance of the moon's eccentric sphere is 60 parts from the centre of the earth. If the radius of the earth is one part, the distance of the moon from the surface of the earth is then 59 parts, and in this measure the 5¼ parts that are the radius of the epicycle are about 5⅙ parts and the whole diameter of the epicycle 10⅓ parts. So when the centre of the epicycle is at the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric sphere, which happens at the times of the mean conjunctions and oppositions, and the moon is at the point of the farthest distance of the epicycle, its farthest distance from the earth is 64;10, and this is the first limit; when it is at the bottom of the epicycle, its distance from the earth is 53;50, and this is the second limit. When the centre of the epicycle is at the point of the nearest distance, whose distance from the centre of the earth was shown to be 39;22, which is 38;43 in that measure — this happens only at the two quarters of the month on either side of the full moon — then, if the moon is at the top of its epicycle, its distance from the earth is 43;53, and this is the third limit; and if it is at its nearest point on the epicycle, its distance from the earth is 33;33, and this is the fourth limit. Between these four limits there are limits of differing distances.
@n These are Ptolemy's four limits (*Almagest* V 13 and 17).
@p AB01-PDF1036-L18..AB01-PDF1035-L08
If you wish to know the distance of the moon from the earth, take the corrected anomaly of the moon: if it is less than 180, work with it, and if it is more than 180, subtract it from 360 and work with the remainder. The way to work with it is this. Look: if the number with which I told you to work is less than 90, take its sine and the sine of what remains of it to complete 90; multiply each of the two sines by 5;15, which is the radius of the epicycle, divide what results from each of them by the radius, and keep the results. Add what results from the sine of the complement of the number to 60, multiply the sum by itself, add to it what results from the sine of the number multiplied by itself, and take the root of the total. If the number with which I told you to work is more than 90, cast off 90 from it; find the sine of the remainder and the sine of what remains to complete the number to 90, multiply each of the two sines by 5;15 and divide by the radius. Subtract what results for the sine of the number from 60, multiply the remainder by itself, add to the product what results for the sine of the complement of the number multiplied by itself, and take the root of the total. What results from either of the two roots is the hypotenuse of the moon, I mean its distance from the centre of the earth at the times of the mean conjunction and opposition. Subtract a minute from each degree of this distance: the remainder is the distance of the moon from the earth.
@n «Hypotenuse» renders *quṭr*, literally «diameter»: the line from the centre of the earth to the moon. In modern terms the distance is √((60 + r cos a)² + (r sin a)²), with r = 5;15 and a the anomaly. Taking a minute from each degree reduces the distance to the measure of the limits above, in which the 60 parts of the eccentric are 59; Ptolemy has no such rules (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF1035-L08..AB01-PDF1034-L04
If the moon is between the conjunction and the opposition, on either side, take what results from multiplying the minutes of the fourth column of the tables of the equation of the moon into the fifth column of them, which is what I told you to add to the single equation of the moon at the time of the computation, and always add it to the 5 parts 1 minute, which are the whole single equation. Find the sine of the sum: the result is the radius of the displaced epicycle. Use it in place of the 5¼ parts in that same way: the result is the hypotenuse of the moon corrected for the displacement of the epicycle, so keep it. Then take the doubled distance between the sun and the moon by their mean course: if it is from 0 to 180, work with it, and if it is from 180 to 360, subtract it from 360 and work with the remainder. The way to work with it is this. Look: if the number with which you work is less than 90, subtract it from 90, and if it is more than 90, cast off 90 from it; find the sine of whichever of these you have: that is the first sine, so keep it under this name. Then subtract the number whose sine you found from 90 and find the sine of the remainder: that is the second sine. Then take the radius of the eccentric sphere, which has been shown to be 49;41, and multiply it by itself: the product is 2468 degrees 26 minutes. [Then multiply] the second sine by 10;19, which is the amount between the centres, divide the product by the radius, multiply the quotient by itself, and subtract it from the 2468 degrees 26 minutes; take the root of the remainder: that is the corrected side, so keep it. Then multiply the first sine by 10 parts 19 minutes as well, divide the product by the radius and keep the result. If the number with which you worked is less than 90, add what you kept to the corrected side, and if it is more, subtract what you kept from the corrected side. Always subtract from 60 what the corrected side comes to after the addition or the subtraction: the remainder is the share of twice the distance between the centres. Subtract it from the hypotenuse of the moon corrected for the displacement of the epicycle, take a minute for each degree of the remainder, and what remains is the distance of the moon from the earth.

@p AB01-PDF1034-L08..AB01-PDF1034-L15
If you wish to know the parallax of the moon on the circle of altitude, take the altitude of the moon at the time that you want and find what the altitude falls short of 90, which is the distance of the moon from the point of the zenith. Find the sine of each of them and multiply it by one minute, so that each degree of the sine becomes a minute and returns to the one part that is the amount of the radius of the earth. Subtract what results of minutes of the sine of the altitude from the distance of the moon from the earth: the remainder is the corrected distance, so keep it. Then multiply the minutes of the sine of the distance of the moon from the point of the zenith by 60 and divide the product by the corrected distance that you kept: the result is minutes; find their arc, and the arc that results is the parallax of the moon on the circle of altitude that passes through the zenith and the moon. Ptolemy set down the amount of this parallax in the tables for the four limits mentioned for the moon, and for the sun for one distance.
@n The altitude here is that seen from the centre of the earth (Schiaparelli). In modern terms the rule is sin p = sin z / (D − cos z), with z the distance from the zenith and D the distance of the moon in radii of the earth; the exact rule has tan p for sin p. Ptolemy's tables are those of *Almagest* V 18.
@p AB01-PDF1034-L16..AB01-PDF1033-L11
If you wish to know the parallax of the moon on the circle of altitude by those tables, which we have set down in this book of ours in that way, so as to know by it the position of the moon in which it is seen on the ecliptic in longitude and latitude, from the arcs and angles that arise from the intersection of the ecliptic and the circle of altitude, find the degree that falls in the middle of the sky and the degree that falls on the eastern horizon, I mean the rising degree of the ecliptic, at the time when the moon happens to be above the earth in the given clime. Then find the amount, of the degrees of the signs, between the rising degree and the degree that falls in the middle of the sky, and also between the degree in question, in which the moon is, and the rising degree. Then find the altitude of the degree that falls in the middle of the sky, as I shall describe, although this was given at the beginning of the book. Look at the declination of the degree in the middle of the sky: if it is northern, subtract it from the latitude of the clime, and if it is southern, add it to it; subtract what the latitude of the clime comes to after the addition or the subtraction from 90: the remainder is the altitude of the degree of the middle of the sky. If the declination of the degree of the middle of the sky is northern and you wish to subtract it from the latitude of the locality, but find the latitude of the locality less than that declination, take the difference between them and subtract it from 90: the remainder is the altitude of the degree of the middle of the sky above the northern horizon, and the computation is then reversed. When you know what I have described to you, multiply the sine of the distance of the degree in question from the ascendant by the radius, divide the product by the sine of the [arc] between the ascendant and the degree of the middle of the sky, multiply the quotient by the sine of the altitude of the degree of the middle of the sky, divide the product by the radius, and find the arc of the quotient: the arc that results is the altitude of the degree in question at that time, on whichever side of the horizon it is, I mean in the east or in the west. Keep it, and keep the sine by which you found it. Then subtract the altitude of the degree in question from 90: the remainder is the distance of the degree in question from the point of the zenith. Keep it: with it you know the amount of the parallax on the circle of altitude from these tables.
@n The altitude of the degree of the middle of the sky is found as in chapter 15. In modern terms the rule is sin h = sin d · sin h_M / sin d_M, with d and d_M the distances of the degree in question and of the degree of the middle of the sky from the ascendant, and h_M the altitude of the latter.

If the distance of the degree in question from the ascendant is less than 90, subtract it from 90 and work with the remainder; if it is more than 90, subtract 90 from it and work with the remainder. The way to work with it is to take the excess over 90 or the shortfall from it, find its sine and multiply it by the sine of the altitude of the degree in question at that hour, which is the sine that I told you to keep under its name; divide the product by the sine of the distance of the degree in question from the ascendant, multiply the quotient by the radius, divide the product by the sine of the distance of the degree in question from the point of the zenith, and find the arc of the quotient: the arc that results is the amount of the angle of longitude, out of the whole of one right angle. Subtract it from the 90 parts that are the amount of a right angle: the remainder is the amount of the angle of latitude. @n The angle of latitude is the angle between the ecliptic and the circle of altitude through the moon, and the angle of longitude its complement. If you wish to know the amounts of the parallax of the moon by the tables that Theon, the astronomer of Alexandria, made, which we have set down in this book as Theon made them. Theon set down the parallax of the moon in longitude and latitude for seven climes, at intervals of half an hour in the longest day, on the assumption that the moon is at the beginnings of the signs, after taking the parallax of the sun from the parallax of the moon in the way set down in Ptolemy's book, according to the inclination used there; and made it known by the equal hours of the distance of the degree of the moon from the circle of noon. So the finding of the parallax by these tables varies near noon, which is the meridian line, by day and by night. These amounts are not like those that result from working with the angles and arcs, for various reasons that interfere in them, although they are easier to obtain than those.
@n Theon's tables are computed with Ptolemy's inclination of 23;51, 16 minutes more than al-Battānī's; the greatest effect of the difference, at al-Raqqa, is about 16 seconds (Nallino's commentary).

The way to work with these tables is as I describe. Find the distance of the degree in which the moon is from the meridian line, by night or by day, so as to know how many equal hours the degree of the moon is from noon or from midnight towards the east or the west, on whichever side the moon is. This is known as I describe. Take the times of the ascensions of the right sphere that stand opposite the degree of the middle of the sky, and also the times of the ascensions of the right sphere that stand opposite the degree in which the moon is. Subtract the times of the ascensions of the degree of the middle of the sky from the times of the ascensions of the degree of the moon if the moon is on the eastern side of the meridian line, and subtract the times of the ascensions of the degree of the moon from the times of the ascensions of the degree of the middle of the sky if the moon is on the western side of the meridian line. Divide the result, whichever of the two it is, by 15: what comes out is the hours of the distance of the moon from the meridian line, in equal hours, on the side where the moon is. Then see whether the moon is above the earth or below it: if the degree of the moon is between the setting degree and the rising degree on the side of the middle of the sky, it is above the earth, and if otherwise, below the earth. When you know that the moon is above the earth, enter with the hours of the distance of the degree of the moon from the middle of the sky into the tables of the parallax for the given clime to which the latitude of your locality is nearest, and look for the same number in the column of the hours set down in the table of the sign in which the moon is, from the horizon. That is, if the moon is on the western side of the meridian line, you look in the hours after the meridian, and if it is on the eastern side, in the hours before the meridian; the hours that you have will be less than the hours set down at the ends of the tables for the signs, and they can be more only if the degree of the moon is below the earth. Then take what stands opposite those hours in the table of the sign in which the moon is and in the table of the sign that follows the sign of the moon, of the minutes of longitude and the minutes of latitude set down there, with interpolation. That is: if there is a fraction with the hours, see what part of an hour the fraction is and take that part of the difference between the complete hour and the hour one above it; add what results for the longitude to the longitude opposite the hour if this is the lesser, and subtract it from it if it is the greater, and do the same with what results for the latitude. Then look at the degrees that the moon has traversed in its sign and find what part they are of the 30 degrees of the sign; take that part of the difference between the minutes of longitude that you wrote down for the sign of the moon and for the sign that follows it, and add the result to the minutes of longitude of the sign of the moon if these are the lesser, or subtract it from them if they are the greater; do the same with the difference of the minutes of latitude. What the minutes of the sign of the moon in longitude and latitude come to after the addition or the subtraction are the minutes of the degree of the moon; keep them.

It may happen that the moon, near the middle of the sky, by an hour or less, up to an hour and a fraction, is to the west of the middle of the sky by measurement while it inclines to the east in its distance from the ascendant, or is to the east of the middle of the sky by the same distance while it inclines to the west. Then examine the parallax in longitude alone, not the latitude, when you take it from these tables near the middle of the sky, on the side on which the parallax in longitude in the hour that follows the meridian is less than its parallax at the meridian, or in the second hour from the meridian less than in the first hour that follows the meridian, on either side, so that you know where the parallax of the moon in longitude should vanish and be nothing at all: that is where the distance of the degree of the moon from the ascendant is exactly 90 parts. When the matter falls out in this way, and the hours of the distance are on the side on which the minutes of longitude vanish, and near the middle of the sky, the way to work is to add together the minutes of longitude for the meridian and for the hour that follows it, or for that one and the hour that follows it, according to the fraction of the hour that you have. If what results is more than the first longitude, which stands opposite the complete hour, or less than it, find its excess over it or its shortfall from it: that is the parallax for the sign in which the moon is, or for the sign that follows it, whichever the matter happens to fall in as we have described, or for both together. Then take the difference between the longitude for the sign of the moon and for the sign that follows it, multiply it by the parts of the moon in the sign in which it is, and divide the product by 30; add the result to the minutes of the sign of the moon if these are the lesser, or subtract it from them if they are the greater. Correct the result by the fourth and the fifth tables of the computation in that way, and proceed with it in the same way in the addition to or the subtraction from the true position of the moon. It may happen that what results for the sign of the moon is opposite to that of the sign that follows it in its inclination to one of the two horizons. When this is so, add together what results for each of the two signs and take of it in proportion to the degrees that the moon has traversed in its sign, out of 30; if the result is more than the parallax of the sign of the moon, take its excess over it, and if it is less, take its shortfall; correct the excess or the shortfall that results by the fourth and the fifth column in that way, and proceed with it in that way. Then you will have found the parallax of the moon on its side in longitude and latitude, and this is nearer to the truth when the moon is on the belt of the signs, if God wills.
@n The point of the ecliptic 90 degrees from the ascendant is on the meridian only when the beginning of Cancer or of Capricorn culminates; otherwise it lies east or west of the meridian, at a distance that may exceed an hour, and there the parallax in longitude vanishes and changes its side. The parallaxes found from the tables may be wrong by 4 or 5 minutes when the moon is off the ecliptic (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
