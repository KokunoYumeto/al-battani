Chapter 48. On knowing the rising of the five wandering stars and their setting, which is their appearing and their disappearing
@p AB01-PDF0976-L11..AB01-PDF0975-L02
He said: If you wish to know when the stars come out in the east and when they go down in the west, which is their rising and their disappearing: Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, if the anomaly of each of them is from 0 to 180, are in their morning rising, and from 180 to 360 in their evening setting. Venus and Mercury, because of their circling around the sun and their quickening and slowing, have four configurations with respect to the sun. As for Venus, if its corrected anomaly is from 0 to 137, it is in its evening rising, when it is seen on the western horizon, and it is then faster in motion than the sun; from 137 to 180 it is in its evening setting, when it slows down and turns back and the sun overtakes it; from 180 to 223 it is in its morning rising, and it is then slower in motion than the sun; and from 223 to 360 it sets in the mornings until it overtakes the sun and disappears in its rays, and it is then faster in motion than the sun. As for Mercury, if its corrected anomaly is from 0 to 112, it rises in the evenings; from 112 to 180 it sets in the evenings; from 180 to 248 it rises in the mornings; and from 248 to 360 it sets in the mornings. Its state of speed and slowness is as we have set out for Venus.
@n The translation follows the corrigenda in reading «غُروبها», «their setting», for «عُروبها» in the title (p. 176). Rising and setting mean here the growing and the shrinking distance of the star from the sun. For Venus and Mercury it grows from the conjunction beyond the sun, at the anomaly 0, to the greatest elongation, where the line of sight touches the epicycle, and shrinks to the conjunction below the sun, at 180. At the mean distance, with Ptolemy's radii of the epicycles, 43;10 and 22;30 in parts of which the eccentric is 60, the greatest elongation comes at the anomalies 136 and 112. For Venus the codex has 136; Nallino prints 137.
@p AB01-PDF0975-L02..AB01-PDF0975-L08
If you wish to know the first appearance of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in the mornings in the east, which is their first coming out from under the rays to the limit of visibility, the sun having passed them, compute for one of them: if its corrected anomaly is about 20 degrees, say that it is seen. For its setting in the evenings, when the sun overtakes it and hides it and covers it with its rays, you reckon it when the corrected anomaly is about 340. As for Venus and Mercury: if the anomaly of one of them is about 20, that is its first appearance in the evenings in the west; if it is about 340 degrees, that is its first disappearance in the mornings in the east; if it is about 160, that is its first disappearance in the evenings in the west; and if it is about 200, that is its first appearance in the mornings in the east.
@n Nallino takes these numbers from Plato of Tivoli and Theon's canon; the codex has 26 for 20, 348 for 340 and 170 for 200.
@p AB01-PDF0975-L08..AB01-PDF0975-L18
If you wish to know the first rising of one of them and its disappearance by computation, find the arc of seeing on the equator for each of them. The amount of the arc of seeing is for Saturn 14 degrees, for Jupiter 12;45, for Mars 14;30, for Venus 5;40 and for Mercury 11;30. Then find the distance of the star from the equator and the degree that culminates with it, according to the latitude that it has, and find with these half the arc of its day, which is half its stay above the earth, and the times of the ascension of the degree with which it rises or sets, in the ways mentioned. If there is between the star and the sun, in degrees of the ascensions or of the settings, as much as the arc of seeing mentioned for it, the star is seen on that day, or disappears. If your computation is for the seeing and the appearance from under the rays, and what lies between it and the sun is less than the arc of seeing, it has not appeared, and if it is more, it has appeared; if your computation is for the disappearance, and the distance between it and the sun in times of the settings or the ascensions, according to the horizon at which it is, is less than the arc of seeing, it has disappeared, and if it is more, it has not disappeared.
@n The arcs are Ptolemy's (*Almagest* XIII 7), which he gives as arcs of the ecliptic near the beginning of Cancer; al-Battānī takes them as arcs of the equator, to be compared with the ascensions (Nallino's commentary). The codex has 16, 16;30 and 6;40 for the arcs of Saturn, Mars and Venus (Nallino). The translation reads «والمطالع», «and the ascensions», for «والمطالم» (p. 177, line 16), a misprint that the corrigenda do not list.
@p AB01-PDF0975-L18..AB01-PDF0975-L19
You should compute the star for the rising or the setting of the sun at the time when its corrected anomaly is about what we have described.
@p AB01-PDF0975-L19..AB01-PDF0974-L04
If you wish to know how long ago it rose, or in how long it will rise, or how long ago it disappeared, or in how long it will disappear, find the amount between the arc of seeing and the distance of the star from the sun and keep it; then divide it by the true corrected course of the star. You find that as I describe: compute the star either a day after that day or a day before it, as needed; what lies between the two positions is the true motion of the star. Subtract it from the true motion of the sun in a day: the remainder is the true corrected course. This is for the three upper stars. For Venus and Mercury, you add the course of the star to the course of the sun if it is retrograde, and take the difference between them if it is direct: the result is its corrected course. What comes out of the division is the number of days and hours in which it will rise or set, or the number of days and hours since it rose or set.
@p AB01-PDF0974-L04..AB01-PDF0974-L17
I have set down for them the amounts of seeing in degrees of the signs in each clime, according to what has been observed of their appearance in each sign, and that is set down at the heads of the signs, although it is not exact, because of the variation of their latitudes. For this reason we have confined ourselves to setting down these amounts for one clime, so that knowing it from this table is easy, approximately; we have made it for the fourth clime. If you wish to know the seeing of the star and its disappearance, take, of the amounts of seeing and of disappearance set down in the table of the morning risings and the evening disappearances of the three upper stars, what is under the sign in which the star is, and what is under the sign after it. Then take the difference between the two signs, multiply it by the degrees of the star in the sign in which it is, and divide the product by thirty. Add the result to the amount of seeing or of disappearance, whichever you are computing, that you obtain under the sign in which the star is, if that is the smaller, and subtract it from it if that is the greater. What remains is the arc of seeing at that place in parts of the ecliptic, or the arc of disappearance. If that is equal to what lies between the sun and the star in degrees of the signs, it rose or disappeared on that day; if it differs, follow what I have set out and explained in this chapter. It is known that the working of the seeing by computation, in the way that I set out first, is more correct and more exact than what comes out of these tables. For Venus and Mercury, you take the four configurations mentioned in the way that we have stated for the upper stars.
