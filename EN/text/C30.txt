Chapter 30. On the description of the spheres of the moon, the anomaly of its motions, the increase and decrease of its light, the causes of the two eclipses, and the distances of the two luminaries from the centre of the earth, their diameters and the sizes of their bodies when they are compared with the earth

@p AB01-PDF1076-L04..AB01-PDF1076-L14
Al-Battānī said: When the motions of the moon were investigated, two anomalies were found in it. One of them is single by itself: it appears alone, only at the times of the conjunctions with the sun and the oppositions to it that take place by the mean course of the sun and the moon, and it depends on the position of the moon on its epicycle. The second anomaly appears through the moon's distances from the sun; it is compounded with the first anomaly, so that the two act together. This is grasped by demonstration with geometrical lines, by imagining that the moon has four spheres. The first is made like the ecliptic: it lies level beneath it, moves with its motion without deviating from it, and has its centre at the centre of the ecliptic. The second sphere is inclined to it towards the north and the south; it is as large as this parecliptic sphere, and the two have one centre. Its greatest inclination to either side is about 5 parts, which is the amount of the moon's distance from the belt of the signs in latitude. The motion of this inclined sphere, against the order of the signs, is about 3 minutes a day. This is the motion of the two nodes. One of them is called the head: from it the moon passes in latitude towards the north. The other is called the tail: from it the moon passes towards the south. These two nodes are the places where the inclined sphere and the parecliptic sphere intersect.
@p AB01-PDF1076-L14..AB01-PDF1075-L09
Inside this inclined sphere is a third sphere, whose centre lies outside the centre of the other two. It hangs from the inclined sphere and touches it at a point that is its highest place; that point is called the farthest distance from the earth. It moves within the inclined sphere against the order of the signs, about 11 parts 12 minutes a day. There is a fourth sphere, called the moon's own epicycle. Its centre is on the circle of the eccentric sphere, and this centre moves on the circle of the eccentric sphere in the order of the signs about 24 parts 23 minutes [a day]. It begins its motion from the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric sphere, which is taken together with the mean position of the sun. Therefore the centre of the epicycle falls on the point of the farthest distance twice in the lunar month, once at the mean conjunction and again at the opposition. The moon moves on the circle of the epicycle about 13 degrees 4 minutes a day, against the order of the signs on the epicycle, beginning from the point of the farthest distance that is seen from the centre of the eccentric sphere. Since the centre of the epicycle falls on the circle of the inclined sphere at these two times, as we have said, nothing prevents us from taking it, in this way, to move on the circle of the inclined sphere about 13 degrees 14 minutes a day; this is its motion in latitude. The node, at the intersection of the two spheres, carries it back against the order of the signs by the 3 minutes that we said are the motion of the inclined sphere, so that its motion in longitude, in the order of the signs, remains about 13 degrees 11 minutes a day. The motion of the moon on the epicycle is the first motion mentioned.
@n The daily motions are Ptolemy's, rounded. The 24;23 is twice the daily excess of the moon's motion in longitude over the sun's, and the eccentric moves back by 24;23 − 13;14 + 0;3 = 11;12 (Nallino). The 13;4 is the daily motion in anomaly, 13;3,53,56,… in *Almagest* IV 3.

It is clear from what we have described that nothing enters the motion of the moon from the eccentric sphere at these two times, since then the moon is not distant from the mean position of the sun or from the point opposite it. So nothing of the second anomaly is mixed with the single anomaly until the moon moves away from the sun; at those distances the second anomaly, which comes from the eccentric sphere, is compounded with it according to the moon's distances from the sun. This is the figure of the four spheres of the moon; the demonstration will be clear to you from the example of their drawing.

As for the cause of the lunar eclipse, it is that the sun is greater than the earth and the earth greater than the moon. The rays of the sun go out along the sides of the earth until they end in the air on the other side in the shape of the head of a pine, and so the shadow of the earth is called the pine-shaped shadow; its end lies beyond the sphere of Mercury. When the passage of the moon is at one of the two nodes of its sphere at the times of its opposition to the sun, its centre comes to the centre [line] of the ecliptic: it falls on the belt of the signs, faces the sun across a diameter of the sphere, with no latitude by which it is inclined away from the sun, and is directly opposite it. Then the earth comes between the moon and the sun and screens it from it, so that the moon falls into the pine-shaped shadow of the earth that we have mentioned; its light is darkened, and it is eclipsed according to how far or near its path is to the belt of the signs, which is the middle of the shadow. If it has no latitude at all and is in the node itself, its passage at the middle of the eclipse is on the middle line of the shadow, and its eclipse is then the most complete of eclipses and the longest in duration. For this reason the moon never has its light complete: it receives all the light only when the sun falls on the half of its circle that faces it, and that happens only when it is on the diameter of the sun, with half the sphere between them, which it can reach only under the darkness, at the middle of the eclipse. If it is inclined from the path of the sun in latitude to the north or to the south, less than half the circle of the sphere lies between it and the sun, and its opposition to the sun is not along a straight diameter. So when it is opposite the sun while inclined from its path in latitude, its eclipse is according to the amount of its latitude, up to the greatest latitude at which its circle can touch the circle of the shadow.

It is clear from what we have said that none of the stars can be eclipsed at their opposition to the sun, since the shadow does not reach their centres; and Mercury does not move as far from the sun as opposition, to fall into the shadow then. But the moon may eclipse the other stars and hide them from sight when its visible path agrees [with theirs] in longitude and latitude, for then it falls on the line that goes out from the eye to the star in question. The stars also eclipse one another, when the lower of them passes under one of those above it and comes onto its course in longitude and latitude. If the sun were equal to the earth, the breadth of the shadow would be the same from its bottom to its top; it would have no end in the air, but would rise without end. The eclipse of the moon would then be of one and the same amount at the top of its epicycle and at the bottom, it would linger in the eclipse longer than it is found to linger, and all the stars would be eclipsed at their opposition to the sun. If the sun were smaller than the earth, the top of the shadow would be broader than its bottom, and it would rise in the sky without end; the higher the breadth of the shadow rose, the more the moon and the stars would be eclipsed, lingering in the eclipse for days that differ according to their course compared with the course of the sun.

@p AB01-PDF1068-L06..AB01-PDF1068-L13
As for the sun, the cause of its eclipse is the moon. When at the times of the conjunctions its centre happens to be seen on the belt of the signs, it comes between the eyes of those who look at the sun and the sun, since it falls on the line that goes out from the eye to the sun, being nearer to the earth than the sun is; just as a small thing can always be seen to hide a large thing when it is nearer to the eyes. The amounts of the eclipse are according to the latitude of the moon as it is seen, up to the limit at which its circle cannot hide anything of the circle of the sun. For this reason the eclipse of the sun has different amounts in places of different latitude, while the eclipse of the moon is seen of one amount in all places.
@p AB01-PDF1068-L13..AB01-PDF1068-L21
As for the distances of the sun and the moon, their diameters and the sizes of their bodies compared with the earth, to find them Ptolemy put forward two lunar eclipses as the basis of the measurement, and carried it out on the assumption that the moon hides the whole sun from sight when it is at its farthest distance from the earth at the times of the conjunctions and is seen on the belt of the signs, and gave the diameter of the sun no perceptible variation, relative to the moon, between its far and near distances from the earth, but made it the same as the moon's. Ptolemy mentioned no observations of solar eclipses in the book and did not use them, and we do not know what prevented Ptolemy. But in the amounts of the solar eclipses that we observed we did not see anything that requires the circle of the moon to cover the circle of the sun and hide it from sight in the ratio mentioned. On the contrary, we also found a clear and evident change in the diameter of the sun, relative to the moon, between its farthest and nearest distances, as geometrical measurement requires, although in itself it is small and imperceptible.

In one of them the sun and the moon were in the region of their farthest distance; in the second the sun was in the region of its nearest distance and the moon near its mean distance. The middle of the first eclipse, as we found it by observation, was in the year 1202 of Dhū l-Qarnayn, which is the year 1214 from the death of Alexander, one seasonal hour after noon on the eighth day of Āb, in the city of al-Raqqa, and more than two thirds of the sun was eclipsed to the sight. We shall draw this figure and place ten circles of the moon in it; the distance of the moon from the sun in each of the nine circles after the circle of the conjunction is 20 parts of the parts of the half circle, and the shape of the light in the circle of the moon is according to the amounts of these distances from the direction of the position of the sun, which is the point B. Since this is so, the increase and decrease of the light of the moon has become clear in this figure, as you see it on the plane of the circle. In the round body, however, it is twice each segment, because its angle, in the spherical way, is unlike this figure; we shall represent it in what follows, if God Most High wills, and success is from God.
