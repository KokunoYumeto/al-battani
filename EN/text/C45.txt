Chapter 45. On knowing the positions of the wandering stars on the ecliptic by the era of the Arabs and of the Romans
@p AB01-PDF0981-L14..AB01-PDF0980-L01
He said: If you wish to know the position of whichever of the wandering stars you wish, find the mean position of the star on the day that you want and at the given hour, by the hours of al-Raqqa, by whichever of the two eras you wish, as I described for knowing the mean position of the sun, and find also the mean position of the sun at the same time at which you find the mean position of the star. If your computation is for Saturn, Jupiter or Mars, subtract the mean position of whichever of them you compute from the mean position of the sun: the remainder is the anomaly of the star. If your computation is for Venus or Mercury, what comes out for you from the table is the anomaly of the star, and the mean position of the sun is used in place of their two mean positions, since it is what carries their positions along.
@n The mean positions are found as in chapter 33.
@p AB01-PDF0980-L01..AB01-PDF0980-L21
When you know the mean position of the star and its anomaly, subtract the farthest distance of the star from its mean position: the remainder is the centre. Look for the same in the two columns of numbers of the tables of the equation of the star, take what stands opposite it in the third column, headed «the equation of the anomaly and the centre», and write it down under the anomaly and under the centre both. If the centre is less than 180 degrees, subtract the equation from the centre and add it to the anomaly; if the centre is more than 180, add the equation to the centre and subtract it from the anomaly. What each of them comes to after the addition or the subtraction is the corrected centre and the corrected anomaly. Enter with the corrected centre in the two columns of numbers and take what stands opposite it of the minutes of the difference of the two distances, set down in the fourth column, and find whether they are increasing or decreasing. You know this from the mark at the head of the column, or from their increasing or decreasing as the number increases — except for Mercury, whose increase and decrease is known only from the mark, because, from the speed of its motion on its epicycle, it comes into the region of its nearest distance twice in a circuit of the signs. If these minutes are decreasing, enter with the corrected anomaly of the star into the tables of the equation of the star, in the two columns of numbers, and take what stands opposite it in the fifth column, headed «the farthest distance», and also in the sixth column, headed «the mean distance»; if the minutes are increasing, take what stands opposite the corrected anomaly in the sixth column and in the seventh column, headed «the nearest distance». Multiply what results from the fifth column by the minutes that you obtained from the fourth column, divide by sixty, and subtract the quotient from what you wrote down from the sixth column; and multiply what results from the seventh column by the minutes of the fourth column, divide by sixty, and add the quotient to what you wrote down from the sixth column. Subtract what the sixth column comes to after the addition or the subtraction from the centre corrected by the third column if the number of the corrected anomaly is more than 180, and add it to it if the corrected anomaly is less than 180. What the centre comes to after the addition or the subtraction is the position of the star from the point of the farthest distance. Add to it the farthest distance of the star, which you subtracted from its mean position at the start: the result is the computed position of the star. Cast it off from the beginning of Aries, thirty for each sign, in the usual way: where it ends, there is the position of the star on the ecliptic.
@n The rules are Ptolemy's. The columns are counted without the two columns of numbers. In Ptolemy's model it is the moving centre of Mercury's eccentric that brings the centre of its epicycle twice in a revolution to a nearest distance (*Almagest* IX 8, cited by Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0980-L22..AB01-PDF0980-L23
If there are minutes with the centre or the anomaly, take their share of the difference of the equation and its increments, in the manner that I showed you at the beginning of the book in the equation of the sun and the moon.
@p AB01-PDF0980-L23..AB01-PDF0979-L06
The farthest distances of the stars from the earth on their epicycles in the year 1191 of the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, son of Philip, Alexander, were: Saturn 244;28, Jupiter 164;28, Mars 126;18, Venus 82;14, and the sun the same, Mercury 201;28. We have computed them again, verified them and set them down for each of them at the beginning of the tables of its equation. These distances that we have set down also move with the motion of the sphere of the fixed stars, one degree in every 66 solar years, and one degree in every 68 lunar years too. So take the amount of the motion in the years between this year mentioned and the year that you want: if it is after the year 1191, add it to these distances, and if it is before the year 1191, subtract it from these distances, and work with them in the usual way.
@n He means the farthest distances of the eccentrics: on the epicycle the farthest distance comes wherever the anomaly is nothing (Nallino). The year 1191 began on 1 September 879 (Nallino). Nallino would read 126;58 for Mars, «نح» for «يح», so that the farthest distances of Saturn and Mars lie equally beyond Ptolemy's (chapter 31). One degree in 66 solar years is one in 68 lunar years (chapter 33).
