Chapter 43. On knowing the eclipse of the moon by computation and by the table, and knowing the amounts of the eclipse, its times, and the direction of the darkening and the clearing on the circles of the horizons, which differ for the localities
@p AB01-PDF1006-L04..AB01-PDF1006-L13
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the eclipse of the moon, examine the mean motion in latitude at the oppositions. If it is between the eclipse limits set down on the page of the months of the conjunctions and the oppositions, the moon can be eclipsed; if it is more than those amounts or less, it cannot be eclipsed. If it can be eclipsed, look at the corrected motion in latitude at the time of the opposition. If it is exactly 360 degrees, the moon is at the very node of the head, and if it is exactly 180 degrees, the moon is at the very node of the tail. If it is more than either of these two numbers, it has passed the node by the amount of the excess, and if less, it is short of the node by the amount of the shortfall. When the moon is at the very node, the eclipse is the most complete that can be. If its distance from either of the two nodes is more than 12 degrees, before it or behind it, it cannot be eclipsed; if it is less than that, it is eclipsed, and its eclipse is according to its distance from the node and its nearness to it. If the time of the opposition is at night, or near sunrise or sunset, the eclipse is seen whole or in part, according to the time.
@n In the codex the eclipse limits stand on the page of the single Egyptian years and on that of the hourly motions (Nallino). At 12 parts from a node the latitude of the moon is 1;2, about the greatest sum of the radii of the moon and the shadow, 0;17,40 + 0;46 = 1;3,40 (chapter 30).
@p AB01-PDF1006-L13..AB01-PDF1005-L03
When you know that it is eclipsed and that the eclipse, or part of it, is seen, enter with the corrected motion in latitude at the time of the opposition into the tables of the equation, take the latitude of the moon and find its side; or, if you wish, find it from the distance of the moon from the node, the meaning being the same in both ways. Keep what results of the latitude of the moon [true for the middle of the eclipse]. Then enter with the corrected anomaly of the moon at the time of the opposition into the tables of the computation and take what is under it in the third column, in which are the shares of the distance. Find what part of 60 the minutes come to, and take the same part of the 5 minutes and a half and a quarter by which the diameter of the moon varies; always add the result to 29 minutes and 30 seconds, the diameter of the moon at its farthest distance: the sum is the corrected diameter of the moon, so keep it. Likewise take, of the 7½ minutes by which the radius of the shadow varies, the part that the minutes of the third column are of 60, and add the result to 38½ minutes, the amount of the [radius] of the shadow at the farthest distance of the moon: the sum is the corrected radius of the shadow.
@n The words in brackets are supplied by Nallino, the first after Plato of Tivoli. The diameter of the moon varies from 0;29,30 to 0;35,20 (chapter 30), by 5⅚ minutes; the codex and Plato have 5¾, and the codex has 0;29,40 for 0;29,30 (Nallino). The radius of the shadow grows from 0;38,30 to 0;46 (chapter 30). The text has «the corrected diameter of the shadow»; Nallino translates «radius», as the numbers require.
@p AB01-PDF1005-L03..AB01-PDF1005-L07
If you wish to know that by computation in another way, take its unequal motion in an hour, multiply it by six less an eighth, and take a sixth of the minutes that result: that is the amount of the corrected diameter of the moon. If you wish to know the corrected radius of the shadow, multiply half the [corrected] diameter of the moon by two and three fifths: the product is the amount of the corrected radius of the shadow.
@n Six less an eighth, divided by six, is 0.979, about the ratio of the diameter of the moon to its hourly motion, 0;29,30 to 0;30,12 and 0;35,20 to 0;36,10 (chapter 30). Two and three fifths is the ratio of the diameter of the shadow to that of the moon, 92 to 35⅓ minutes at the nearest distance and 77 to 29½ at the farthest (Nallino, from chapter 30). «Corrected» is supplied by Nallino.
@p AB01-PDF1005-L07..AB01-PDF1005-L19
When you know the diameter of the moon and the radius of the shadow, in whichever of the two ways you wish, take half the corrected diameter of the moon and add it to the corrected radius of the shadow: the sum is half the two diameters, so keep it. Then look: if the true latitude of the moon is equal to half the two diameters, the moon touches the outermost line of the shadow, and nothing of it is eclipsed. If it is less than half the two diameters, subtract it from half the two diameters and look at the remainder: if it is equal to the diameter of the moon, the moon is eclipsed whole and has no stay; if it is more than the diameter of the moon, it is eclipsed whole and has a stay; if it is less, it is not eclipsed whole. Multiply the minutes that remained to you when you subtracted the latitude of the moon from half the two diameters by 12 and divide the product by the corrected diameter of the moon: the quotient is the amount of the diameter of the moon that is eclipsed, in the measure in which its diameter is 12 parts; these are called the digits of the eclipse, so keep them. If the moon has a stay, subtract the corrected diameter of the moon from those remaining minutes: the remainder is the minutes of the stay. Multiply them also by 12, divide the product by the diameter of the moon, and add the digits that result to the 12 digits that are the whole diameter of the moon: the sum is the digits of the eclipse from the beginning of the eclipse to its middle; keep them. Or, if you wish, multiply those minutes that remain of half the two diameters, whether they are less than the diameter of the moon or more, by 12, and divide the product by the diameter of the moon: the result is the digits of the eclipse.
@n Half the two diameters is the sum of the radii of the moon and the shadow. Here the minutes of the stay are the amount by which the eclipse exceeds the diameter of the moon; below they are half the path of the moon in the shadow during the total eclipse, and «the whole stay» is their greatest value, for a central eclipse, half the difference of the diameters of the shadow and the moon (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF1005-L19..AB01-PDF1004-L06
Then multiply half the two diameters by itself, subtract from the product the latitude of the moon multiplied by itself, and take the root of the remainder: the result is the minutes of the fall and the stay together, if the moon has a stay, and if it has no stay, the minutes of the fall. Whichever of these two it is, divide it by the gain of the moon: the result is the hours of the fall, or the hours of the fall and the stay, as the case may be. Subtract them from the hours of the opposition, which are the hours of the middle of the eclipse: the remainder is the hours of the beginning of the eclipse. Add them also to the hours of the middle of the eclipse: the sum is the equal hours of the end of the clearing. If the moon has a stay, subtract the corrected diameter of the moon from half the two diameters: the remainder is the minutes of the amount of the whole stay. Multiply them by themselves, subtract from the product the latitude of the moon multiplied by itself, take the root of the remainder, and divide it by the gain of the moon: the quotient is the hours of the stay. Subtract them from the middle of the eclipse: the remainder is the hours of the beginning of the stay; and add them also to the hours of the middle of the eclipse: the sum is the hours of the beginning of the clearing.
@n The root is the path of the moon relative to the shadow from the contact to the middle of the eclipse, the latitude being taken as constant; the contact is where the distance of the centres is half the two diameters, or, for the stay, the whole stay.
@p AB01-PDF1004-L07..AB01-PDF1004-L11
If the moon is not eclipsed whole, the eclipse has three times, and likewise if it is eclipsed whole and has no stay; if it has a stay, the eclipse has five times. These times mentioned are given in general terms and are not the most exact truth in the computation, for the latitude of the moon changes between the beginning of the eclipse and its middle, and from its middle to the end of the clearing, so that the amounts of the times on the two sides of the middle of the eclipse change with the change of the latitude of the moon; the middle of the eclipse clearly does not change.
@p AB01-PDF1004-L11..AB01-PDF1004-L23
If you wish to make that exact, so that no flaw enters the computation from it, take the minutes of the fall and the stay, or the minutes of the fall, whichever it is — those that I told you to divide by the gain of the moon — and add to them half their sixth. Subtract the sum from the corrected motion in latitude at the time of the opposition: the remainder is the motion in latitude for the plain beginning of the eclipse, so keep it. Then add those minutes with half their sixth also to the corrected motion in latitude at the time of the opposition: the sum is the motion in latitude for the plain end of the clearing. Find the latitude of the moon at each of the two times from the motion in latitude at it. Then multiply the latitude of the moon at the beginning of the eclipse by itself and subtract it from half the two diameters multiplied by itself; to the remainder add the difference between the latitude of the moon at the beginning of the eclipse and its latitude at the middle of the eclipse, multiplied by itself, and take the root of the sum: the result is the minutes of the fall from the beginning of the eclipse to its middle. Divide them by the gain of the moon and subtract the hours that result from the hours of the opposition: the remainder is the hours of the exact beginning of the eclipse. Then multiply the latitude of the moon at the end of the clearing by itself and subtract it from half the two diameters multiplied by itself; to the remainder add the difference between the latitude of the moon at the middle of the eclipse and its latitude at the end of the clearing, multiplied by itself, and take the root of the result: the result is the minutes of the fall and the stay. Divide them by the gain of the moon and add the result to the hours of the opposition: the sum is the hours of the exact end of the clearing.
@n The plain times are those of the general rule above. Half a sixth is added because the moon moves in latitude about 13/12 as fast as it gains on the shadow, the node also moving backwards. At the contact the moon is the root of D² − b² from the middle in longitude, D being half the two diameters and b the latitude at the contact; with the change of the latitude as the other side, the root gives its path to the middle.
@p AB01-PDF1004-L24..AB01-PDF1003-L08
Likewise, if you wish to make exact the time of the beginning of the stay and the time of the beginning of the clearing, you subtract the minutes of the stay that I told you to divide by the gain of the moon, with half their sixth, from the motion in latitude at the time of the opposition, and add them also to the motion in latitude at the time of the opposition, so that you know the motion in latitude for the two times. Then with it you find the latitude of the moon in that way at each of the two times, and subtract it from half the two diameters; you take the excess of the remainder over the diameter of the moon and multiply it by itself, and subtract the product from the total of the minutes of the stay multiplied by itself, and keep what remains to you of each of the two. Then you add to it what lies between the latitude of the moon at the middle of the eclipse and its latitude at that time, take the root of the sum and divide it by the gain of the moon. What results for the time of the beginning you subtract from the hours of the opposition, and what results for the time of the clearing you add to the hours of the opposition: what each of them comes to is the hours of the beginning of the stay and of the beginning of the clearing.
@n The passage is corrupt in the codex and in Plato of Tivoli. As Schiaparelli restores it (Nallino's commentary), the latitude of the moon at each of the two times is squared and subtracted from the square of the minutes of the whole stay, half the difference of the diameters of the shadow and the moon; the square of the difference between that latitude and the latitude at the middle of the eclipse is added, and the root of the sum is divided by the gain of the moon.
@p AB01-PDF1003-L08..AB01-PDF1002-L05
If the moon is not eclipsed whole and you wish to count the digits of the eclipse by computation, so as to know the area of the part of the circle of the moon that falls in the circle of the shadow, in the measure in which the area of the circle of the moon is 12 parts — these are what are called the digits — take half the corrected diameter of the moon and subtract from it 0;14,50, the radius of the moon at its farthest distance. Multiply the remainder by six and divide it by the radius of the moon at its farthest distance mentioned; add the digit, or part of a digit, that results to the six digits that are the radius of the moon at its farthest distance: the digits that result are the digits of the corrected radius of the moon, so keep them. Then double that: the result is the digits of the whole diameter of the moon. Multiply them by 3 parts 8½ minutes: that is the measure of the circumference of the moon. Take half of it and multiply it by the digits of the radius of the moon: the result is the area of the circle of the moon, so keep it. Then take the excess of the corrected radius of the shadow over 0;38,30, double it, multiply the result by 12 and divide it by 77, the whole diameter of the shadow at the farthest distance; add the digits that result to 31 digits and a fifth of a digit, the least diameter of the shadow, at the farthest distance of the moon: the sum is the digits of the diameter of the shadow. Multiply them by 3 parts 8½ minutes: the result is the circumference of the circle of the shadow. Take half of it and multiply it by the digits of the radius of the shadow: the result is the area of the circle of the shadow. Then add together the digits of the diameter of the shadow and the digits of the diameter of the moon: the sum is the digits of the two diameters, so keep it. Then multiply the digits of the eclipse by the digits of the diameter of the moon and divide the product by 12: the quotient is the corrected digits of the eclipse. Double them and subtract the result from the digits of the two diameters: the remainder is twice the distance between the two centres. Then subtract the corrected digits of the eclipse from the digits of the diameter of the moon, multiply the remainder by the corrected digits of the eclipse, and divide the product by twice the distance between the centres: the result is the sagitta of the circle of the shadow. Subtract it from the corrected digits of the eclipse: the remainder is the sagitta of the circle of the moon. Subtract that from the digits of the diameter of the moon, multiply the remainder by the sagitta of the circle of the moon, and take the root of the product: that is half the common chord, so keep it.
@n 0;14,50 is half of 0;29,40, the codex's reading above for the farthest diameter of the moon. With that diameter as 12 digits the 77 minutes of the diameter of the shadow are 31.15 digits, with 0;29,30 they are 31.32; the text has 31⅕. 3;8,30 is Ptolemy's ratio of the circumference to the diameter (*Almagest* VI 7; chapter 3). The corrected digits of the eclipse are the digits on the moon's actual diameter turned into these digits, in which its farthest diameter is 12. With a and c the radii of the shadow and the moon, b the distance of their centres and e the corrected digits, e = a + c − b, the sagitta of the shadow is e(2c − e)/2b, the sagitta of the moon is e less that, and half the common chord is the root of the sagitta of the moon times the diameter of the moon less that sagitta. Schiaparelli, who proves these rules in Nallino's commentary, did not remember finding them in other writers and calls the solution most elegant. The translation reads «التي» for «الني» (p. 149, line 12), a misprint that the corrigenda do not list, and follows the corrigenda in reading «وخُمْس», «and a fifth», for «وحُمْس» (p. 149, line 17).

Then take the corrected digits of the eclipse: if they are less than the digits of the radius of the moon, subtract them from the digits of the radius of the moon, and if they are more, subtract from them the digits of the radius of the moon. What results from the shortfall, add to the sagitta of the shadow; what results from the excess, take the difference between it and the sagitta of the shadow. Multiply what results in either way by half the common chord: the product is the area of the triangle of the moon, so keep it. Then take the digits of the radius of the shadow, subtract the sagitta of the shadow from them, and multiply the remainder by half the common chord: the product is the area of the triangle of the shadow, so keep it. Then multiply half the common chord by six, divide the product by the digits of the radius of the moon, and multiply the result by 10 parts, so that it becomes its share of the radius; find its arc from the table of the halved chords. Multiply the arc that results by the digits of a [quarter] of the circumference of the circle of the moon: the result is the share of the arc. Divide it by 90: the result is the arc of the moon. Multiply it by the digits of the radius of the moon: the result is the area of the arc of the moon; keep it in mind. Then take half the common chord again and multiply it by 15 parts and three fifths of a part, which are the digits of the least radius of the shadow; divide the product by the digits of the radius of the shadow, and multiply the result by 3 parts 50 minutes and a half and a quarter of a minute and a [fifth] of a tenth of a minute, so that it becomes its share of the radius; find its arc in the tables of the halved chords. Multiply the arc that results by a quarter of the circumference of the circle of the shadow and divide by 90: the result is the arc of the shadow. Multiply it by the digits of the radius of the shadow: the result is the area of the arc of the shadow. Add it to the area of the arc of the moon, and subtract from the sum the area of the triangle of the moon together with the area of the triangle of the shadow: the remainder is the area of the eclipsed segment of the circle of the moon. Multiply it by 12 and divide by the area of the circle of the moon that you kept at the beginning: the digits that result are the amount of the circle of the moon that is eclipsed, in the measure in which its whole area is 12.
@n Where the text has «the sagitta of the shadow» in this paragraph, the codex and Plato of Tivoli have «the circle of the moon»; Nallino corrects them, and Schiaparelli's proof confirms the correction. «Quarter» is supplied by Nallino after Plato, and «fifth» is Nallino's conjecture for a word eaten by worms: 60/15⅗ = 3;50,46 (Nallino). Half the common chord over the radius, in a radius of 60, is the sine of half the angle of the eclipsed arc at the centre; the arc so found, as a length, times the radius is the area of the whole sector, and the sector less the triangle is the segment, for the moon and for the shadow alike (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). As given, the rule is exact while the common chord lies between the centres of the moon and the shadow. In a deeper eclipse, where the excess of the corrected digits over the radius of the moon is greater than the sagitta of the shadow, the eclipsed segment of the moon is more than half its disc: the table of the halved chords then gives the smaller arc where the larger is needed, and the triangle of the moon has to be added, not subtracted, so that the rule gives too little.

If you wish to know the azimuth of the side from which the darkening begins on the circle of the moon, and the side from which it clears, on the circle of the horizon, and the figure of the eclipse, set up the ascendant of each of the times of the eclipse and find the azimuth of the ascendant of each of them on the circle of the horizon in the manner given at the beginning of the book. Then take the latitude of the moon at the time of the beginning of the eclipse and at the time of the end of the clearing, if the moon is not eclipsed whole; if the moon is eclipsed whole and has a stay, take also its latitude at the beginning of the clearing. Multiply each of the latitudes that you have by the radius and keep the products. Divide what results for the beginning of the stay and the beginning of the clearing by all the minutes of the whole stay, and what results for the beginning of the eclipse and the end of the clearing by half the two diameters: what results is degrees. Find their arc in the table of the halved chords: the arc for each of those times is the amount of the deviation of the darkening of the eclipse at that time; keep each of them with its side. If the true centre of the moon is on the belt of the signs, I mean that it has no latitude, at one of the times: if that is at the beginning of the eclipse or the beginning of the clearing, the beginning of the darkening and the beginning of the clearing are on the side of the azimuth of the ascendant at each of the two times; if it is at the beginning of the stay or the end of the clearing, both are on the side of the azimuth of the setting degree at each of the two times.
@n The azimuth of the ascendant is found as in chapter 7; such azimuths are amplitudes, counted from the east and the west points (Nallino). The latitude times the radius, divided by the distance of the centres at the contact, is the sine of the angle that the line of the centres makes with the ecliptic; the darkening begins, or the clearing ends, where that line meets the edge of the moon.

If the moon is not on the belt of the signs but has a latitude on one of the two sides, lay off the deviation of the eclipse at the time of the beginning of the eclipse and of the end of the clearing: at the time of the beginning of the eclipse, from the limit of the azimuth of the ascendant at the beginning of the eclipse on the circle of the horizon, towards the side opposite to the latitude of the moon; at the time of the end of the clearing, from the limit of the azimuth of its setting degree, again towards the side opposite to the latitude of the moon. At the time of the beginning of the clearing and the time of the beginning of the stay, you lay off the deviation of the darkening of the eclipse at the time of the beginning of the clearing from the limit of the azimuth of the degree that rises at it, towards the side of the latitude of the moon, and likewise you lay off the deviation at the time of the beginning of the stay from the limit of the azimuth of the degree that sets at it, towards the side of the latitude of the moon. Where the count ends for you on the circle of the horizon at each of the times, towards the azimuth of that part of the circle of the horizon is the deviation of the darkening and of the clearing on the circle of the moon.

If the moon is not eclipsed whole, the deviation of the darkening at the middle of the eclipse always falls at a right angle to the ecliptic, where it is bounded by the arc that passes through the two poles of the ecliptic, through the place of the moon and through the circle of the horizon. You know it by taking the angle of longitude known for the time of the middle of the eclipse, in the way that we described in finding it for knowing the parallax of the moon, and laying it off from the limit of the azimuth of the ascendant at the middle of the eclipse towards the side opposite to the latitude of the moon, when the moon is towards the east; when it is towards the west, you lay it off from the limit of the azimuth of the setting degree at the middle of the eclipse, again towards the side opposite to the latitude of the moon. Where the count ends for you on the circle of the horizon, to that part of it runs the inclination of the azimuth of the darkening at the middle of the eclipse. This is when the latitude of the moon is to the north; when it is to the south and the moon is on the eastern side, you lay off the angle from the azimuth of the setting degree, and when it is on the western side, from the azimuth of the ascendant, towards the side opposite to the latitude of the moon.
@n The angle of longitude is the one found for the parallax in chapter 39. At the middle of the eclipse the centres of the moon and the shadow differ only in latitude, so that the line of the centres is at right angles to the ecliptic.

If you wish to know the eclipse of the moon approximately by the table, enter with the true latitude of the moon at the time of the opposition into the two tables of the eclipse of the moon, for the farthest distance and for the nearest distance. If you find it in the table of the nearest distance alone and not in that of the farthest, take what stands opposite it of the digits and the minutes of the fall, and take of each of them the part that the minutes of the shares of the distance, set down in the third column of the tables of the computation opposite the corrected anomaly of the moon at the time of the opposition, are of 60 minutes: what results of each of them is the amount of the digits of the eclipse and the amount of the fall. If the latitude of the moon falls in both tables, take what stands opposite it in each of them of the digits, the fall and the stay, if the moon has a stay, and write down what comes from each of the two tables on its own side. Take the difference between the two tables in the digits, the fall and the stay, and take of each of these differences the part that the minutes of the third column of the tables of the computation, opposite the anomaly of the moon, are of 60 minutes; always add what results from each of them to its counterpart from the first table, that of the farthest distance. What the digits of the first table and the minutes of the fall and the stay in it come to after the addition is the digits of the eclipse of the diameter of the moon, the amount of the fall, and the amount of the stay, if you find a stay for the moon. If these digits are less than 12, the moon is not eclipsed whole, and you will find no stay for it then; if they are more than 12, the moon is eclipsed whole and has a stay according to how far it enters the darkness; and if the digits are exactly 12, the moon is just eclipsed whole.

If you wish to know the side from which the darkening of the eclipse begins and the side from which the clearing ends, enter with the digits of the eclipse, those of the diameter of the moon, in the column of the number of the digits of the tables of the deviations of the darkenings, and take what stands opposite them in the third column, and also in the fourth column if the moon has a stay. What results from the third column is the deviation at the time of the beginning of the eclipse and of the end of the clearing, and what results from the fourth column is the deviation at the time of the stay and of the beginning of the clearing; keep it. What the azimuth of the ascendant or of the setting point comes to after the addition or the subtraction is the azimuth of the degree of the ascendant at each time and of its setting degree. Know that the azimuth of the setting point is like the azimuth of the ascendant, on the opposite side: if the azimuth of the ascendant is northern, the azimuth of the setting point is southern, so whichever of the two azimuths you know, you know the other by it. The side of the azimuth is known from the drawing of the circle, by the marked directions on which the summer and the winter risings and settings fall, the summer ones being northern and the winter ones southern.

Al-Battānī said: We begin, with the help of God, by drawing a straight line and dividing it into as many equal parts as you wish, provided they are as many as the number of half the two diameters or more. Then take from this line the amount of half the two diameters and draw a circle with it: this is the circle of half the two diameters, on which the centre of the moon falls at the time of the beginning and at the end of the clearing. Then take also the amount of the radius of the shadow and draw with it, about the centre of the first circle, a circle that falls inside that circle: this is the circle of the shadow. Then divide the two circles into equal quarters, and write on the ends of the lines the directions of the east, the west, the north and the south. Then take from the divided line, with the compasses, the amount of the latitude of the moon at the middle of the eclipse; put one of the two ends on the centre of the two circles and turn the other end towards the side of the latitude of the moon. Where it falls on the line of the north or of the south, mark a sign on the line, which is the centre of the moon at the middle of the eclipse. Then take from the divided line the radius of the moon as well, and draw with it three circles. The centre of one of them is the western point of the circle of half the two diameters, where the parallel line meets it, and the centre of the second circle the eastern point of the circle. These two circles each necessarily touch the circle of the shadow: the one about the western centre is the circle of the moon at the beginning of the eclipse, and the one about the eastern centre is its circle at the end of the clearing. As for the circle whose centre is the sign of the latitude of the moon at the middle of the eclipse: if it falls wholly within the circle of the shadow, the moon is eclipsed whole and stays in the darkness according to what lies between its circle and the circle of the shadow; if the circle of the moon is inside the circle of the shadow and touches it, the moon is eclipsed whole and has no stay; and if the circle of the moon does not fall wholly within the circle of the shadow, as much of the circle of the moon is eclipsed as the circle of the shadow cuts off from it, which is the segment that falls in the circle of the shadow, whose diameter and area are known.

Let the example of this be the circle of the two diameters, on which are K and M, about the centre Z, and the circle of the shadow inside it, on which are S and Ḥ, whose centre is also the point Z. Since we wish to show the azimuth of the deviation of the darkenings and of the clearing on the circle of the horizon, we draw also about the centre Z a third, great circle, with the circle of half the two diameters inside it; let this circle be for the horizon, and mark on it A, B, G, D. We divide the three circles into quarters by two lines that intersect at the centre Z at right angles, the diameters AG and BD; let the sign A be the point of the south, the sign G the point of the north, the sign B the point of the east and the sign D the point of the west. We take the latitude of the moon to the south, and mark on its latitude at the beginning of the eclipse the point Ṭ, on its latitude at the middle of the eclipse the point H, and on its latitude at the end of the clearing the point L. We draw the two lines ṬK and LM parallel to the diameter BD, and join the point K to the point M by a line that passes through the point H. So the point K is the centre of the circle of the moon at the beginning of the eclipse and the point M the centre of its circle at the end of the clearing, and the line KHM passes through the three centres of the circles of the moon; on it is the passage of the moon from the beginning of the eclipse to the end of the clearing. It is clear that the circle whose centre is K touches the circle of the shadow at the sign S, and the one whose centre is M touches the circle of the shadow at the point Ḥ. Therefore, when the line M Ḥ Z LA and the line KSZQ are drawn, the line KSZQ is the azimuth of the beginning of the eclipse on the circle ABGD, bounded by the arc BQ, and the line M Ḥ Z LA the azimuth of the end of the clearing on the circle ABGD, bounded by the arc D LA. It is known that the point D is the azimuth of the setting degree and the point B the azimuth of the ascendant at every time. Since each of the triangles ZṬK and ZLM is right-angled, and each of the lines ZK and ZM is the known amount of half the two diameters, and each of the lines ZṬ and ZL is also known, each of the lines ṬK and LM, the remaining sides of the two triangles, is known as well. Again, since the angles of the triangles MLH and KṬH at L and Ṭ are right, and each of the lines LH and ṬH is of known amount, each of the lines KH and HM is of known amount: they are the amount of the fall and the stay, KH from the beginning of the eclipse to its middle and HM from the middle of the eclipse to the end of the clearing. It is clear in this figure, mentioned above, that the line KH is greater than the line HM. That is what we wished to show.
