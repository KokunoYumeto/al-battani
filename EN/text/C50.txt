Chapter 50. On knowing the distances of the stars, their diameters, the sizes of their bodies and the extent of their spheres, set down as handed down, in the way in which the ancients and the first ones stated them
@p AB01-PDF0971-L13..AB01-PDF0970-L08
Al-Battānī said: The distances of the sun and the moon, their diameters and the sizes of their bodies we have explained according to what is said in the book of the *Almagest* and what we found by measuring the eclipses. Let us now begin the account of the other spheres, up to the sphere of Saturn and the sphere of the fixed stars, as the later excellent sages after Ptolemy took pains to set it out. Their account of it was this. It has been shown that the farthest distance of the moon from the earth, in the measure in which the radius of the earth is one part, is 64;10, and that is the nearest distance of Mercury; the spheres of Mercury and Venus lie between the farthest distance of the moon and the nearest distance of the sun. The limit of the air and the fire is the nearest distance of the moon from the earth, which is when it is on either side of the full moon, I mean at the mean quadrature with the sun; it has been shown that its distance from the earth is then 33;33 in that measure. What is above that is the ether, in which the stars run. The two remaining elements, water and earth, end at the amount of the radius of the earth. These four elements, earth, water, air and fire, are the roots of the earthly natures and the cause of coming to be and passing away; as they differ, things differ, according to what of the sun, the moon and the stars is above them, and their change goes on within the nearest distance of the moon from the earth; by that every thing, of the animals and the plants, changes. What lies within this limit, between the centre of the earth and the ether, reaches, as we have said, 33;33 from the earth, in the measure in which the radius of the earth is one part: that is the farthest extent of the earth, the air, the water and the fire. What is above that is a fifth nature, of which nothing true can be said: the senses do not reach it, and the mind does not grasp its quality.
@n The translation reads «after Ptolemy» with Plato of Tivoli and Nallino's translation; the text has «before», which does not fit «the later sages». The distances of this chapter rest on Ptolemy's eccentricities and epicycles, with the spheres taken to touch one another without a gap, so that the least distance of each planet is the greatest distance of the one below it. Al-Farghānī gives the same scheme with somewhat different numbers, and Proclus reports it in the *Hypotyposes* (Nallino's commentary). 64;10 and 33;33 are the greatest and the least distance of the moon (chapter 39); the codex has 38;38 for 33;33.
@p AB01-PDF0970-L08..AB01-PDF0970-L21
In it is the sphere of Mercury, above the sphere of the moon. What appeared of its distance and size, as they grasped it, is this: it is reported that they measured its size at its farthest and at its nearest distance from the earth and found the variation of its size to be as two and a third and a quarter to one. Since the nearest distance of Mercury is equal to the farthest distance of the moon, which has been shown to be 64;10, when that is multiplied by the two and a third and a quarter, which is the variation of the size of its diameter, its farthest distance comes to 166 times the radius of the earth. When half of what lies between its farthest and its nearest distance is taken and added to its nearest distance, its mean distance is 115. Then they measured its size, when it was at its mean distance, against the sun at its mean distance, and found its diameter to be one part in fifteen of the diameter of the sun. When the 115 is divided by fifteen, the result is 7 parts and two thirds of a part. Since the diameter of the sun is five and a half times the diameter of the earth, if the mean diameter of the sun is made 1108, as we have shown by measurement, the diameter of the earth is 201½ in that measure; and when the 7⅔ parts are compared with the 201½, they are found to be about one part in 26¼ of it. Since the diameter of the earth subtends 1 degree and 57 minutes of the circle of the sphere, the diameter of Mercury subtends about 4 minutes and a half and a sixth of a minute. When that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the size of the body of Mercury comes to about one part in [eighteen thousand] parts of the body of the earth.
@n 64;10 × 2 7/12 = 165;46, and (64;10 + 166)/2 = 115. Making the diameter of the sun 1108, the number of its mean distance in radii of the earth (chapter 30), puts the diameter of each planet, its mean distance divided by the ratio of its apparent diameter to the sun's, in the same measure, in which the diameter of the earth is 1108/5½ (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary); 1108/5½ is 201 5/11 (Nallino). 1;57 divided by 26¼ is 0;4,27. The text has «seventeen»; Nallino takes it for an error of the codex for «eighteen thousand», since (26¼)³ = 18,088.
@p AB01-PDF0970-L21..AB01-PDF0969-L08
Then they looked at the size and the distance of Venus and found the variation of its size between its farthest and its nearest distance to be as two to thirteen. When the 166, which is the farthest distance of Mercury and the nearest distance of Venus, is multiplied by the six and a half that is the amount of the variation of the size of Venus relative to one, the farthest distance of Venus is 1070, which is the nearest distance of the sun; its mean distance is therefore 618. They measured the diameter of Venus against the diameter of the sun when it was at its mean distance and found it one part in ten of the diameter of the sun. When a tenth of the 618 parts is taken, it is 61 parts and four fifths; when that is divided by the 201½, it is a quarter and half a tenth of the diameter of the earth, and a little that is not to be counted. Therefore its diameter subtends 32 minutes and 27 seconds of the circle of the sphere; when that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the size of Venus is about one part in thirty-six of the size of the earth.
@n 166 × 6½ is 1079; Nallino keeps the text's 1070, the nearest distance of the sun in chapter 30, since the mean distance 618 is half of 166 + 1070. 61⅘ divided by 201½ is 0.307 (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0969-L08..AB01-PDF0969-L17
As for the centres of the epicycles of Mercury and Venus, their course on the circle of the eccentric is like the course of the centre of the epicycle of the sun. The size of the diameter of the epicycle of each of them is known from what lies between the farthest distance and its first station, which is its greatest distance from the sun on the side preceding in the order of the signs. The greatest distance of Mercury from the sun is 26 parts, when the sun is opposite the centre of the epicycle and the centre of the epicycle is at the farthest distance of the eccentric; Mercury is then once ahead of the sun and once behind it. The greatest distance of Venus from the sun is 46 degrees, when the sun is opposite the centre of the epicycle and the centre of the epicycle is at the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric; Venus is then once in front of the sun, once ahead of it and once behind it. The least distance of Venus from the sun, from the first station to the second, is 41 parts, and the least distance of Mercury from the sun, the amount between the two stations, is 21 parts. From this it is clear that the diameter of the epicycle of Mercury subtends 47 degrees, and the diameter of the epicycle of Venus 87 degrees.
@n The apparent diameter of the epicycle is the sum of the greatest and the least elongation: 26 + 21 = 47 and 46 + 41 = 87 (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). The numbers are Ptolemy's (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0969-L17..AB01-PDF0968-L09
As for Mars, its distance and the size of its body are according to what was said of it when they measured the variation of its size. They also reported that they found its size at its nearest distance to be seven times its size at its farthest distance. Its nearest distance is the farthest distance of the sun, which by our measurement is 1146; multiplied by the seven, that comes to 8022, and its mean distance is 4584. They measured it at its mean distance and found its diameter to be one part in twenty of the diameter of the sun. When its mean distance is divided by the twenty, it comes to 229 times and a fifth; when that is divided by 201½, the diameter of the earth, its diameter is about once the diameter of the earth and less than a seventh. Therefore the diameter of Mars subtends about 2 degrees, 1 minute and 37 seconds of the circle of the sphere; when that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the size of Mars is once and a third the size of the earth, less a little that is not to be counted. The size and the extent of its epicycle are known from the motion of the star Mars from its first station to its second. The epicycle moves 0;31 a day, and Mars moves on the epicycle 0;28 a day, so that its motion is left at 3 minutes; from this it is known that it stays in a sign five months and six, because of the variation of its motion as it is seen. In truth it neither increases nor decreases: it is one motion, for all time, for it and for the other stars. The diameter of the epicycle of Mars subtends 82 degrees and 28 minutes.
@n 1146 × 7 = 8022, and (1146 + 8022)/2 = 4584; 4584/20 = 229⅕, and 229⅕/201½ = 1.14. Nallino would read 82;21, which Ptolemy's ratio of the radius of the epicycle of Mars to that of its eccentric, 39;30 to 60, gives (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0968-L09..AB01-PDF0968-L20
As for the distance of Jupiter and its size, they found its size at its nearest distance to its size at its farthest distance to be as thirty-seven to twenty-three, which is one and a half and a ninth. When that is multiplied by the farthest distance of Mars, which is 8022, the farthest distance of Jupiter comes to about 12,924, and its mean distance is likewise 10,473. They found the size of Jupiter at its mean distance to be one part in twelve of the diameter of the sun. When its mean distance is divided, its diameter comes to about 872 and a half and a quarter; when that is compared with the 201½, it is four times and a third the diameter of the earth, less a little. When that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, its size is about eighty-one times the size of the earth, and its diameter subtends about 8;18 of the circle of the sphere. From [its motion from] its first station to its second, and the motion of its epicycle, which is five minutes a day in the order of the signs, while its motion in the lower part of its epicycle is 54 minutes each day, seemingly towards the west, it is clear that the diameter of its epicycle subtends 22 degrees.
@n 37/23 is 1.609, one and a half and a ninth 1.611; 8022 × 1 11/18 = 12,924 and (8022 + 12,924)/2 = 10,473. The translation reads 872 with Nallino's translation, as the division gives (10,473/12 = 872¾); the text has 892. The bracketed words are Nallino's conjecture. From Ptolemy's ratio of the epicycle, 11;30 to 60, the diameter of the epicycle comes to 22;6 (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0968-L20..AB01-PDF0967-L09
As for the distance of Saturn, the variation of its size to the sight between its nearest and its farthest distance is, as they found it, as one and two fifths to one, which is as seven to five. When that is multiplied by the farthest distance of Jupiter, the farthest distance of Saturn comes to 18,094, and its mean distance is therefore 15,509. They found the diameter of Saturn at its mean distance to be one part in eighteen of the diameter of the sun; when its mean distance is divided by that, the diameter of Saturn comes to about 861 and a half and an eighth. When that is compared with 201½, the diameter of the earth, its diameter is about four times and a sixth and an eighth the diameter of the earth; when that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the size of Saturn is about seventy-nine times the size of the earth. The diameter of Saturn subtends 8;12 of the circle of the sphere. The extent of its epicycle is known [from its motion] from its first station to its second: the motion of its epicycle is two minutes a day, and its motion in the lower part of the epicycle 0;57 a day, seen as if towards the west; so the diameter of its epicycle subtends 12;26. The diameter of the sun must subtend 39;48 of the circle [of the sphere].
@n 12,924 × 7/5 = 18,094 (the codex has 18,074) and (12,924 + 18,094)/2 = 15,509; 15,509/18 is 861 11/18 (Nallino). The bracketed words are Nallino's. From Ptolemy's ratio of the epicycle, 6;30 to 60, its diameter comes to 12;26 (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). The last sentence makes no sense here: al-Battānī's mean apparent diameter of the sun is 0;32,30 (chapter 30), and Schiaparelli takes the sentence for a senseless interpolation (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0967-L09..AB01-PDF0967-L19
As for the distance of the fixed stars and their size, they reported fifteen stars of the first magnitude and said that their distance is, in the manner we have described, about nineteen thousand times the radius of the earth. They measured their size against the sun and found it one part in twenty of the sun. When their distance is divided by that, the diameter of each of these stars is 950; compared with the diameter of the earth, that is about four times and two thirds and half a tenth of it. When that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the size of each of these stars is about one hundred and five times the size of the earth. We have divided the fixed stars that are in the constellations into six magnitudes; each class below these fifteen stars mentioned decreases in size until it comes to the sixth magnitude, where the size of a star is sixteen times the size of the earth. So the greatest of the created bodies of the world is the sun; the second, the fifteen stars mentioned of the first magnitude; the third, Jupiter; the fourth, Saturn; the fifth, the rest of the fixed stars, of the second magnitude [to the sixth]; the sixth, Mars; the seventh, the earth; the eighth, the moon; the ninth, Venus; and the tenth, Mercury, the smallest of the bodies of the world.
@n 950/201½ is 4.71, and (4.71)³ = 105. The bracketed words are supplied by Nallino.
@p AB01-PDF0967-L19..AB01-PDF0966-L02
Whoever wishes to repeat the test of any of these diameters should take an alidade and fit in it two pierced sights, with their holes facing each other; let the hole next to the eye be small, and the one next to the star of a size to take in the whole body of the star, neither more nor less. Let them measure the sun with another alidade in the same way, know the diameter of the hole with which they measured the sun, and compare with it the hole with which they measured the star; and let the measurement be made at one and the same place of the horizon.
@p AB01-PDF0966-L02..AB01-PDF0966-L13
What remains of what we have described of the amounts of the stars is what their diameters subtend of the circle of the sphere at their mean distance; we shall also show the amounts that they must subtend in the measurement when they are at their farthest distance and at their nearest distance. The Sun: the diameter of the sun, we have shown, is 39;48, and we keep it so at all the degrees of its distances, since its change amounts to nothing sensible. The Warrior: the diameter of Saturn subtends at its farthest distance 16;22, at the mean 36;39 and at the nearest 33;36. Jupiter: the diameter of Jupiter subtends at its farthest distance 35;36, at the mean 14;45 and at the nearest 29;36. Mars: the diameter of Mars subtends of the circle of the sphere at its farthest distance 36;10, at the mean 16;14 and at the nearest 33;36. Venus: the diameter of Venus subtends of the sphere at its farthest distance 0;10, at the mean 0;39 and at the nearest 0;36. The Scribe: the diameter of Mercury subtends of the circle of the sphere at its farthest distance 0;33, at the mean 0;6 and at the nearest 0;14. The Moon: the diameter of the moon has been shown to subtend of the circle of the sphere at its farthest distance 0;29,40, at the mean 0;39,36 and at the nearest 0;36,22.
@n Schiaparelli and Nallino take this passage for an interpolation. Its numbers are wrong past repair in the codex and in Plato of Tivoli alike; the mean diameters are mostly smaller than the extreme ones; the sun is again given 39;48; and only here are Saturn and Mercury called al-Muqātil, «the Warrior», and al-Kātib, «the Scribe», names used by the Arabs of north-west Africa and Spain. From al-Battānī's distances and the mean diameter of the sun, 0;32,30, Schiaparelli computes what the passage should have given at the farthest, the mean and the nearest distance: Saturn 0;1,33, 0;1,48, 0;2,10; Jupiter 0;2,12, 0;2,42, 0;3,32; Mars 0;0,56, 0;1,37, 0;6,30; Venus 0;1,53, 0;3,15, 0;12,6; Mercury 0;1,30, 0;2,10, 0;3,54; the moon 0;29,30, 0;32,25, 0;35,20 (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0966-L13..AB01-PDF0966-L18
These amounts vary between the distances mentioned according to the degrees of the stars in their distances, and that is known from their equations. The distance of the star from the point of the farthest distance of the epicycle, and from the point of the nearest distance, is known from the mean equation corrected by the fifth or the seventh column; the distance of the centre of the epicycle from the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric is known from the equation of the anomaly. From these two equations the degree of the star in its distance and nearness to the earth is known, when that is related to the sixty that are the radius, as we have shown in knowing the distances of the moon from the variation of its motions.
@n The chapter on the distances of the moon is chapter 39 (Nallino).
