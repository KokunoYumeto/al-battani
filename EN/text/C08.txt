Chapter 8. On finding the altitude of the pole from the excess of the longest day
@p AB01-PDF1122-L08..AB01-PDF1122-L19
Al-Battānī said: Suppose you wish to find the altitude of the north pole of the equator above the horizon, which is the latitude of the locality, from the excess of the longest day over the equinoctial day, or from the shortfall of the shortest day. Take half the excess of the longest day, the day of the first degree of Cancer, in degrees, and add it to 90; the sum is half the arc of the longest day. Or, if you wish, subtract it from 90; the remainder is half the arc of the shortest day. Whichever of the two you work with, it comes to the same. Then subtract the whole inclination from 90 and find the sine of the remainder: this is the sine of the complement of the whole inclination. Multiply the sine of half the arc of the day by the sine of the complement of the whole inclination, divide the product by the radius, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the distance of the rising of the beginning of Cancer from the north point. Subtract it from 90; the remainder is the distance of the rising of the beginning of Cancer from the equinoctial rising point, as we have explained in the preceding chapter. Then multiply the sine of half the excess of the longest day by the sine of the distance of the rising of the beginning of Cancer from the north point, and divide the product by the sine of the distance of the rising of the beginning of Cancer from the rising of the beginning of Aries. Multiply the quotient by the radius, divide the result by the sine of half the arc of the longest day, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the altitude of the pole where the excess of the longest day is the given amount with which you worked.
@n The equinoctial day (*al-nahār al-muʿtadil*) is the day of 12 hours. In modern terms the rule is sin φ = sin e · cos A / (sin A · sin H), where φ is the latitude, e half the excess of the longest day, H = 90° + e half the arc of the longest day, and A the amplitude of the rising of the beginning of Cancer. The rule is exact, since sin e = tan φ · tan ε, sin A = sin ε / cos φ and cos A = cos ε · sin H.
@n Here the codex writes 90 in Indian figures, «٩٠» (Nallino: «so the codex itself»); elsewhere the text writes numbers in words or in abjad letters.
