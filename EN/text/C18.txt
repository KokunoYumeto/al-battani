Chapter 18. On finding the distances of the stars from the equator, and the parts of the signs that culminate with them
@n The translation reads «تجوز» for «نجوز» (p. 47, line 3), a misprint that the corrigenda do not list.
@p AB01-PDF1106-L14..AB01-PDF1105-L13
Al-Battānī said: Suppose you wish to find the distances of the stars from the equator, and the part of the signs that culminates [with them], from their positions in longitude and latitude. Every star that is on the circle of the signs, that is, that has no latitude, behaves like the sun in its declination from the equator, which is its distance from it. But for a star that has a latitude on one side or the other, its distance from the equator falls short of its latitude and the declination of the part in which it is, when the two are added together or one is subtracted from the other, as the case requires. For the latitude of a star is measured on an arc that passes through the poles of the ecliptic and the position of the star in longitude and latitude, while its distance from the equator is measured on an arc that passes through the poles of the equator and the position of the star. Therefore a star that has a latitude culminates with a part of the signs other than its own, except one that in its course coincides with the point of the beginning of Cancer or of the beginning of Capricorn. Only then do its distance from the equator and its latitude lie on one arc, so that its distance is equal to the declination with the latitude of the star added to it or subtracted from it; and so it culminates with that one of the two points in which it is. Of the stars that have latitude, one between the beginning of Cancer and the end of Sagittarius culminates after the culmination of the degree in which it is, if its latitude is to the north of the belt of the signs, and before the culmination of the degree in which it is, if its latitude is to the south. One between the beginning of Capricorn and the end of Gemini culminates, if its latitude is to the north, with parts that precede the part in which it is, I mean before the culmination of the part in which it is; and if its latitude is to the south, with parts that follow the part in which it is, I mean after the culmination of the part of the signs in which it is.
@n Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary, found the second sentence obscure. With «the declination of the part» understood as its second declination (see the next note), it is a correct statement: the declination δ of a star is never greater than its latitude β and the second declination β′ of its degree taken together, since sin δ = sin(β ± β′) · cos ε / cos β′ and cos β′ ≥ cos ε; it is equal to them only when β′ = ε, at the beginnings of Cancer and Capricorn, as the following sentences say.
@p AB01-PDF1105-L13..AB01-PDF1105-L19
If you wish to find the distance from the equator of one of the stars that have latitude, and the parts of the signs with which it culminates, find the latitude of the star and its direction, and the declination of the part in which it falls. If the latitude and the declination are in the same direction, add them together; if they are in different directions, subtract the smaller from the greater. The result is the adjusted latitude; note the direction in which it falls. Then take the sine of this adjusted latitude, multiply it by the sine of what the whole inclination falls short of 90, divide the product by the sine of what the part falls short of 90, that is, of the complement of its declination, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the distance of the star from the equator, in the direction of the adjusted latitude. Keep it.
@n The «declination» of the part here must be its second declination: the arc of the circle through the poles of the ecliptic between the degree of the star and the equator. With it the rule is exact, sin δ = sin(β ± β′) · cos ε / cos β′ (Nallino, after Schiaparelli, who derives it from Menelaus' theorem). Al-Battānī calls both declinations by the same name, and does not say here how the second is found.
@p AB01-PDF1105-L19..AB01-PDF1104-L08
Then take the distance of the degree in which the star is from the beginning of Cancer or the beginning of Capricorn, whichever the degree is nearer to, before it or behind it. That is: take the times of ascension in the right sphere opposite the part in which the star is. If they are less than 90, take them as they are; if they are more than 270, subtract them from 360. What you obtain in either case is the distance of the star from the beginning of Capricorn. If they are more than 90, up to 180, subtract them from 180 and take the remainder; if they are more than 180, up to the full 270, take 180 from them. What you obtain in either case is the distance of the degree from the beginning of Cancer. Keep whichever it is, and find its sine as well. Then take the sine of the latitude of the star, multiply it by the sine of the whole inclination, and divide the product by the sine of the complement of the distance of the star from the equator. Multiply the quotient by the sine of the distance of the degree in which the star is from the beginning of Cancer or of Capricorn in the right sphere, which you have kept, divide the product by the radius, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the difference of the passage of the star through the middle of the sky.
@n The translation keeps the reading of the codex and of Plato of Tivoli, «by the radius». Nallino, following Schiaparelli, prints in its place «by the sine of what the declination of the degree in which the star is falls short of 90», holding the rule to be false otherwise. The emendation is not needed. The distance of the star's degree from the solstitial point is taken here in the right sphere, as an arc of the equator, and the sine of that distance is cos α, where α is the right ascension of the degree; with it the rule of the codex is exact: sin Δ = sin β · sin ε · cos α / cos δ, where Δ is the difference of the passage. Schiaparelli's own derivation, sin Δ = sin β · sin ε · cos L / (cos D · cos δ), with L the longitude and D the declination of the degree, is the same rule, since cos L = cos α · cos D; Schiaparelli's objection takes the distance to be measured on the ecliptic. With the emendation the result is too large by the factor 1/cos D: for a star at longitude 60° and latitude 10° north, the codex's rule gives 2.45°, the true value, and the emended one 2.61°.

If the star is between the beginning of Cancer and the end of Sagittarius and its latitude is northern, add the difference of its passage through the middle of the sky to the times of ascension in the right sphere opposite the part in which the star is; if its latitude is southern, subtract the difference of its passage from these times, and proceed as before. If the star is between the beginning of Capricorn and the end of Gemini and its latitude is northern, subtract the difference of its passage from these times; if its latitude is southern, add the difference of its passage to the times. Then take the degree of the signs that stands opposite the times of ascension of the star's degree in the right sphere, after the addition or the subtraction, in the ascensions of the right sphere: that is the part of the signs that culminates with the star, I mean [a degree] of the sign in which you found the number of times, if God wills.
