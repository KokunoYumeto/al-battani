Chapter 53. On knowing the times of the revolutions of the years and their ascendants, and the positions of the stars when [the sun] returns to the part in which it was at the root
@p AB01-PDF0960-L09..AB01-PDF0959-L03
He said: If you wish to know the revolution of any year you like of the years of nativities and of other things that are reckoned from a fixed beginning — that is, the time at which the sun returns to the part in which it was at the time of the beginning — find the year of the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn in which the beginning was, and also the year in which you wish to know the revolution. Subtract the smaller from the larger: what remains is the complete years that have passed for the nativity or the beginning you want, up to the same day of the Roman month on which the nativity or the beginning was. Multiply these complete years by 86 degrees and 36 minutes, which is the excess of the length of the year over the complete days; cast off the circles from the product, and divide whatever remains below a circle by 15: the result is equal hours. Add them to the hours of computation at the root. If they come to less than 24, take them as they are, with the day of the month that has passed; if they exceed 24, cast off 24 from them and add one day to the days of the month that have passed. Keep the days and the hours that you then have. If the year is a leap year and Subāṭ has run out, subtract one day from the days of the month that have passed, and the result is the day of computation; if the year is not a leap year, leave the days as they are, and the days of the month that have passed and the hours that result are the days and the hours of computation. With them find the mean of the sun in the year you want, in the way described before: the mean of the sun comes out exactly the same as the mean at the root.
@n The revolution of a year is the moment at which the sun returns to the longitude it had at a birth, or at another beginning such as that of a city or a people; astrologers draw their predictions from it (Nallino's commentary). The root is the moment of that beginning. The heading says only «when it returns»; the text shows that the sun is meant, while Nallino's translation has the planets return to the degree they held at the beginning. 86;36 is the 5 hours 46 minutes 24 seconds by which al-Battānī's year exceeds 365 days, at 15 parts to the hour (Nallino). The rule takes the whole days cast off with the circles to be the leap days that have passed since the root, but they can be fewer, and the day then comes out one too late: after 4 years nothing is cast off, since 4 × 86;36 is 346;24, while one leap day always passes, and the rule subtracts it only when the root falls in a leap year after Subāṭ. A day's error makes the mean sun differ from that of the root by 0;59,8, the sun's motion in a day (chapter 27), and the comparison of the true sun below turns this difference into the missing 24 hours.
@p AB01-PDF0959-L03..AB01-PDF0959-L08
Or, if you like, multiply the years you have by 3 parts and 24 minutes, which is the amount by which the length of the year falls short of the full quarter of a day above 365, and keep the product. Then cast off the years you have four by four: if one remains, take 90 parts for it; if two remain, take 180 for them; if three remain, take 270 for them; and if four remain, take 360 for them. From whichever of these you have, subtract what you kept, and for every 15 degrees of the remainder reckon an hour; add it to the hours of computation in the same way. The meaning of these two ways is one.
@n 3;24 is the quarter of a day, 90 parts, less 86;36 (Nallino). The two ways agree: the years times 86;36 are the years times 90 less the years times 3;24, and the years times 90 leave 90, 180, 270 or 360 when the circles are cast off. From 29 years on, the product can exceed the parts taken, and 360 must then be added to them first.
@p AB01-PDF0959-L08..AB01-PDF0959-L16
Whatever comes out for you by either of the two ways is the date. Compute the mean of the sun for it in the first manner, as usual, then compute its true position. If the true position of the sun comes out the same as the first position, that is the time of the revolution. If it exceeds the true position of the sun at the root, see how much the excess amounts to in the sun's motion in an hour and subtract that from the hours of computation; if it falls short, see how much it falls short in the sun's motion in an hour and add that to the hours of computation, so that you correct the hours of computation by which you bring the sun to the true position in which it was at the root. With them compute also the position of the moon and of the other wandering stars. Then convert them into the hours of the unequal days as they are found, by subtracting the equation of the days that stands opposite the part of the sun in the ascensions of the right sphere, after you know its amount in equal hours: what results is equal hours after noon, with which you find the ascendant and the cardines in the usual way.
@n Converting the hours turns mean time into true time (Nallino). The equation of the days is that of chapter 29; the cardines are those of chapter 35.
@p AB01-PDF0959-L16..AB01-PDF0959-L20
This variation in the true position of the sun arises only from the motion of its apogee in the years between the year of the root and the year of the revolution. When the sun is near the apogee, the change is only slight and imperceptible, and likewise near its perigee; the farther it moves from these two points, the greater the variation in it. It is known that, whenever 106 years have passed, the times of the revolutions come one day before the day of the month on which they were at the root.
@n The rule about the apogee is the wrong way round. At the revolution the mean sun is the same as at the root, so the motion of the apogee changes the sun's anomaly by the same arc, and the true sun moves by that arc times the rate at which the equation changes with the anomaly. That rate is greatest at the apogee and the perigee, where the equation is nothing, and nothing where the equation is greatest. With the radius 2;4,45 of the sun's epicycle (chapter 28), one degree of the apogee's motion, 66 years at al-Battānī's rate (chapter 45), moves the true sun by 2.0′ at the apogee, by 2.2′ at the perigee and by about 5″ at 90° from the apogee. The Roman year of 365 days and a quarter exceeds al-Battānī's year by 13 minutes 36 seconds, which in 106 years make 24 hours 1 minute 36 seconds (Nallino).
