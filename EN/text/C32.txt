Chapter 32. On knowing the eras of the Arabs, the Romans, the Copts and the Persians, and on converting them into one another
@n The translation reads «المستقبلة» for «المستقباة» (p. 105, line 4) and «بتأريخ» for «يتأريخ» (p. 105, line 9), misprints that the corrigenda do not list.
@p AB01-PDF1052-L07..AB01-PDF1052-L09
He said: The names of the months of the Arabs are al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, Rabīʿ I, Rabīʿ II, Jumādā I, Jumādā II, Rajab, Shaʿbān, Ramaḍān, Shawwāl, al-Qaʿda and al-Ḥijja. They are a month of 30 days and a month of 29 days, alternately, except Dhū l-Ḥijja, which has 29 days and a fifth and a sixth.
@n A fifth and a sixth make 11/30 of a day: the lunar year has 354 11/30 days, which rest on the synodic month of 29;31,50 days (Nallino). Over thirty years Dhū l-Ḥijja gains 11 days, and the year in which it has 30 days is a leap year.
@p AB01-PDF1052-L09..AB01-PDF1052-L14
The names of the months of the Romans, with the beginning [of the year] of the Greeks and the people of Egypt: Aylūl, 30 days; Tishrīn I, 31 days; Tishrīn II, 30 days; Kānūn I, 31 days; Kānūn II, also 31 days; Subāṭ, 28 days and a quarter of a day: the quarter is added to the quarter for three successive years, and the year is intercalated in the fourth, Subāṭ then having 29 days; Ādhār, 31 days; Nīsān, 30 days; Ayyār, 31 days; Ḥazīrān, 30 days; Tammūz, 31 days; Āb, 31 days. So the days of the non-Arab year are 365 and a quarter of a day in all, and in the leap year 366; this is the year in which Subāṭ has 29 full days, if God wills.
@n These are the Syrian names of the Julian months, from September to August. Other Arabic writers begin the year with Tishrīn I, October, as was the custom of the Syrians; al-Battānī begins it with 1 September, as the Byzantine indiction did (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1052-L14..AB01-PDF1051-L01
The names of the months of the Persians: Farwardīn-māh [its first day is the Nayrūz], Ardibihisht-māh, Khurdādh-māh, Tīr-māh, Murdādh-māh, Shahrīwar-māh, Mihr-māh, Ābān-māh — the twenty-sixth day of Ābān-māh [begins] the Farwardajān, which is ten days: five of them are the rest of Ābān-māh, up to the thirty that are due to it, and five are days that are dropped and not counted among the months; the sixteenth day of Mihr-māh is the Mihrajān — Ādhar-māh, Day-māh, Bahman-māh and Isfandārmadh-māh. Each of these months has 30 days, and five days after Ābān-māh are a cast-off addition; so the days of the Persian year are 365 in all, without a fraction, if God wills.
@n *Māh* is Persian for «month»; *Nayrūz*, «new day», is the Persian new year. The words in brackets stand after Ardibihisht-māh in the codex; Nallino moves them here. The five added days stood after Ābān-māh in al-Battānī's time; the Farwardajān is the festival of the last ten days of the year.
@p AB01-PDF1051-L01..AB01-PDF1051-L05
The names of the months of the Copts: Tūt, Bābah, Atūr, Kiyahk, Ṭūbah, Amshīr, Barmahāt, Barmūdhah, Bashans, Baʾūnah, Abīb and Misrā. Each of these months has 30 days, and five days, which are cast after the months, are called the added days; so the days of the Coptic year are 365 and a quarter of a day in all, and in the fourth year 366 days. The era of the Romans and the Copts is, for the people of Egypt, from the death of Alexander the Macedonian, and for the Romans from the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn Alexander; between the two there are twelve Egyptian years.
@n The era of Dhū l-Qarnayn is the Seleucid era, which al-Battānī begins on Saturday 1 September 312 BC; the era from the death of Alexander is the era of Philip, which he takes from Theon's tables (Nallino). The Coptic era of this chapter, as the rules below show, is the Alexandrian era of Augustus, whose first day al-Battānī puts on Friday 29 August 25 BC, 287 Julian years less 3 days after the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1051-L06..AB01-PDF1051-L17
If you wish to know, by the years of the Hijra, the beginning of any month of the years of the Arabs that you want, take the complete years of the Hijra and multiply them by 354 days and a fifth and a sixth of a day. Look at the result: if there is a fraction in it, and the fraction is less than half a day, drop it and do not count it; if it is more than half a day, count it as a day and add it to the sum of the days. The number of days that results is the number of days that have passed from the beginning of the Hijra to the end of that complete year; it is the root, so keep it. Then take this root, add 5 days to it, and cast out the sum seven by seven; what remains, less than seven or seven, is the sign of the coming year. Cast it from Sunday, and the reckoning brings you to the day on which al-Muḥarram begins in the year you are in, which is the incomplete year. If you want another of the months, add to the sign of the year, for the months of the complete year that have passed, two days for one month and one day for the next, which makes three days for every two full lunar months; if there is a single month, or a single month remains, take two days for it. Then cast that out seven by seven, and cast what remains, less than seven or seven, from Sunday; the reckoning stops at the day on which the month whose sign you sought begins. This is the reckoning on which the zījes and the chronologies are made; do not go past it to another, whether it gives more or less.
@n To cast a number from Sunday is to count it off from Sunday: 1 is Sunday, 2 Monday, and 7 Saturday. With no complete years the sign is 5, a Thursday: the astronomers' first day of the Hijra, 15 July 622; in civil use the era began on Friday (Nallino). Months of 30 and 29 days leave 2 and 1 when the weeks are cast out.
@p AB01-PDF1051-L17..AB01-PDF1050-L04
If you wish to know the beginnings of the Roman months by the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn, with the beginning [of the year] of the Egyptians, take the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn and add to them a quarter of them. If there is a fraction in the result, do not count it, whether it is more than a half or less; then multiply the total by 365 days and cast out the result seven by seven. What remains, less than seven or seven, is the sign of the year; cast it in the first way, and you come to the first day of Aylūl of the coming year, the one you are in. If the fraction is exactly half, the year that is coming in on you, I mean the coming year, is a leap year; if it is more than half or less, it is not. If you want another month than Aylūl, add to the sign of the year, for the complete months of the year that have passed, two days for each month of 30 days and three days for each month of 31 days, and take nothing for Subāṭ unless the year is a leap year, when you take one day for it. Cast out the result seven by seven and proceed with it in the way of casting given before, and you come to the first day of the month that you want, if God wills.
@n Since 365 is 52 weeks and 1 day, the multiplication by 365 does not change what remains when the sevens are cast out. As stated, the rule leaves out the leap day in the year after a leap year: for 1 Aylūl 1192 of Dhū l-Qarnayn, 1 September 880, a Thursday, it gives Wednesday. Counting a fraction of three quarters as a whole day gives the right day in every year.
@p AB01-PDF1050-L04..AB01-PDF1050-L10
If you wish to know the beginnings of the Persian months by their known years, take the complete years of Yazdajird son of Shahriyār son of Kisrā, king of the Persians, always add three to them, and multiply them by 365. Cast out the result seven by seven, and cast what remains, less than seven or seven, from Sunday; the day at which the count stops is the first day of Farwardīn-māh, which is the day of the Nayrūz. If you want another of the Persian months, add to the sign of the year, by which you found the day of the Nayrūz, two days for each of the complete months of the year that have passed, except Ābān-māh, for which you take nothing. Then cast that out seven by seven, proceed in the way of casting it from Sunday, and take the day at which the count stops as the beginning of the month that you sought.
@n The era of Yazdajird began on Tuesday 16 June 632 (Nallino). Ābān-māh with the five added days has 35 days, a whole number of weeks (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1050-L10..AB01-PDF1050-L18
Know that the Copts precede the Greeks of the people of Egypt at the entry of Aylūl by three days, and that they [the Greeks] outrun them in the era by one day every four years. If you wish to know the beginnings of the months of the Copts, take the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, always add six to them, and multiply it by the days of the year. Cast out the result seven by seven and carry out what remains, less than seven or seven, in the way given before; where the count brings you is the first day of Aylūl, which is Tūt, of the coming year. If you want another of the months, add to the sign of the year two days for each complete month of the year that has passed; cast out the result seven by seven and cast what remains, less than seven or seven, from Sunday; the day to which the count brings you is the beginning of the month that you want. If all the months have run out, cast five more days after that, and then the coming year begins, because those days are the added days, which are not counted among the months, if God wills.
@n In the year before a Roman leap year the Copts begin their year only two days before Aylūl: the Alexandrians put their extra day at the end of that year (Nallino, after a chronological fragment ascribed to Heraclius). The six makes the count begin on a Friday, the first day of the Coptic era as al-Battānī reckons it; the codex has 7, Plato of Tivoli 5 (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1050-L19..AB01-PDF1049-L05
If you wish to know the era of the Romans by the era of the Hijra — this is the conversion — and so to know the day of the Roman months on which you are and how many years [have passed] of Dhū l-Qarnayn, take the Arab root that I told you to keep and add to it 317. Add to the result the Arab months and days of the year that have passed, and divide the total by 365 days and a quarter of a day. What comes out is complete years; always add 932 years to them, and the total is the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, so keep them. From the days that remain short of a year cast off, for each month, the number of its days, beginning with Aylūl: what comes out is complete months, and what does not make up a month is the days that have passed of the month at which you stopped, which is the current month, the one you are in. If a fraction is left over, do not count it; if the fraction is exactly a half, the year you are in, which is the coming year, not included in the number of years that you kept, is a leap year, so take 29 full days for Subāṭ in that year, if God wills.
@n The 317 days run from 1 Aylūl 933 of Dhū l-Qarnayn, 1 September 621, to the first day of the Hijra, 15 July 622; 932 years of the era were then complete (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1049-L05..AB01-PDF1049-L17
If you wish to know the era of the Copts exactly from the era of the Romans, take the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn together with the year that you are in, even if only one day of it has begun, and take 287 from them. Take a quarter of what remains: if there is a fraction in it, do not count it; if there is no fraction, the year that you are in is a leap year. When there is no fraction, take one day from what the quarters give you, unless Subāṭ has run out; once Subāṭ has run out, add that day to the quarters. Always add to the result the three days by which the Copts precede the Greeks at the entry of Aylūl, which is Tūt, and add to the result [the days] from the first of Aylūl to the day that you are in. If that is more than 365, cast off 365 from it, and add a complete year to the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn that you have. If the year is a leap year and Subāṭ has run out, take 29 days [for it] and cast off 366 days from the days that you have collected. The days that remain after that are the days that have passed of the Copts in the year of the Copts that you are in; cast off 30 days for each month, beginning with Tūt. What comes out is complete months, and what remains below 30 is the days that have passed of the month of the Copts that you are in. With this era the motions of the stars are found by Theon's canon, after 12 years have been added to the years, so that they run from the death of Alexander the Macedonian; and do not count the first month set down in the tables among the months.
@n 287 is the number of years between the era of Dhū l-Qarnayn and the Coptic era of this chapter (Nallino). Theon's tables give the motions at the beginnings, not the ends, of the months, days and hours, so that their first month adds nothing (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1049-L17..AB01-PDF1048-L04
If you wish to know the era of the Persians from the era of the Hijra by computation, take the Arab root that I told you to keep, and add to it, for the months of the year that have passed, 30 days for one month and 29 days for the next; add also the days that have passed of the Arab month that you are in. The result is the number of days that have passed from the beginning of the Hijra to the day that you want. Take from it 3624 days, the days between the Hijra and Yazdajird, and divide the remainder by 365: what comes out is complete years from the death of Yazdajird. From what remains below 365 take for each month the number of its days, beginning with Farwardīn-māh; the day at which you stop is the day that has passed of the current month of the Persians that you want. When you count Ābān-māh, reckon 35 days for it, for the five added days that go with it, I mean that are dropped after it and not counted. The day that follows the day on which the number of the days of the Persian year is complete is the day of the Nayrūz among the months of the Persians. Know this and understand it, and you will find what is right, if God wills.
@n The 3624 days run from the first day of the Hijra, 15 July 622, to the first day of the era of Yazdajird, 16 June 632. «The death of Yazdajird» is a slip for his accession, which is not rare in Arabic writers (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1048-L04..AB01-PDF1048-L13
If you wish to know the era of the Hijra from the era of the Romans, with the beginning of the Egyptians, take 932 years from the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn and multiply the remainder by 365 days and a quarter of a day; if there is a fraction, keep it. Then take 317 days from the days that you have collected, and add to the remainder the days that have passed of the year that you are in, from the first of Aylūl to the day that you are in. The result is the number of days that have passed from the first year of the Hijra to the day that you want. Divide it by 354 days and a fifth and a sixth of a day: what comes out is complete years that have passed from the beginning of the Hijra. If what remains below that has a fraction, and it is less than a half, drop it and do not count it; if it is more than a half, make it up to a day and add it to the days. Then take the days out from al-Muḥarram, for each month the number of its days, a month of 30 days and a month of 29 days. What comes out is complete months that have passed of the coming year, I mean the current year of the years of the Hijra that you are in, which is not included in the number of years; and the days that remain below a month are what has passed of the month of the Arabs that you are in.
@p AB01-PDF1048-L14..AB01-PDF1048-L16
If you seek the era of the Hijra from the era of the Persians, take the complete years of Yazdajird and multiply them by 365 days; add to the result [the days] from the first of Farwardīn-māh to the day that you want, and add to the total 3624. The result is the days that have passed from the beginning of the Hijra; make them Arab years in the way given before.
@p AB01-PDF1048-L16..AB01-PDF1047-L04
If you wish to know what has passed of the era of the Persians from the era of the Romans by computation, take the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn and cast off 943 years from them; what remains is the years that you want, so keep them. Then take a quarter of them; if there is a fraction in it, do not count it. To the days of the quarters always add 77 days, and add to the result [the days] from the first of Aylūl to the day that you want. If the total is more than 365 days, cast off 365 days from it and add another year to the years that you kept; from the days that remain cast off for each month the number of its days, beginning with the first of Farwardīn-māh, in the way given before. If the fraction that comes from the quarters is three quarters, that year is a leap year, so take 29 days for Subāṭ in it. If you need to know on which day of the Roman months the Nayrūz of the coming year falls, take what you have collected from the quarters together with the 77 and always subtract it from 366; cast off what remains from Aylūl, for each month the number of its days. The day of that Roman month at which you stop is the day of the Nayrūz, which is the first day of the coming year of the years of the Persians that you are in. What follows the Nayrūz in the days and months of the Persians is as I have described.
@n 943 years of Dhū l-Qarnayn were complete on 1 September 632, and the 77 days run from the beginning of the era of Yazdajird, 16 June 632, to that day (Nallino). The Persian year has no quarter day, so the days of the quarters are added.
@p AB01-PDF1047-L05..AB01-PDF1047-L09
If you wish to know what has passed of the era of the Romans from the era of the Persians by computation, take the complete years of the Persians and multiply them by 365 days; add to that [the days] from the first of Farwardīn-māh to the day that you want, and divide the result by 365 days and a quarter of a day. What comes out is complete years; add to them 943 years, and the result is the complete years of Dhū l-Qarnayn. Cast off what remains of the days from Aylūl, for each month the number of its days, and do not count a fraction; if no fraction remains, the year is a leap year, so allow 29 days for Subāṭ in that year.
@n As stated, the rule counts the days of the Persian era from 1 Aylūl, although the era began on 16 Ḥazīrān, 16 June 632, 77 days earlier: the 77 days must first be taken from the days, as the converse rule above adds them.
@p AB01-PDF1047-L09..AB01-PDF1047-L16
If you wish to know the era of the Romans by the era of the Copts, take the years of the Copts, which are the Egyptian years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, the complete ones, and cast off 287 from them. Find a quarter of what remains, and whatever it is, take it from the days that have passed of the coming year of the years of the Copts that you are in, from the first of Tūt to the day that you want. Cast off three days from what remains, and cast off the rest from the first of Aylūl: where you arrive is the day that has passed of the Roman month that you are in. If the days of the quarters are more than the days collected from the first of Tūt, take a year from the years of the Copts, add 365 days to the days that you have, and take from them the days that come from the quarters; take out what remains from Aylūl in the way given before. When there is a fraction in the quarters, do not count it.
@p AB01-PDF1047-L16..AB01-PDF1047-L21
If you add 12 years to the complete years of the Copts, they run from the death of Alexander the Macedonian; if you then add to that 424 Egyptian years, the total is the years of Ptolemy's book, with which he worked in finding the motions, from the beginning of the reign of Nabonassar to the year at which you arrive of the years of the Copts and their days of the coming year. We have made tables for the eras of the Arabs and the Romans, by which they are found from one another, and tables by which the beginnings of their months are found, and we have explained the working with them at those tables, so that whatever is needed of this may be easily known at any time you wish, if God wills.
@n The Arabic calls Nabonassar «the first Bukhtanaṣṣar»; the era of Philip began 424 Egyptian years after the era of Nabonassar (Nallino).
