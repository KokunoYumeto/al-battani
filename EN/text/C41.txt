Chapter 41. On the seeing of the crescent at the beginnings and the ends of the months, the azimuth of its place in which it is seen at its altitude and its depression, and the shape of its figure according to the light in it, and the levelness of its horns and their inclination to the belt of the signs
@p AB01-PDF1023-L04..AB01-PDF1023-L12
He said: The knowledge of the seeing of the crescent at the beginnings and the ends of the months is among the most useful of the things whose knowledge has been given, since the era of the Arabs and the beginnings of their months depend on the seeing of the crescents. Knowing it truly has some difficulty, from various sides: among them the nearness of the moon to the sun and its distance from it, its distance from the earth and its nearness to it, the variation of the latitude of the moon on the northern and the southern side, then the parallax that occurs in the longitude and the latitude of the moon in every locality, the shortness and length of the risings and the settings of the signs in the climes, and the abundance and scarcity of the light in it. For this reason some people of our time who sought the knowledge of it fell into error in knowing the seeing of the crescents, and fell short of reaching the truth of things. They even imagined that the distance of a star from the equator and the latitude of the star come out together on one arc; they worked on the assumption that the parallax of the moon does not come from its variation on the circle of altitude, but arises from its distance from the middle of the sky in degrees of the signs; and they multiplied arcs into sines, with principles that they had adopted before, which measurement does not require and demonstration does not confirm.
@n The Muslim months begin with the first sighting of the crescent after the conjunction, attested by witnesses; computation was admitted only by a few jurists of the Shāfiʿite school (Nallino). Nallino supposes that those who erred were jurists or the timekeepers of the mosques.
@p AB01-PDF1023-L13..AB01-PDF1023-L19
As for the ancients, they were not compelled to know this, because the era with them, with which they worked, is the years of the sun, and the beginnings of the lunar months were known to them from the times of the conjunctions, whose truth computation shows. For this reason they set aside any mention of it, despite how much arises in it of what we have mentioned, except in general terms: they mentioned that the crescent cannot be seen in less than a day and a night. When the causes of the seeing are examined, this statement is found to be the principle on which one works. For the amount of the seeing found by observation, although it comes close to the amount that appears in this way mentioned, is known, when the matter is examined, not to be graspable with the most exact truth: what is grasped of it is grasped only approximately.
@p AB01-PDF1023-L19..AB01-PDF1022-L17
Since the knowledge of the seeing of the crescent found by observation is right only from the amounts of the arcs of the equator that lie between the sun and the moon at sunrise or at sunset — when these arcs are observed in one of the climes, the amount is known in one clime, and when it is known in one clime it is known in all the climes — that on which people's opinions agree for the amount of the arc of seeing is, as we found by observation, about 12 parts of the times of the equator. It is clear that the course of the moon, when it parts from the sun, in a day and a night, when the mean course of the sun in a day and a night is taken from it, is 12 parts 11 minutes; that is the amount of the distance that arises between the sun and the moon in parts of the signs, and this agrees approximately with what is taken by observation when these parts are of the equator. It is evident that the amount of these times mentioned is about four fifths of an hour, and we find that the moon gains on the sun in this amount of an equal hour about two fifths of a part. So when the sun sets with about 11 and a half and a quarter times of the equator between it and the moon, the moon does not set until the 12 parts 11 minutes are complete. On this reckoning, therefore, the mean arc of seeing is 11 parts and a half and a quarter of a part of the times of the equator, which are the risings and the settings of the signs in the localities. What is lit of the circle of the moon when the distance of the moon from the sun is the amount of these parts of the ecliptic [is] about four fifths of a part, if the whole circle of the moon is 12 parts. The moon may be farther from the sun than this amount, or less, at the times of seeing, so that the light in it is more or less according to the amounts of the distance, and it is seen at less than this arc or at more; moreover, it is near the earth or far from it at those times because of its position on the epicycle, which adds to these amounts or takes from them. For this reason the crescent cannot be seen at one and the same arc: it is seen at different arcs.
@n The times of the equator are degrees of the equator, 15 to the hour. 12;11 is the mean daily gain of the moon on the sun (chapter 30): the moon gains about two fifths of a degree in the four fifths of an hour that 12 times take to set, so that 11;47, which al-Battānī rounds to 11;45, is the mean arc of seeing (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). Four fifths of a part of light follows the rule of chapter 30, one part for each 15 parts of distance. The bracketed word is supplied by Nallino, who also restores the order of the words of the codex.
@p AB01-PDF1022-L17..AB01-PDF1021-L10
If you wish to know whether the crescent will be seen or not, in this way, compute the sun and the moon for the time of sunset of the second day from the conjunction, which is the 29th day of the Arab month, and find their true positions on the ecliptic in the locality that you want; find also the true latitude of the moon and its side. Then find the amount of the parallax of the moon at the time of sunset in longitude and latitude in those ways, so that the visible position of the moon on the ecliptic in longitude and latitude and the side of the latitude come out right for you. When you know that, find its visible distance from the equator and the degree that culminates with it, and with them find half the arc of the day of the moon, which is half its stay above the earth, in the way explained at the beginning of the book, in the chapter on knowing the distance of a star from the equator and the degree that culminates with it from the latitude of the star and the declination of the degree in which it is, and in the chapter on knowing half the arc of the day of a star from its distance from the equator. Add what results of half the arc of the day of the moon to the times of the ascensions of the degree that culminates with it in the right sphere: the result is the times of the ascensions of the counterpart of the degree with which the moon sets in that clime. Subtract from them the times of the ascensions that stand opposite the degree opposite the degree of the sun in that clime: the remainder is the distance between the sun and the moon in degrees of the settings, so keep it. Then find the true degree in which the moon was, and its true latitude; take what lies between the true degrees of the sun and of the moon, multiply it by itself, add to it the latitude of the moon multiplied by itself, and take the root of the sum: the result is the distance of the moon from the sun, approximately.
@n The crescent is looked for at the sunset that ends the 29th day, which begins the night of the 30th, the Muslim day beginning at sunset; Nallino translates «[after] the 29th day». The chapters cited are 18 and 19. The distance between the sun and the moon in degrees of the settings is counted with the oblique ascensions of the opposite degrees, since a degree sets with the rising of its counterpart.
@p AB01-PDF1021-L10..AB01-PDF1020-L08
If you wish to know this from what we mentioned at the beginning of the book, in the chapter on knowing the distances between the stars in their arrangement on the sphere, that is more correct and more exact. If the distance of the moon from the sun is [more than] 12;11, take what exceeds 12;11, and if it is less, find what it falls short of it, and note the amount of the excess or the shortfall with its mark. Then see what part of 12;11, which is the amount of the light in the moon for seeing, that excess or that shortfall is, and take that much of the excess or of the shortfall: that is the part, by which the arc of seeing grows or decreases. Then enter with the corrected anomaly of the moon into the table of the computation and take the minutes that stand opposite it in the third column, in which the shares of the distances of the moon are set down. If those minutes are exactly 30, the moon is at its mean distance from the earth; then, if that part has the mark of the shortfall, add it to 11;45, which is the amount of the arc of seeing, and if it has the mark of the excess, subtract it from 11;45. If the minutes are more than 30 or less than 30, look at what exceeds 30 or falls short of it, find what part of the 30 minutes it is, and take as much of the part. Of the result take half a sixth, since the diameter of the moon varies so that its excess over its mean diameter and its shortfall from it are about half a sixth of its mean diameter. Add what you obtain of the half sixth to the part if the part has the mark of the excess and the minutes of the third column are more than 30; if the minutes of the third column are less than 30, subtract that half sixth from the part. If the part has the mark of the shortfall and the minutes are more than 30, subtract the half sixth that you obtained from that part, and if the minutes are less than 30, add it to the part. Then look at what the part comes to after the addition or the subtraction: if it has the mark of the excess over 12;11, subtract it from 11;45, and if it has the mark of the shortfall, add it to 11;45. The result is the amount of the arc of seeing corrected for the increase and the decrease of the light of the moon in its distance from the earth at that time. If what you kept of [the arc] between the sun and the moon in degrees of the settings is equal to the corrected arc of seeing or more than it, the crescent will be seen; if it is less than the corrected arc of seeing, it will not be seen in that locality.
@n The chapter on the distances between the stars is chapter 26. The words in brackets are supplied by Nallino; the codex has 41;45 for 11;45 here and below. As the text stands, the fraction found is taken of the excess or the shortfall itself. In Schiaparelli's account of the rule (Nallino's commentary) the arc of seeing, 11;45, is reduced in the inverse ratio of the elongation to 12;11, a greater elongation giving a broader crescent, so that to first order the part is that fraction of 11;45; in the parallel rule below Nallino supplies «of those 10;50». The arc is then corrected for the distance of the moon: the third column, counted with the two columns of numbers, has 30 at the mean distance, 0 at the farthest and 60 at the nearest, and the apparent diameter of the moon, 0;29,30 to 0;35,20 (chapter 30), varies by about a twelfth on either side of its mean.
@p AB01-PDF1020-L08..AB01-PDF1020-L14
Clearness and purity of the air help the seeing of the crescent, and its thickness and turbidity hinder it, besides the differences that arise between eyes in looking, in strength and weakness. The twilight may be thick and then grow thin before the moon sets from the horizon and reaches the limit of setting, and then the crescent is seen after the time of seeing on which one works. For this reason one should not despair of seeing the crescent until it is known to have set, if it is at a place of seeing, and it is certain that it has gone down below the horizon; then one may despair of it. For these reasons it may be seen in one place and not in another; the like happens also because of the difference of the risings and the settings of the signs in the localities, in length and shortness.
@p AB01-PDF1020-L14..AB01-PDF1019-L06
As for what opinion inclines to, and whose truth is not doubted, according to what the ancients set down about the seeing of the crescent in what they laid down and described, that it cannot be seen in less than a day and a night: if we take the distance of the moon from the sun when the moon moves with its least course and the sun with its greatest, that is, when the moon is at its farthest distance on the epicycle and the sun at its nearest distance, we find its distance from the sun in a day and a night to be 10 parts and a half and a third of a part, and that is the amount of the arc of seeing on the equator by this reckoning. When the moon moves with its greatest course and the sun with its least, that is, where the moon is at its nearest distance and the sun at its farthest distance on the epicycle, we find the distance of the moon from the sun in a day and a night to be about 13⅔ parts. So we use this amount of the ecliptic for the light that is in the moon at the time of seeing, and we say that when there are 10 parts and a half and a third of the times of the equator between the sun and the moon, and its distance from the sun in parts of the signs is 13⅔ parts, it is at the place of its seeing, unless something of the state of the air that we have mentioned prevents it; no doubt enters into this for us. Since the moon may be farther from the sun than these parts of the ecliptic mentioned, or less, and may move on the epicycle away from the point of the farthest distance towards its nearest distance, the amount of the seeing changes for that reason, as we said just now.
@n Schiaparelli calls this explanation of the two numbers a rather strange one (Nallino's commentary). Plato of Tivoli has 13;30.

If you wish to know the truth of the seeing in this way, compute the sun and the moon for the time mentioned in those ways, so that you know its distance from the sun in parts of the settings of the locality. Then find the distance of the moon from the sun in parts of the signs according to the latitude of the moon, in that way. If it is more than 13;40, find the amount of the excess, and if it is less than that, find the amount of the shortfall; see what part of 13;40 either of them is, and take that much of it: that is the part. If the moon is at its farthest distance, in which it was at the time of the given amount of the arc of seeing — which happens when the corrected anomaly of the moon is about 360, with no excess or shortfall except of no account — subtract that part from 10;50 if it has the mark of the excess, and add it to 10;50 if it has the mark of the shortfall: the result after that is the corrected arc of seeing. If the moon has left its farthest distance, enter with its corrected anomaly into the tables of the computation and take the minutes that are in the third column; find what part of 60 they are, and take that part of the part. Take a fifth of the result, since the excess of the greatest diameter of the moon over its least diameter is that part of its least diameter. Subtract what you obtain of the fifth from that part that has resulted for you if the part has the mark of the shortfall, and add it to it if it has the mark of the excess. See how much the part comes to after the addition or the subtraction in times of the equator; add the result to 10;50 if the part has the mark of the shortfall from 13;40, and subtract it from it if it has the mark of the excess: the result is the amount of the corrected arc of seeing. If it is equal to the distance between the sun and the moon in times of the settings, or less than it, you know that the moon is at the place of seeing, no doubt about it, whether some of what we have mentioned hinders it or not; if the corrected arc is more than the times of the settings, you know that the crescent cannot be seen in that locality. We know how much the part that results comes to in times of the equator in this way. We enter with the times that we described as the times of the ascensions of the degree opposite the degree of the moon in the clime, and find what stands opposite them of the degrees of the signs: that is the degree opposite the degree with which the moon sets. We add to it what has resulted of the part, and keep the result; this we do if the part has the mark of the excess. If the part has the mark of the shortfall, we subtract what has resulted of the part from the degrees of the signs that we have obtained, and keep the remainder. Whichever of the two happens, we find what stands opposite it of the times of the ascensions, and see how much they exceed those first times, the times of the ascensions of the degree opposite the degree of the moon, or how much they fall short of them: the result is the amount of the part that has resulted, in times of the equator. We subtract it from the arc of seeing or add it to it, as is due, if God wills.

As for seeing the moon in the mornings at the ends of the months, it is on this pattern, except that you use the times of the ascensions of the degree of the sun itself and the times of the ascensions of the degree of the moon itself. You find the times of the ascensions of the degree of the moon by subtracting half the arc of the day of the moon from the times of the ascensions of the degree that culminates with it in the right sphere: the remainder is the times of the ascensions of the degree that rises with the moon in the clime. Subtract from that the times of the ascensions of the degree of the sun: the remainder is the amount between the sun and the moon in times of the ascensions, when the moon is on the eastern side. If the arc of seeing that results is equal to the distance between the sun and the moon in times of the ascensions, or less than it, the moon is seen before sunrise in the morning; if it is more, it has been hidden by the rays and is not seen. The computation of the sun and the moon should be made for the time of sunrise of the 28th day of the Arab month, which is a day before the conjunction.

If you wish to draw the shape of the crescent in the state in which it is seen, with its two horns level or inclined, and the amount of the light that is in it: divide the distance between the sun and the moon in parts of the signs, according to the latitude of the moon, by 15, so that the result is a part out of 12 parts of the circle of the moon: the result is the digits of light. Then draw a circle of any size you wish, divide it into quarters by two lines that intersect at the centre at right angles, mark on the ends of the lines their directions on the horizon, and divide each quarter of the circle into 90 parts. Then mark, according to the true latitude of the moon, from the point of the east and the point of the west towards the side of the latitude of the moon, two marks, so that the amount of each of the two arcs is as much as the latitude of the moon. Then put the edge of the ruler on the two marks and draw along it a straight line that passes through the two marks and is parallel to a diameter of the circle, and extend it beyond the circumference of the circle towards the east by the amount of the radius of the circle. On this line is the passage of the moon in longitude at that time, and at the other times according to the latitude that it may have, from the time of its first appearance to the time of its half light, for the centre of its circle then falls at the point where the circumference of the circle meets this line; and from the time of its half light to the time of its fullness the centre of its circle is on the line that goes out from the circumference of the circle and runs towards the east, until it reaches the end of the line, between its circle and the circle of the sun. So the first circle, drawn for the sun, is the circle of the moon at its fullness. Then count on the circumference of the circle, from the point of the north towards the east, the same number as is between the sun and the moon, and likewise from the side of the south towards the east, mark two marks on it, and join one of the marks to the other by a straight line: where the two lines intersect is the centre of the circle of the moon. Draw about it a circle the size of the first circle: the crescent that falls between the two arcs is in the shape of the crescent and the image of its appearance. Then join the two points at which the two circles intersect by a straight line, which is a second diameter of the circle, and draw also a straight line that passes through the centres of the two circles and through the two arcs and divides the crescent into two halves. From this it becomes clear to you how much each of the two horns of the crescent inclines from the middle of the belt of the signs, by the parts into which you divided the circumference, for the ecliptic then has a known limit on the horizon, from the azimuth on the circle of the horizon of what rises and sets with it at that time.
@n Twelve digits are the whole visible half of the moon at the full, each 15 degrees of the semicircle of its disc (Nallino); Plato of Tivoli has 15 for these 12 parts. The construction gives the inclination of the line of the horns to the ecliptic quite exactly, as long as the elongation is not much greater than on the first evening of visibility. The figure between the two arcs is the visible lune; the crescent actually seen is much thinner, since that part of the moon's surface is seen very obliquely (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).

He said: We draw the circle of the horizon mentioned, marked ABGD, about the centre H, and let the point H be the place of the centre of the circle of the horizon on the plane, which is the zenith; the point A the point of the south, the point B the point of the east, the point G the point of the north, and the point D the point of the west. We draw the two lines AG and BD. We take the moon to be on the western side, which is the quarter AD, and make the point B of the ecliptic the beginning of Aries, so that the point D is the beginning of Libra: these are the rising and the setting points of the ecliptic. We take the southern half of the ecliptic to be the arc DLB; then the line GL is clearly the place of the beginning of Capricorn, which is on the meridian line. Let the degree that culminates with the moon be the point Ṭ of the ecliptic, which is the beginning of Scorpio. We put the sign Z at the place of the moon in its southern latitude, and make the line KHṬZḤ the place of the edge of the ruler, or of the middle of the thickness of the tube, which passes through the centre of the circle, through the place of the moon and through the degree that culminates with it. We find the arc DḤ of the horizon. Clearly the arc ṬḤ is the altitude above the horizon of the degree that culminates with the moon, and the arc ZḤ the altitude of the moon above it; likewise the arc AL is the altitude of the beginning of Capricorn in the middle of the sky, and the arc DṬ of the ecliptic is that from the point of the beginning of Libra to the degree that culminates with the moon; and the point Ḥ is the azimuth of the moon. So the arc DḤ of the horizon is the distance of the azimuth of the moon from the point of the equinoctial setting. When the line KḤ is raised from the point H and the point Ḥ, by the amount of the altitude of the moon set on the astrolabe, towards the air, the place K of it goes down towards the earth, the sight passes through the two holes of the alidade of the astrolabe, which are the points M and K, and the whole line is joined, so that the whole line MḤ becomes one straight line. When the observer looks from the place K or the place M, the crescent will be seen with that pointer in the direction of the line KḤ, if the air is clear and thin, no doubt about it. If the air is changeable and turbid, it prevents its being seen in that town; but it is seen in other towns whose distance from the equator is like the distance of that town, since a change of the air need not extend over every locality. For this reason also it may not be seen in the villages and dwellings near it.
@n The words «which is the beginning of Scorpio», «and through the degree that culminates with it», «the arc ṬḤ is the altitude above the horizon of the degree that culminates with the moon, and» and «the arc DṬ of the ecliptic is that from the point of the beginning of Libra to the degree that culminates with the moon», though they are in the codex and in Plato of Tivoli, should be struck out (Nallino, after Schiaparelli): the degree that culminates with the moon lies on the great circle through the moon and the pole of the equator, not on the line of sight, and serves no purpose here. Nallino's translation accordingly names the line KHZḤ. In the last sentence Nallino translates «it may be seen in the villages near it».
