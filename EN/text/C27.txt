Chapter 27. On finding the length of the year and the motion of the sun in it
@p AB01-PDF1091-L05..AB01-PDF1091-L12
Al-Battānī said: The ancients disagreed about the length of the year. Some of the ancient people of Egypt and Babylon said that it is 365 days, a quarter of a day and 1/120 of a day. Ptolemy says that they reckoned it from the sun's leaving one of the fixed stars until it returns to it, and finds fault with them for this and says that it is most absurd. For if this view were allowed, nothing at all would prevent us from saying that the year is the time from the sun's leaving Saturn, or another of the wandering stars, until it returns to it, and this view is very plainly false. The year is only the time from the sun's leaving an unmoving point of the sphere until it returns to it, either from one of the two equinoctial points to the same point, or from one of the two solstitial points to the same point; for no starting points on the ecliptic are more fitting than these points.
@n 365 1/4 + 1/120 days is 365 days 6 hours 12 minutes, a year measured by the fixed stars (Nallino). Ptolemy discusses the length of the year in *Almagest* III 1.
@p AB01-PDF1091-L12..AB01-PDF1091-L16
Hipparchus worked with a year of 365 days and a quarter of a day only, although it had become clear to Hipparchus that it is less than that, as Ptolemy reports in collecting Hipparchus's views. For Hipparchus said that the year is in truth 365 days and less than a quarter of a day, having found that the summer solstice came before the time of the full quarter day in excess of 365 days. This made Hipparchus uncertain about the motion of the sun, to the point of imagining that it had another sphere, eccentric to the centres of the two spheres.
@n Hipparchus found 365 1/4 − 1/300 days, that is 365 days 5 hours 55 minutes 12 seconds (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1091-L17..AB01-PDF1090-L06
The ancients took this mostly from summer observations, made when the sun passes the summer solstitial point. These are not found to be as accurate as observations made when the sun passes one of the two equinoctial points, especially the autumnal equinoctial point, because the air is clearer and purer at that time than at the time of the spring equinox. For when the sun passes the solstitial point it moves slowly in declination, and when it passes the two equinoctial points its motion in declination is very fast. That is why Ptolemy relied only on the autumnal observations and based the reckoning on them. One of the observations of Hipparchus on which Ptolemy relied, and of whose truth there was no doubt, is the observation reported by Hipparchus, who found that the sun passed the autumnal equinoctial point in the year 178 after the death of Alexander, on the third of the five epagomenal days, at midnight at Alexandria, of the night whose morning was the fourth of the epagomenal days, and was sure of it.
@n Ptolemy does not say that Hipparchus made this observation at Alexandria; Hipparchus observed at Rhodes, which was taken to lie on the meridian of Alexandria (Nallino). The epagomenal days are the five days added after the twelve months of 30 days of the Egyptian year.
@p AB01-PDF1090-L06..AB01-PDF1090-L16
Ptolemy observed 285 Egyptian years later; this is the observation that Ptolemy's book says was verified and made exact with the greatest care. Ptolemy found that the sun passed the autumnal equinoctial point in the third year of the reign of Antoninus, which is the year 463 after the death of Alexander, on the ninth day of Athyr, one of the months of the Copts, about one hour after sunrise at Alexandria. Taking the time between the two observations, Ptolemy found it to be in truth 285 Egyptian years, 70 days and a quarter of a day and 1/20 of a day, in place of the 71 days and a quarter that should accumulate from the full quarters in these 285 years. The ratio of this one day less 1/20 of a day, by which the time of the observation came before the time of the quarter day in excess of 365 days, to the 285 years between the two observations is as the ratio of one day to 300 years. So the length of the year found from these two observations is 365 days and a quarter of a day less 1/300 of a day, which is one part and a fifth of a part out of 360 parts.
@p AB01-PDF1090-L16..AB01-PDF1089-L08
Ptolemy also reports taking the ancient summer observations made before Hipparchus, namely the observation made in the time of Apseudes, king of Athens, in which the sun passed the summer solstitial point 108 Egyptian years before the death of Alexander, in the morning of the twenty-first of Pharmouthi, one of the months of the Copts, in that year; and observing the sun in person and finding that it passed the summer solstitial point in the year 463 after the death of Alexander, on the eleventh of Mesori, one of the months of the Copts, about two hours after midnight of the night whose morning was the twelfth of that month. Between these two observations there were about 571 Egyptian years, 140 days and a half and a third of a day, in place of the 142 days and a half and a quarter of a day that would accumulate from the quarters of the said years if the quarters were full in the years. So Ptolemy found that the summer solstice came before the time of the full quarter by one day, two thirds and a quarter of a day, and the ratio of this one day, two thirds and a quarter of a day to the said 571 years is as the ratio of two full days to 600 years. This agreed with what Ptolemy had worked with, that the observation comes before the time of the full quarter day by one day in every 300 years, although these summer observations are not as reliable as the autumnal ones, for the reason we have given. It is clear that the observation made before Hipparchus was earlier than the observation of Hipparchus by about the time between the observation of Hipparchus and that of Ptolemy, for it was 286 years before Hipparchus.
@n The observation in the time of Apseudes, archon of Athens, is the summer solstice observed by Meton and Euctemon, 27 June 432 BC (Nallino). For 108 years the codex has 168.
@p AB01-PDF1089-L08..AB01-PDF1088-L01
Then we observed at the city of al-Raqqa. One of our autumnal observations, on which we rely and whose accuracy we trust as far as the instrument showed us, is the observation made 743 years after the autumnal observation of Ptolemy mentioned above. When we measured, we found that the sun passed the autumnal equinoctial point in the year 1194 of the years of Dhū l-Qarnayn, which is the year 1206 after the death of Alexander, about four hours and a half and a quarter of an hour before sunrise on the nineteenth day of Aylūl, one of the months of the Rūm, which is the eighth day of Pachon, one of the months of the Copts. Since the meridian circle of Alexandria comes before the meridian circle of al-Raqqa by about two thirds of an equal hour, there are between the two observations, I mean our observation and the autumnal observation of Ptolemy, 743 Egyptian years, 178 days and a half and a quarter, less about two fifths of an hour, in place of the 185 days and a half and a quarter of a day that should accumulate from the quarters in these years if the quarters were full between the two observations. When we divide these 7 days and two fifths of an hour, by which the time of the observation came before the time of the quarter day in excess of 365 days, by the 743 years between the two observations, the share of one year comes to 3 parts 24 minutes of the 360 parts that make up the revolution of a day and its night. When we subtract this from the time of the quarter day, which is 90 parts, there remains as the excess over the full 365 days 86 parts 36 minutes; so the true length of the year is 365 days, 14 minutes and 26 seconds [of a day], approximately.
@n The years of Dhū l-Qarnayn are those of the Seleucid era, which al-Battānī begins on 1 September; the observation fell before sunrise on 19 September 882 (Nallino). Two thirds of an hour is 10° of longitude; al-Battānī's own table of cities gives al-Raqqa 73° 15′ and Alexandria 60° 30′, a difference of 12° 45′ (Nallino).
@n The minutes and seconds of the year are sixtieths of a day: 365;14,26 days is 365 days 5 hours 46 minutes 24 seconds. Hipparchus' and Ptolemy's year is 365;14,48 days, 2;12 parts of a day of 360 parts longer.
@p AB01-PDF1088-L01..AB01-PDF1088-L11
When we divide the 360 parts of the circle of the sphere by the length of the year that has been found, the mean motion of the sun in a day and its night comes to 0;59,8,20,46,56,14; in the 30 days that make the Egyptian month, 29;34,10,23,28,6,47; and in the 365 days that make the Egyptian year, 359 parts 45 minutes 46 seconds 25 thirds 32 fourths 2 fifths 31 sixths, approximately. We have compounded these motions and entered them in the tables for the collected years and the single years, the months, the days and the hours, in the era of the Arabs and the era of the Rūm, so that the position of the sun by its mean motion, which is called the mean of the sun, can easily be found at any time we want, in whichever of the two eras we like. It is clear that the length of the year that we obtained by observation is less than the length that Ptolemy gives by 2 parts and a fifth of a part, and so the motion of the sun that we found exceeds the motion that Ptolemy gives by 0;0,0,3,33,43,43 in a day, and in the Egyptian year by 0;0,21,40,10,53,56, approximately, if God wills.
@n Sexagesimal numbers with many places are written here in the usual notation: 0;59,8,20,46,56,14 is 0 degrees, 59 minutes, 8 seconds, 20 thirds, 46 fourths, 56 fifths and 14 sixths. Ptolemy's daily motion is 0;59,8,17,13,12,31 and the yearly motion 359;45,24,45,21,8,35; the two differences given here are exact. In the yearly difference the text has 50 fifths; the difference is 53, as Nallino's translation has it.
@n The collected years are the periods of years used as arguments of the tables, the single years the years within a period. The era of the Arabs is that of the Hijra; the era of the Rūm is the Seleucid era of the Syrians.
