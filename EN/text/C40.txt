Chapter 40. On knowing the distance of the moon from the earth from its parallax on the circle of altitude, when that is known
@p AB01-PDF1025-L16..AB01-PDF1025-L21
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the distance of the moon from the earth from its parallax on the circle of altitude, whether this is found by observation or from the tables: add to the corrected parallax of the moon in longitude and latitude, when you have taken it from the tables of Theon, one part in eighteen of it. Multiply what each of the two parallaxes comes to by itself, add them together, and take the root of the sum: that is the parallax of the moon with the sun on the circle of altitude. If you have taken it from the tables of the parallax on the circle of altitude, do not subtract the parallax of the sun from it, so that it is its parallax with the sun on the circle of altitude.
@n Nallino reads one part in eighteen with Plato of Tivoli; the codex has one in thirty-eight. Theon's tables give the parallax of the moon less that of the sun, and Theon adds a twentieth for it; 3 minutes, the parallax of the sun, are an eighteenth of 54 minutes, about the parallax of the moon at the first limit (53′ 34″), so that the eighteenth is right (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1025-L21..AB01-PDF1024-L15
If you wish to find it by observation, you find it as I describe. Observe the altitude of the moon when it is 90 parts from the ascendant, with a large quadrant or with the two long alidades whose making is described in Ptolemy's book, so that the taking of the altitude is more correct and precise. When you know its altitude at that place, keep it. Then find the true position of the moon on the ecliptic in longitude and latitude, and so know its distance from the equator in the way that we have explained at the beginning of the book. If its distance from the equator is to the north, subtract it from the latitude of the locality, taken by observation, and if it is to the south, add it to it; subtract what the latitude of the locality comes to after the addition or the subtraction from 90: the remainder is what its altitude in the middle of the sky must be. Then from its altitude in the middle of the sky find what its altitude must be when it is 90 parts from the ascendant, in the way that we have shown for the altitude of the degree of the moon, and compare this with the altitude of the moon that you found by observation when it was 90 parts from the ascendant. Whatever the altitude taken by observation falls short of the altitude known by computation is the parallax of the moon with the sun on the circle of altitude. It is greater the farther the moon is from the zenith, that is, when it is in the southern signs, and above all at the beginning of Capricorn: for, besides what we have described, if its latitude is to the south, its distance from the equator is the whole declination together with the latitude of the moon; and likewise if its latitude is to the north, its distance from the equator is the amount of the whole declination less the latitude of the moon, since the declination and the latitude then, and only then, come out on one arc. At the beginning of Cancer, which is like the beginning of Capricorn in this respect, the parallax is small, because the moon is near the zenith.
@n The two long alidades are Ptolemy's parallactic instrument, the long alidade of chapter 4 (*Almagest* V 12), also described in chapter 57 (Nallino). At 90 parts from the ascendant the circle of latitude through the moon is the circle of altitude, so that the whole parallax falls in latitude; Tycho Brahe sometimes observed the moon there for this reason, while Ptolemy (*Almagest* V 13) observed it on the meridian near the beginning of Capricorn (Nallino). The altitude at 90 parts from the ascendant is found as in chapter 39.

When you know the parallax of the moon with the sun on the circle of altitude, take its visible distance from the point of the zenith, which is what remains to complete the altitude of the moon to 90, and find its sine and the sine of the visible altitude of the moon as well. Then find the sine of the parallax on the circle of altitude: if it is more than a degree, make it all minutes and keep it; if it is less than a degree, it is minutes. Then multiply the sine of the distance by the radius and divide the product by the sine of the parallax of the moon that I have set out for you: the result is parts. Add to them one minute for each degree of the sine of the altitude: what the parts come to after that is the distance of the moon from the earth in the measure in which the radius of the earth is one part.
@n Two rules run together here in the codex and in Plato of Tivoli. Nallino restores the missing words after «the result»: «[is the distance of the moon from the earth. If you wish, take the true distance of the moon from the zenith, which is what the true altitude of the moon falls short of 90, find its sine and the sine of the true altitude of the moon, multiply the sine of the distance by the radius and divide the product by the sine of the parallax of the moon that I have set out for you; what results] is parts». With the visible distance from the zenith ζ and the parallax p, the distance of the moon is sin ζ / sin p radii of the earth; with the true distance from the zenith z, sin z / sin p is the distance from the observer, and the minute for each degree of the sine of the altitude adds the radius of the earth times cos z (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
