Chapter 13. On finding the ascensions of the signs in every locality in two ways, by computation and from the table, and on the procedures with them that follow from this, if God wills
@p AB01-PDF1113-L04..AB01-PDF1113-L11
He said: The ascensions of the signs at the place of the equator we have described above: there the signs cross the middle of the sky and the horizon with the same amount of times of the equator, and with this amount they also cross the middle of the sky in every locality. At the other places, those that decline from it towards the north, in all the localities, their ascensions differ from horizon to horizon. For when a locality has a latitude, that is, when it declines from the equator, the ascensions of the signs vary there, being greater or smaller than their ascensions in the middle of the sky, which are their ascensions in the right sphere. Every sign that rises in a locality with more than it rises with in the right sphere has a counterpart that rises in that locality with less than it rises with in the right sphere, by the amount of that excess; and in every locality each sign sets with as much as its counterpart rises with there.
@n The counterpart (*naẓīr*) of a sign or a degree is the sign or degree opposite to it on the ecliptic.
@p AB01-PDF1113-L11..AB01-PDF1112-L01
If you wish to know the amount of the equator that rises together with given parts of the ecliptic in any locality you want, take [the arc] from the beginning of Aries to the degree of the ecliptic you want, in ascensions of the right sphere. Find its sine, multiply it by the sine of half the excess of the longest day in that locality, divide the product by the radius, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the share of the difference of the day, in the quarter of the circle, that belongs to [the arc] from the first degree of Aries to that degree. Keep it. If you wish to find it another way, multiply the sine of the latitude of the locality by the sine of the declination of the degree, divide the product by the sine of what the latitude of the locality falls short of 90, multiply the quotient by the radius, divide it by the sine of what the declination of the degree falls short of 90, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the difference of the day in the quarter of the circle of the sphere. When you have found this share in either of the two ways, look: if the declination of the degree is northern, subtract the share you obtained from the times of the ascensions in the right sphere between the beginning of Aries and that degree; if the declination of the degree is southern, add the share to those ascensions. The ascensions you obtain after the addition or the subtraction are the ascensions from the beginning of Aries to that degree in that locality.
@n In modern terms the share is the ascensional difference Δ: sin Δ = sin α · sin e = tan φ · tan δ, where α is the ascension in the right sphere, e half the excess of the longest day, φ the latitude and δ the declination. The ascension at the locality is α − Δ for a northern declination and α + Δ for a southern one.
@p AB01-PDF1112-L01..AB01-PDF1112-L04
Know that the ascension of Pisces is equal to that of Aries, the ascension of Virgo to that of Libra, the ascension of Aquarius to that of Taurus, the ascension of Capricorn to that of Gemini, the ascension of Sagittarius to that of Cancer, and the ascension of Leo to that of Scorpio. So for finding the ascensions it is enough to know the shares between the beginning of Aries and the beginning of Cancer, that is, from 1 degree to 90 degrees.
@p AB01-PDF1112-L04..AB01-PDF1112-L13
If you wish to tabulate the ascensions degree by degree, or at larger intervals, find the share of the difference of the day for one degree, for two degrees, three and four, up to the full 90 that complete the difference of the whole quarter of the circle. When you have done this, take the ascension in the right sphere of the first degree of Aries and put it in two places. Then subtract the share of the degree from one of the two places and add it to the other. The one subtracted from is the ascension of the first degree of Aries, and the one added to is the ascension of the first degree of Libra. Add the latter to 180: the sum is the ascension from the beginning of Aries to the first degree of Libra. Subtract it from 180: the remainder is the ascension from the beginning of Aries to 29 degrees of Virgo. Also subtract the ascension of the degree of Aries from 360: the remainder is the ascension from the beginning of Aries to 29 degrees of Pisces. Do the same with the share of two degrees, three and four, up to the full 90, until you have divided [the ascensions] over the whole sphere at whatever interval of the parts you want, if God wills.
@n «Subtract it from 180»: the codex has «from 360»; Nallino corrects it.
@p AB01-PDF1112-L13..AB01-PDF1112-L16
We have entered the ascensions of the signs for the city of al-Raqqa in full, degree by degree, and for the other climates at intervals of 10 degrees, because the ascensions differ little within an interval of this size. In the ascensions entered in the tables we have made the excess of the day increase by a quarter of an equal hour [from one table to the next], so that they are more accurate for the work with the ascensions that is needed, and more exact than ascensions computed at intervals of half an hour.
@p AB01-PDF1112-L17..AB01-PDF1111-L06
If you wish to find the ascension of any degree you like from the table, look for the degrees whose ascension you wish to know, of whatever sign you like, in the common column of numbers in the table of the ascensions of the signs for the given climate, or in the ascensions of the right sphere, whichever you want, and take the times of ascension that stand opposite them in the column of the sign to which that number belongs. If you are working with the ascensions of the climates, this is the ascension from the beginning of Aries to that degree; if you are working with the ascensions of the right sphere, it is the ascension from the beginning of Capricorn to that degree. If there are minutes with the degrees, find what part of 60 they are, when the numbers increase by one degree, and take the same part of the difference between those ascensions and the ascensions that follow them, one degree further. Add the result to the ascensions you obtained opposite the whole degrees; the sum is the ascension of the degree and minute that you wanted. If the numbers increase by 10 degrees, see what part of the 10 degrees are the degrees and minutes that you have left over beyond what you find in the table, and take the same part of the differences of the ascensions in the table, I mean of the ascensions you found and those 10 degrees further on in the numbers. Add the result to the ascensions under the tens; the sum is the ascension of that degree.
@p AB01-PDF1111-L06..AB01-PDF1111-L16
If you wish to find the degrees of the signs from the ascensions, which is called taking the arc of the ascensions and converting them into degrees of equality, that is, degrees of the signs: look for the number of times of ascension that you have in the table of the ascensions of the right sphere or of the climate, whichever you want. Where you find it, or the nearest value smaller than it, take the degree of the signs entered opposite it in the common column of numbers: that is the degree you want of the sign in which you found the number of times. Then subtract [the times found in the table from] the times you have, and look at the remainder: if the numbers increase by one degree, multiply it by 60 minutes; if they increase by 10 degrees, multiply it by 600 minutes. Divide the product by the difference of the ascensions between that entry and the entry that follows it, and add the degrees and minutes that result from the division to the degrees you obtained first. The result is the amount of that sign that rises, or that culminates, according to which [table] you worked with. Or, if you wish, see what part of the difference of the ascensions the remainder is, and take the same part of the interval of the numbers; add the result to the degrees you had obtained.
@n Degrees of equality (*daraj al-sawāʾ*) are degrees of the ecliptic, as against the times of the equator that rise or culminate with them; the Alfonsine Tables call them «equal degrees» (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1111-L16..AB01-PDF1110-L02
If you wish to find the arc of the day and of the night from the table, that is, the amount of the equator that rises from sunrise to sunset, or from sunset to sunrise the next day: find the degree in which the sun is on the day you want, and take the times of ascension opposite it in the given climate whose latitude is equal to that of the city, or nearer to it than the other climates. Subtract them from the ascensions opposite the degree opposite to the degree of the sun in that climate; the remainder is the amount of the arc of the day. If the ascensions of the degree of the sun are greater than those of the degree opposite to it, which is the counterpart of the degree of the sun, add a revolution to the ascensions of the counterpart of the degree of the sun and subtract from the sum the ascensions of the degree of the sun: the remainder is the arc of the day. When you know the arc of the day, subtract it from a revolution: the remainder is the arc of the night.
@p AB01-PDF1110-L02..AB01-PDF1110-L11
If you want the arc of the day in another way, take the times of ascension opposite the degree of the sun in the climate, and also the times of ascension opposite the degree of the sun in the right sphere, and subtract 90 from the latter, so that they are counted from the beginning of Aries. When you have done this, take the difference between them and the ascensions that you obtained from the climates. Then look: if the times of the ascensions of the climates are the greater, subtract this difference from 90; if they are the smaller, add this difference to 90. The 90 after the addition or subtraction is the amount of half the arc of the day; double it, and you have the whole arc of the day. Know that this difference between the ascensions is the share of the degree of the sun in the difference, I mean the difference of the day. When you know it, look at the degree of the sun: if it is in the northern signs, add the difference to 90; if it is in the southern ones, subtract it from 90. The result is half the arc of the day, which is the part of the equator from the rising of the sun to its culmination at noon, and its double is the whole arc of the day; the two procedures come to the same.
@p AB01-PDF1110-L11..AB01-PDF1110-L14
If you wish to know the number of the equal hours of the day and of the night, divide the arc of the day or the arc of the night by 15; the result is the hours of whichever of the two you computed. When you know the hours of one of them, subtract them from 24; the remainder is the hours of the other.
@p AB01-PDF1110-L14..AB01-PDF1110-L18
If you wish to find the times of the seasonal hours of the day and of the night, of which there are always 12 in the day and as many in the night, and which are called the crooked hours, divide the arc of whichever you like, the day or the night, by 12; the result is the times of its hours. Subtract the times of the hours of whichever you computed from 30; there remain the times of the hours of the other. For these 30 are the times of two equal hours, and what the times of an hour of the night or of the day lack is added to the other.
@n The times of a seasonal hour are its length in degrees of the equator; an equal hour is 15 times.
@p AB01-PDF1110-L18..AB01-PDF1109-L05
If you wish to find the times of the hours in another way, take a sixth of the difference of the day mentioned earlier in this chapter. If the sun, or the degree you want, is in the northern half of the sphere, add this sixth to 15; if it is in the southern half, subtract it from 15. The result after the addition or subtraction is the times of the hours of the day. If you wish to find the times of the hours of the day from the table, enter the degree of the sun, or any other degree of the signs, in the common column of numbers of the table of the ascensions of the climate that is given for that city, and take the times of the hours entered opposite it in the column of the sign to which that number belongs; the result is the times of the hours of the day. If you wish to know the times of the hours of the night, enter in those ascensions the counterpart of the degree of the sun, which is opposite to it, or the counterpart of the degree you want, and take the times of the hours opposite it in the same way; the result is the times of the hours of the night. Either can also be found from the other, by subtracting one of them from 30 degrees: there remain the times of the other.
@n The 12 seasonal hours of the day take 180° + 2Δ of the equator, where Δ is the difference of the day; one of them is therefore 15 + Δ/6 times.
@p AB01-PDF1109-L05..AB01-PDF1109-L09
If you wish to find the arc of the day from the times of the hours, or the arc of the night from them, whichever you like, multiply the times of whichever you want by 6; the result is the arc of half the day or of half the night, whichever you computed. Then double it; the result is its whole arc. If you multiply the parts of the times by 12, the result is the amount of the arc of the day or of the night for the degree for which you computed.
@p AB01-PDF1109-L09..AB01-PDF1109-L13
If you wish to convert equinoctial hours into seasonal ones, multiply the equal hours by 15 and divide by the times of the hours of the day or of the night, whichever you like; the result is seasonal hours of the night or of the day, according to what those equal hours were. If you wish to convert seasonal hours into equal ones, multiply the hours of the day by the times of the hours of the day, and the hours of the night by the times of the hours of the night, and divide the product by 15; the result is equal hours, with their fractions if a fraction remains, if God wills.
