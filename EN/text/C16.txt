Chapter 16. On finding how many hours of the day have passed, by measuring the sun, and on finding the ascendant
@p AB01-PDF1108-L17..AB01-PDF1107-L10
He said: If you wish to know how many hours of the day have passed, by measuring the sun, find the altitude of the sun at noon on that day, and then half the arc of the day on that day. Then measure the altitude of the sun with a quadrant or by the shadow. When you know the altitude, at whatever time of the day you like, find the versed sine of half the arc of the day, in the way set out at the beginning of the book, in the chapter on finding the versed sines from the arcs. Then take the sine of the altitude of the sun at the time of the measurement, multiply it by the versed sine of half the arc of the day, and divide the product by the sine of the altitude at noon. Subtract the quotient from the versed sine of half the arc of the day, and find the arc of the remainder as a versed sine, as I described for taking the arc of the versed sines; keep the arc you obtain. If the measurement was made before noon, subtract this arc from half the arc of the day; if it was made after noon, add it to half the arc of the day. Half the arc of the day, after the addition or the subtraction, is the amount of the sphere that has turned from sunrise to the time of the measurement. Divide it by the times of the hours of the day, I mean of that day, found from the degree of the sun; the result is the number of seasonal hours of the day that have passed. If you divide it by 15, they are equal hours.
@n In modern terms vers t = vers H · (1 − sin h / sin h₀), where t is the distance of the sun from the meridian in times (the hour angle), H half the arc of the day, h the altitude and h₀ the altitude at noon. The rule is exact.

If you wish to find the ascendant from the amount of the sphere that has turned, add the amount of the sphere that has turned to the times of ascension opposite the degree of the sun in the clime; from the sum you find the ascendant, in the way that we have explained earlier in the book. If you wish, take the arc that you obtained as a versed sine: it is the distance of the sun from the line of the middle of the sky. Divide it by the times of the hours of the day, and subtract the result from 6 hours if the measurement was made before noon, or add it to 6 hours if the measurement was made after noon; the result is the number of seasonal hours of the day that have passed, which you can convert into equinoctial hours if you wish. If you wish to find the ascendant from this arc, subtract it from the ascension of the degree of the sun in the right sphere if the measurement was made before noon, and add it to the ascension if the measurement was made after noon. With the ascension you obtain you find the ascendant and the middle of the sky, by taking the arc of these ascensions in the clime and in the right sphere and finding the degrees of the signs that correspond to them, in the way described in the chapter on finding the degrees of the signs from the times of ascension.
@n The ascension in the right sphere is counted from the beginning of Capricorn (chapter 5): taken in the table of the clime, it gives the ascendant; taken in the table of the right sphere, the degree in the middle of the sky.
