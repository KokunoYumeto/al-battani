Chapter 34. On knowing the hours of the computation in every locality, which are the equal hours, also called the mean hours, after noon at the city of al-Raqqa
@p AB01-PDF1044-L11..AB01-PDF1044-L16
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to know the hours of the computation in any locality you want — the hours by which the motions of the stars are found in this book, since we have set the computation of the stars in it for the time of noon, at the city of al-Raqqa, of the day for which you compute, which is the time of noon of the morrow — the seventh seasonal hour of the daytime becomes for this reason the first hour of the day that follows it; and so on to sunset, which is its sixth hour; and so on to the sixth hour of the night, which is its twelfth; and so on to sunrise of the morrow; and at noon of the morrow the day that follows also comes to an end.
@n Al-Battānī's day begins at noon, whereas the civil day of the Muslims begins at sunset (Nallino, after chapter 29).
@p AB01-PDF1044-L16..AB01-PDF1043-L10
When you know the number of the hours from the time of noon to the given hour of the daytime or of the night, multiply all that are hours of the daytime by the times of the hours of the daytime, which are taken with the degree of the sun, from its degrees, in the given clime, and multiply what are hours of the night by the times of the hours of the night, which are taken with the counterpart of the degree of the sun. If the hours are equal hours, multiply them all by 15. From what results, whichever kind it is, subtract the degrees and minutes set down under the degree of the sun in the tables of the equation of the days with their nights, which is set down in the ascensions of the right sphere, in the sign in which the sun is. Divide the remainder by 15: the result is the equal, mean hours, converted from the irregular days into the mean days, after noon. If your computation is at the city of al-Raqqa, these are the hours of the computation. If it is at another city, take the amount between al-Raqqa and that city of the longitude set down in the tables of the longitudes of the cities, whatever it is, and divide it by 15: the hours and parts of an hour that result are the hours of the distance, so keep them. Then look: if the longitude of the city is greater than the longitude of al-Raqqa, which is 73;15, subtract the hours of the distance from those equal, mean hours after noon that have resulted at that city, for that city is then to the east of al-Raqqa; if the distance of the city is less, add the hours of the distance to those hours. What results after the addition or the subtraction is the equal hours after noon at al-Raqqa, which are the hours of the computation. Enter with them into the tables of the hours, take what stands opposite them of the motion of the sun, the moon and the stars, and add it to their mean positions found for the time of noon of the day for which you compute.
@n The times of a seasonal hour are the degrees of the equator that rise in it. The equation of the days with their nights is that of chapter 29. Plato of Tivoli has 75;15 for the longitude of al-Raqqa (Nallino). Theon's tables give similar rules for the mean equal hours of Alexandria (Nallino).

If the given hours are before noon of the day for which you compute, take one day from the days that have passed of the month up to the day for which you compute; then take the hours from the time of noon of the day before to the given hour, and proceed with them as you proceeded at first.
