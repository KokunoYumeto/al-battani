Chapter 44. On knowing the eclipse of the sun, its amounts and its times in every locality, and the directions of its darkening and of its clearing, by computation and by the table
@p AB01-PDF0995-L04..AB01-PDF0995-L08
Al-Battānī said: If you wish to compute the eclipse of the sun, examine the conjunctions of the sun and the moon. If the mean motion in latitude falls in them between the eclipse limits set down for the sun at the top of the page of the months of the conjunction and the full moon, the sun can be eclipsed; if it is more than that or less, it cannot be eclipsed in any of the climes. If you know that it can be eclipsed, examine the time of the conjunction, whether it falls in the daytime or near sunrise or sunset, so as to know, if an eclipse occurs, whether it can be seen whole or in part.
@n In the codex the limits stand on the same pages as those of the moon (Nallino; chapter 43).
@p AB01-PDF0995-L08..AB01-PDF0994-L04
When you know that something of it is possible, find the true equal hours of the conjunction after noon in the locality that you want, and set up the ascendant and the middle of the sky on the ecliptic at that time. Then find the parallax of the moon in longitude only, by the computation of the angles and of the arcs of distance from the zenith on the circle of altitude, as I have explained at the beginning of the book, from the parallax on the circle of altitude less the parallax of the sun, found in that manner. Divide the result by the unequal motion of the moon in an hour: the result is the hours of the first parallax. If the distance of the degree of the conjunction from the ascendant is less than 90, the moon is in the eastern quarter of the sphere: subtract the hours of the first parallax from the hours of the conjunction, and the minutes of the parallax from the position of the moon and its anomaly at the time of the conjunction. If the distance of the degree of the conjunction from the ascendant is more than 90, the moon is in the western quarter: add the hours of the first parallax to the hours of the conjunction, and the minutes of the parallax to the position of the moon and its anomaly. With what the hours of the conjunction come to after the addition or the subtraction, find the ascendant again, and find the parallax of the moon in longitude again in the same way, with the second position of the moon and its anomaly. Divide what you obtain of this second parallax by the gain of the moon on the sun in that hour: the hour or part of an hour that results is the hours of the second parallax. Subtract them from the hours of the true conjunction if the distance of the moon from this second ascendant is less than 90, and subtract the minutes of the second parallax from the position of the moon and its anomaly; if the distance of the moon from this second ascendant is more than 90, add the hours of the second parallax to the hours of the true conjunction, and the minutes of the second parallax to the position of the moon and its anomaly. By «the position of the moon and its anomaly» I mean its position at the time of the true conjunction: so cancel what you had written down on account of the first parallax.
@n The parallax in longitude is found as in chapter 39. Between the ascendant and the point 90 parts from it the parallax increases the longitude of the moon, so that the visible conjunction comes before the true one; beyond that point it comes after.
@p AB01-PDF0994-L04..AB01-PDF0994-L15
With the hours of the second conjunction that result, I mean these last ones, find the ascendant and the middle of the sky as usual, and with it and with the position of the moon and its anomaly find the parallax in longitude a third time in the very same way. If this third parallax is exactly equal to the second, the hours that you obtained for the conjunction with the hours of the second parallax are the hours of the middle of the eclipse, for the amount of the parallax in longitude is then equal to the minutes between the sun and the moon at that time, without excess or shortfall. If the third parallax is more than the second, the parallax at that time is more than the minutes between the sun and the moon at it by the excess of the third parallax over the second; if the third parallax is less than the second, you know that the parallax at that time is less than the minutes between the sun and the moon at it by the amount by which the third parallax falls short of the second. Therefore the time must be found at which the amount between the sun and the moon is equal to the parallax of the moon in longitude at it, so that it is the middle of the visible eclipse. Finding it, and the way of knowing it, are as I describe.
@n Nallino prints the two sentences on the third parallax being more or less than the second in italics, and Schiaparelli calls them false: that the difference of the two parallaxes and the difference of the visible longitudes agree when both are nothing does not make them agree when they are something (Nallino's commentary). The sentences are correct. The hours of the second parallax are the second parallax divided by the gain of the moon, so at the new time the true moon is exactly the second parallax away from the sun; the parallax at that time is the third, and it exceeds the minutes between the sun and the moon by the excess of the third over the second, the gain being taken as uniform over those hours. Schiaparelli's objection therefore fails, and with it Schiaparelli's claim that al-Battānī divides the wrong quantity by the corrected motion below. In a numerical test with a smoothly changing parallax, the rules bring the time within about a second of the visible conjunction, where the second parallax alone leaves an error of minutes.
@p AB01-PDF0994-L15..AB01-PDF0993-L11
Look: if the parallax is more than the second parallax, subtract from the hours that you have obtained an amount that does not take you out of the whole hour. That is: if the hours that you have obtained, with which you found the third parallax, have a fraction with them, and it is more than a sixth of an hour, subtract a sixth of an hour from them; if it is less than a sixth of an hour, subtract an eighth or a tenth of an hour from them, as is possible, and as does not require you to break an hour from the hours. Use this subtraction if the distance of the moon from the ascendant is then less than 90; if the distance of the moon from the ascendant is more than 90, use addition instead of subtraction on the same condition, namely that what you add to those hours does not make up a whole hour: if the fraction is less than a half and a third, add a sixth of an hour to it, and if it is more than a half and a third, add an eighth of an hour, an amount that does not complete an hour. I have told you this only for finding it from the tables of Theon, which are set up for the climes, so that the difference of the parallax does not go outside that hour. If you work it with the arcs and the angles, you use only the addition of a sixth of an hour and the subtraction of a sixth of an hour. Then find the parallax in longitude with the hours that result after the addition or the subtraction of the sixth. Of the parallax that you obtain at whichever of the two times you use, see what exceeds the third parallax, and multiply it by six if you worked it with the angles, and likewise with the tables of Theon if you used a sixth of an hour in the addition or the subtraction; if you used an eighth of an hour, multiply it by eight, and likewise if you used a tenth of an hour, multiply it by ten, so that what results is the amount for an equal hour. When you have done that, subtract it from the gain of the moon on the sun in that hour: the remainder is the corrected motion. Divide by it the excess of the third parallax over the second: the result is a part of an hour. Add it to the hours of the parallax found with the gain of the moon: the sum is the corrected hours of the second parallax; keep them.
@n Theon's tables give the parallax for whole hours from noon in each clime; with them a fraction below a sixth is taken off, an eighth or a tenth, so that the second time falls on a whole hour of the table (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). Over so short a time the change of the parallax can be taken as uniform; the corrected motion, the gain less the change of the parallax in an hour, is the gain of the visible moon on the sun (Schiaparelli).
@p AB01-PDF0993-L11..AB01-PDF0993-L20
If the third parallax is less than the second, you work the other way round from what you did at first: add a sixth of an hour to the hours that you have obtained if the distance of the moon from the ascendant is less than 90, and subtract a sixth of an hour if the distance of the moon from the ascendant is more than 90. With the hours that result, find the parallax in longitude at the time that you obtain of the two times, in that way; then see the shortfall relative to the third parallax and multiply it by six; if you used less than the sixth, such as the eighth or the tenth, make the multiplier accordingly, so that it comes to a whole hour. Add the product to the gain of the moon on the sun at that time: the sum is the corrected motion. Divide by it what the third parallax falls short of the second: the quotient is a part of an hour; subtract it from the hours of the second parallax — and say that this does not happen except when the moon is near the horizon, the difference between the two parallaxes being small then. The remainder is the corrected hours of the second parallax.
@p AB01-PDF0993-L20..AB01-PDF0992-L15
When you know the corrected hours of the second parallax, multiply them by the unequal course of the moon in an hour and by the unequal course of the sun in an hour, and find the amount of each product. If the distance of the moon from the ascendant is less than 90, subtract the corrected hours of the second parallax from the hours of the true conjunction; subtract what results of the course of the moon from the position of the moon at the time of the true conjunction, from the anomaly of the moon and from the motion in latitude as well, and subtract also from the motion in latitude the motion of the northern node in the corrected hours of the second parallax; and subtract what results for the sun from the position of the sun — the position of the sun and of the moon is, as is known, the position of the conjunction. If the distance of the moon from the ascendant is more than 90, use addition instead of subtraction in everything that I have described to you. What the hours of the true conjunction come to after the addition or the subtraction are the hours of the middle of the visible eclipse, with the positions of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the motion in latitude at it. Likewise, if the third parallax is exactly equal to the second, you multiply the hours of the second parallax, which have then become the corrected ones, by the course of the moon, of the sun and of the northern node in an hour, and add the products to the positions of the sun and the moon, the anomaly of the moon and the motion in latitude if you added the hours to the hours of the conjunction, and subtract them from these positions if you subtracted those hours from the hours of the conjunction; you use the motion of the node in the motion in latitude only and in nothing else, so that the position of each of them comes out right in truth. Necessarily, the amount of the parallax in longitude that you obtain at that time is then equal to what lies between the sun and the moon. It is known also that if the distance of the moon from the ascendant at the time of the true conjunction is 90, the hours of the true conjunction are the hours of the middle of the eclipse without difference, and likewise the position of the conjunction is the visible position of the moon and the position of the sun together.
@n The motion of the node goes with the moon's in the motion in latitude, because that is counted from the northern node, which moves backwards.
@p AB01-PDF0992-L15..AB01-PDF0991-L02
Then set up the ascendant for the middle of the eclipse, and with it and with the position of the moon at it find the parallax of the moon in latitude in that way, which is the procedure given before; then the true latitude of the moon, from the corrected motion in latitude for the middle of the eclipse, in that way, either by the table or by computation. Find the side of this true latitude and the side of the parallax in latitude. If the true latitude of the moon and its parallax in latitude are on one side, add them together; if they differ, subtract the smaller from the greater and find the side of the remainder. What results after the addition or the subtraction is the visible latitude of the moon on the side on which it falls at the middle of the eclipse. If it is more than 0;34,30, the sun is not eclipsed; if it is less, it is eclipsed, though it may not be eclipsed until the visible latitude of the moon comes down to 0;30,25; if it is less than that, the eclipse is certain. The doubt arises only between 0;34,30 and 0;30,25, because of what the radii of the sun and the moon can come to together at their distances from the centre of the earth.
@n The translation reads «in latitude» with Nallino's translation, as the sense requires; the text has «in longitude» (p. 160, line 19). 0;34,30 and 0;30,25 are the greatest and the least sum of the radii of the sun and the moon, half of 0;33,40 + 0;35,20 and of 0;31,20 + 0;29,30 (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary); the codex has 0;36,39 and 0;34,36.
@p AB01-PDF0991-L02..AB01-PDF0991-L15
When you know that the sun is eclipsed, or that it can be eclipsed, take the anomaly of the moon for the middle of the eclipse, enter with it in the two columns of numbers of the tables of the computation, and find the minutes of the third column that are under it. Find what part of 60 the result is, and take that part of the 2 minutes and a quarter of a minute by which the diameter of the sun, relative to the moon, varies between the farthest and the nearest distance of the sun; add the result to 0;31,20, the diameter of the sun at its farthest distance: the sum is the corrected diameter of the sun. [If you wish to know that by computation, you should correct the arcs for the degree of the moon from the zenith, and the angles too, with the latitude of the moon in that way, for that is more correct for the computation.] When you know the diameter of the sun in whichever of the two ways you wish, find the diameter of the moon in the way given for the eclipse of the moon. Then add together the corrected diameter of the sun and the corrected diameter of the moon and take half the sum: the result is half the two diameters; keep it. If the visible latitude of the moon is equal to half the two diameters or more than it, the sun is not eclipsed; if it is less than half the two diameters, it is eclipsed. When you know that it is certainly eclipsed, subtract the visible latitude of the moon from half the two diameters: the remainder is the amount of the diameter of the sun that is eclipsed. Multiply it by 12 and divide the product by the corrected diameter of the sun: the quotient is the digits of the eclipse by which the sun is eclipsed, in the measure in which its whole diameter is 12 parts; they are called the digits.
@n The translation follows Nallino's emendanda (Part II, p. XXXI), with Plato of Tivoli, in reading «القمر», «the moon», for «الشمس», «the sun» (p. 161, line 3). The diameter of the sun varies from 0;31,20 to 0;33,40 (chapter 30), by 2⅓ minutes; «a quarter» is perhaps an error of the archetype for «a third» (Nallino). The bracketed passage, which Nallino prints in brackets, has nothing to do with the diameter of the sun; Schiaparelli takes it for an unskilled substitute for a lost rule, perhaps one finding the diameter of the sun from its hourly motion, since al-Battānī makes the two proportional in chapter 30 (Nallino's commentary).

Divide them by the gain of the moon in an hour: the quotient is the hours of the fall. Subtract them from the hours of the middle of the eclipse: the remainder is the hours of the plain beginning of the eclipse; and add them also to the plain hours of the middle of the eclipse: the sum is the hours of the plain end of the clearing. Then find the motion of the sun and the moon in the hours of the fall by multiplying the hours of the fall by the course of the sun and of the moon in an hour, as usual, and keep what results for each of them. Then subtract what results for the sun from the true position of the sun for the middle of the eclipse, and what results for the moon from the true position of the moon for the middle of the eclipse, from the anomaly of the moon and from the motion in latitude; and add it also to these positions mentioned for the middle of the eclipse, using in the motion in latitude alone, besides, the addition and the subtraction of the course of the node in the hours of the fall. If the minutes of the fall that result for the time of the plain beginning are equal to the minutes between the sun and the moon at it, the time of the plain beginning is the time of the actual visible beginning; and if the minutes of the fall for the plain clearing are also equal to the minutes between the sun and the moon at it, it goes the same way, without difference. If that differs at one of the two times or at both, the time of the actual visible beginning is not the same as the time of the plain beginning, nor is the time of the clearing either. That happens only because of the parallax of the moon in longitude and its changes at those times. The correction of the computation is as I describe. Look at the plain beginning and the plain clearing that you have found before, the positions of the moon at them corrected by its parallax in longitude, which are its visible positions, and the parallax of the moon in longitude that you obtained at them, and set these down as they are.

So subtract a sixth of an hour from the hours of the plain beginning, then set up the ascendant and find the parallax of the moon in longitude in that way. If this parallax is more than the parallax of the plain beginning, take the excess and multiply it by six, so that it comes to a whole hour, and subtract that from the gain of the moon; if it is less, multiply the shortfall by six and add it to the gain of the moon. What the gain of the moon comes to is the corrected motion. Divide by this corrected motion the difference between the minutes between the sun and the moon at the time of the plain beginning and its minutes of the fall: the result is a part of an hour; subtract it from the hours of the plain beginning. If the minutes between the sun and the moon are more than the minutes of the fall, the moon has not reached the place at which it can cover anything of the sun: add a sixth of an hour to the hours of the plain beginning and find the parallax of the moon in longitude in that manner. If you wish to correct the digits of the eclipse, so as to know the amount of the circle of the sun that is eclipsed to the sight when its whole area is 12, the way of doing it is to make the diameter of the sun always 12 digits, whether its diameter is small or large, and to multiply it by 3 parts 8½ minutes: the product is the circumference of the circle of the sun, which is 37 digits and 42 minutes of a digit. Take half these digits, which is 18;51, and multiply it by the digits of the radius of the sun, which are six: the area of the circle of the sun comes to 113;6. Then take half the corrected diameter of the moon, multiply it by six and divide it by half the corrected diameter of the sun: the quotient is the digits of the radius of the moon; keep them. Then double them: the result is the digits of the whole diameter of the moon. Multiply them by 3 parts 8½ minutes: the product is the circumference of the circle of the moon; keep it. Then add to the digits of the radius of the moon the six digits that are the radius of the sun, and subtract from the sum the digits of the eclipse: the remainder is the amount between the two centres. Double it: the result is twice the distance between the two centres. Then subtract the digits of the eclipse from 12, multiply the remainder by the digits of the eclipse, and divide the product by twice the distance between the two centres: the quotient is the sagitta of the circle of the moon. Subtract it from the digits of the eclipse: the remainder is the sagitta of the circle of the sun. Subtract that from 12, multiply the remainder by the sagitta of the circle of the sun, and take the root of the product: the result is half the common chord; keep it.

@n The radius of the sun, 6 digits, times ten is the radius 60 of the table of the halved chords (Nallino); the codex has six. The text has «the diameter of the moon» for the area of its arc; Nallino translates «radius», as the rule requires. As in chapter 43, the rule is exact while the common chord lies between the two centres. In a deeper eclipse the chord passes the centre of the sun, which happens when the excess of the digits over six is greater than the sagitta of the circle of the moon, or, when the moon is the smaller, its own centre; one of the two segments is then more than half its circle, the table of the halved chords gives only the smaller arc, and the rule gives too little.

If you wish to know the sides of the darkening of the eclipse and of the clearing on the circle of the horizon, look: if the visible centre of the moon at any one of the times of the eclipse is on the belt of the signs, the beginning of the eclipse is on the side of the azimuth of the setting degree of the ecliptic at that time, and the end of the clearing, when it is possible, is on the side of the azimuth of the ascendant at that time; the middle of the eclipse has no side when the darkness surrounds the whole circle of the sun. If the visible centre of the moon is off the belt of the signs, take the visible latitude of the moon at each of the two times, I mean the time of the visible beginning and the time of the visible clearing, which I told you to compute, to know and to keep, multiply it by sixty and divide by half the two diameters together: what results is degrees. Find their arc from the table of the chords: the arc is the amount of the deviation of the darkening or of the clearing at each of the two times. At the beginning of the eclipse you lay off the deviation of the darkening from the limit of the azimuth of the setting degree at that time on the circle of the horizon, towards the side of the visible latitude of the moon at it; at the end of the clearing you lay off the arc that you obtain from the limit of the azimuth of the ascendant then, towards the side of the visible latitude of the moon at the time of the clearing. At the middle of the eclipse the direction of the darkening is at right angles to the ecliptic, and its azimuth on the circle of the horizon is bounded by the arc that passes through the two poles of the ecliptic, through the centre of the moon and the horizon, according to the distance of the sun and the moon from the equator.

@p AB01-PDF0986-L12..AB01-PDF0985-L06
If you wish to know the eclipse of the sun approximately by the tables, when you know that the sun is eclipsed or that it can be eclipsed, take the distance of the true equal hours of the conjunction from the time of noon in the locality that you want. You find it by converting the hours of the conjunction into hours of that locality; then look: if the conjunction is before noon, subtract the hours from 24 hours, and if it is after noon, before sunset, take the hours themselves. What results in either way is the distance of the hours of the conjunction from the meridian line. With it find the parallax of the moon in longitude from the tables set down for the parallax of the moon in the climes, so as to know its parallax corrected by the fourth column of the tables of the computation for the degree in which the moon is, in that manner.
