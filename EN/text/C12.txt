Chapter 12. On finding the meridian line, which is the direction of the south
@p AB01-PDF1118-L13..AB01-PDF1117-L03
Al-Battānī said: Suppose you wish to find the direction of the south, which is the meridian line, in any locality you want and at any time of the year you like, and the place of the sun is not known to you. Go to a place with an open horizon and a smooth, level surface that does not slope, and draw on it a circle of any size you like. Fix at the centre of the circle a thin rod with a pointed tip, straight and without any bend; its best length is a quarter of the diameter of the circle. Measure [the distance] from four sides of the circle to the tip of the rod, so that it stands truly upright on the centre. Then watch the shadow at the beginning of the day, when it is long. It keeps getting shorter; when it reaches the circumference of the circle and is about to enter it, mark its place on the circumference with a point, as a sign of the tip of the shadow. Then wait until noon has passed and the shadow begins to grow; when it reaches the circumference of the circle and is about to leave it, mark the place of its tip on the circumference with a second point, as [a sign of the tip of the shadow].
@n The words in brackets at the end are Nallino's conjecture.
@p AB01-PDF1117-L03..AB01-PDF1117-L11
Then divide the arc between the two points into halves, mark the place of the half of the arc with a point, and from this point draw a straight line through the centre of the circle to the other side of the circumference, extending it as far as you like. To do this, lay the edge of the ruler on the point at the half of the arc and on the centre, and draw a line through both the point and the centre to the circumference on the other side. This line is always the meridian line: whenever the shadow of the rod at the centre falls on this line, it is noon, whether the shadow is long or short. This line is the direction between south and north. Then quarter the circle with another line through the centre of the circle at right angles; the circle is divided by these two lines into four equal quarters, and this line is the direction between east and west. Then write at the ends of the lines the directions of the horizon, that is, east, west, north and south.
@n «Half», in «the half of the arc», is lacking in the codex and supplied by Nallino.
@p AB01-PDF1117-L11..AB01-PDF1117-L14
The nearer the sun is to one of the two solstitial points, the more accurate the observation, because the sun moves slowly in declination between the two observations, I mean the two observations of the shadow. It is known that the moment of noon cannot be fixed exactly, because the sun passes quickly in declination through the meridian circle; but the moment of noon, or the moment nearest to it, is found. This is a diagram of the circle described, if God, the Exalted, wills.
@n «In declination», in the second sentence, is Nallino's conjecture (in the emendanda printed in Part II).

@p AB01-PDF1117-L15..AB01-PDF1116-L03
Al-Battānī said: Draw a circle about the centre H, and fix the indicating rod at the place of point H. Mark point A at the place of the tip of the shadow before noon, and the sign B at the place of its tip after noon. Divide the arc AB into halves at the sign G, and draw the line from G to the sign D. The line GHD is the meridian line; point D is the direction of the south, and point G the direction of the north. Then divide the arc GAD into halves at point Ṭ, and draw through the points Ṭ and H a line that goes out to point Z. Point Z is the direction of the east, and point Ṭ the direction of the west. Whenever the shadow of the indicating rod falls on the line HG, it is always noon.
@n The indicating rod (*al-ʿamūd al-mūrī*, «the rod that shows») is the rod set up at the centre.
@p AB01-PDF1116-L03..AB01-PDF1116-L11
If the place of the sun is known to you, find the azimuth of any altitude you like at some time of the day, and watch for the given altitude. When the altitude becomes equal to it, mark on the circumference of the circle, at the shadow of the indicator, a sign that stands in the middle of the breadth of the shadow of the indicating rod. Then divide the quarter of the circle in which this point falls into 90 parts, and count from that point, towards the side opposite to the direction of the azimuth of that altitude, as many parts as the azimuth of the altitude. The division at which you arrive is the east point or the west point, according to the time of the altitude with which you worked, before noon or after it. From this sign draw a line through the centre of the circle, and quarter the circle with another line through the centre at right angles. Then you know the meridian line by this line, and the line of east and west by the line before it.
@p AB01-PDF1116-L11..AB01-PDF1116-L13
Likewise, if the horizon is in view from this circle at sunrise or sunset, the east point or the west point is known from the azimuth of the rising or setting of the degree of the sun on the horizon circle, which we have drawn as ABGD.
@p AB01-PDF1116-L13..AB01-PDF1115-L02
If you wish to find the line between east and west in another way, and through it the line between north and south, this is done by finding the altitude whose azimuth has no inclination from the equinoctial rising or setting point. This is possible only when the sun is in the northern signs, from the beginning of Aries to the end of Virgo. This altitude is found as follows. Find the place of the sun in the ecliptic on the day you want, and its altitude at noon on the day for which you work. Then find the sine of this altitude and the sine of what it falls short of 90. Then find the azimuth of the rising or setting of the sun, at its place in the ecliptic at the noon for which you work; by the condition we have laid down it is always northern. Then take the sine of this azimuth and multiply it by the sine of the altitude, divide the product by the sine of the azimuth and the sine of the complement of the altitude added together, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is the altitude whose azimuth has no inclination. When you know this altitude, watch the sun; when its altitude becomes equal to this altitude, mark a point on the circumference of the circle in the middle of the shadow of the indicator. It is the east point or the west point, according to the time at which you measure, that is, the equinoctial rising or setting point. Quarter the circle at this point with two lines that intersect at the centre at right angles, and with them mark the directions of the horizon.
@n The altitude whose azimuth has no inclination is the altitude of the sun when it stands due east or due west; al-Battānī uses it again for the sundial in chapter 56 (Nallino). In modern terms the rule is sin h = sin A · sin H / (sin A + cos H), where A is the amplitude of the rising and H the altitude at noon; it is exact, and equal to sin δ / sin φ.
@p AB01-PDF1115-L02..AB01-PDF1115-L10
We give an example of this, and place it in the fourth clime, where the altitude of the pole is 36° 22′. We take the place of the sun at the beginning of Cancer. The altitude of the sun at noon is then 77° 13′, and its altitude at midnight, from the northern horizon, 30° 3′; it is known that this is equal to the altitude above the earth, on the line of the middle of the sky, of the degree opposite to it, which is 30° 3′. We also find this in another way, by doubling the altitude of the beginning of Aries in the said clime and subtracting from the result the altitude of the beginning of Cancer in the middle of the sky. Clearly the altitude of the beginning of Aries in the middle of the sky in this clime is 53° 38′, and its double 107° 16′; when 77° 13′ is subtracted from this, there remains its altitude in the middle of the sky below the earth, 30° 3′. We shall show that the azimuth of the beginning of Cancer at its rising in this clime lies 30° 0′ to the north of the rising of Aries.
@n For 30° 0′ the codex has 30° 3′; Nallino reads 30° 0′ with Plato of Tivoli, the value used at the end of the chapter, where the sine of this azimuth is taken as 30 parts.
@p AB01-PDF1115-L10..AB01-PDF1115-L22
Since this is as we have described, we draw a circle of the middle of the sky, ABL, about its centre H, with the diameter LB. Let the diameter LB be half the horizon, and let point A be the place of the zenith. We join point A to point H, so that the arc BA is the quarter of the circle between the zenith and the horizon. Point H is the place of the rising of the beginning of Aries, and point G the place of the rising of the beginning of Cancer: for HB is the southern half of the horizon, the line HL the northern half of the horizon, and the line HA the line of the quarter circle that passes through the zenith and the rising point of the beginning of Aries. We mark at the point of the beginning of Cancer on the circle of the middle of the sky the point Z. The arc BZ is the altitude of the sun at noon, and the arc ZA its distance from the zenith, which is the complement of the altitude to a quarter of the circle. We mark at the altitude of the sun at midnight the point Ṭ, so that the arc ṬL is the arc of the altitude at midnight below the earth, and we draw the line ṬZ, which passes through the point G at which the beginning of Cancer rises. The point common to the line ṬZ and the line HA is the place at which the sun, when it rises to it, is on the azimuth of H, from which the beginning of Aries rises; it then has no inclination from the azimuth of the equinoctial risings, since the line from the zenith passes through the place of the sun and through point H of the horizon. We mark the place of the sun on the line HA with the sign M.
@n The figure is the meridian circle, with the horizon, the vertical through the east point and the day circle of the sun seen edge on as the straight lines LB, HA and ṬZ. Here and below Nallino corrects several letters of the figure where the codex has others; the translation follows Nallino's text.
@p AB01-PDF1115-L22..AB01-PDF1114-L10
It is clear in this figure that the line HG is the line of the azimuth of the rising of the beginning of Cancer, and it is the sine of the azimuth. Further, we draw from point Z a perpendicular to the line HB, parallel to the line HA: this is the perpendicular ZK, which is the sine of the altitude at noon, so that the line KH that remains is the sine of the arc ZA, which is the complement of the altitude. We want to find the line HM, which is the sine of the altitude whose azimuth has no inclination; for the line HM is equal to the perpendicular DḤ, which is clearly the sine of the arc BD, the amount of the altitude that we seek, since the circle ABL passes through the zenith and the point of the beginning of Cancer. Now the right-angled triangle ZKG has known sides, and it is similar to the small triangle MHG, since the angle MHG is equal to the angle ZKH, the angle GMH is equal to the angle KZG, and the angle KGZ is common to the two triangles. Therefore the ratio of the line ZK to the line KG is as that of the line MH to the line HG. Further, the ratio of the line HG to the line GK is as that of HM to KZ, and this is also the ratio of the line GM to the line GZ. If we take of the line KZ the part given by the ratio [of the line GM to the line GZ], there remains for us the ratio of the line HM to the line KZ; and it is the same if we take of the line KZ the part that the line GH is of the line GK: we obtain the ratio of HM to KZ.
@n The print names the small triangle «م ه ح», MHḤ; the angles that follow show that it is MHG, as in Nallino's translation. The words in brackets are Nallino's; the codex has «the line KG to the ratio of the line GM».

The computation of this: multiply the line HG, which has been shown to be 30 parts, by the line KZ, which is 58 parts 31 minutes and is the sine of the arc BZ; the result is 1755 parts and half a part. The line KH, which is the sine of the complement of the altitude, is 13 parts 17 minutes, so the lines HG and HK together are 43 parts 17 minutes, which is the whole line KG. When we divide that by the line KG, the result is 40 parts 33 minutes, which is the amount of the line HM that we seek, and the line DḤ is equal to it. Therefore the arc DB is 42° 32′, and this is the altitude whose azimuth has no inclination. This is what we wished to show, if God wills.
@n «And five», in 1755, is supplied by Nallino; the codex has 1750. The quotient 1755;30 / 43;17 is 40;33,29 (Nallino).
@n At latitude 36° 22′ the sine of the amplitude of the beginning of Cancer, HG, is 29;49 parts, not 30, and the rule then gives 42° 26′, the exact altitude of the sun on the prime vertical (sin h = sin δ / sin φ). The 42° 32′ of the text comes from taking HG as 30.
