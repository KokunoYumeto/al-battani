Chapter 19. On finding the arc of the day of a star, and the times of its hours, in every locality
@p AB01-PDF1104-L18..AB01-PDF1103-L10
Al-Battānī said: Suppose you wish to find the arc of the day of any star you like, that is, its stay above the earth from the time it rises until it sets, which is the part of the equator that rises from the rising of the star to its setting. Multiply the sine of the distance of the star from the equator by the sine of the latitude of the locality, and divide the product by the sine of what the latitude of the locality falls short of 90. Multiply the quotient by the radius, divide the product by the sine of what the distance of the star from the equator falls short of 90, and find the arc of the result. The arc you obtain is the difference of the quarter of the circle of the star. If the distance of the star from the equator is northern, add the difference of the quarter of its circle to 90; if it is southern, subtract it from 90. The result after the addition or the subtraction is half the arc of the day of the star; keep it. Then take a sixth of it: the result is the times of its hours above the earth. Then double half the arc of its day: the result is the whole arc of the day of the star above the earth. Subtract it from 360: the remainder is the whole arc of its night below the earth. Likewise subtract the times of its hours of the day from 30: the remainder is the times of its hours of the night, which are below the earth. As for a star that has no latitude, its arc of the day is the arc of the day of the degree in which it is, without any difference, because it runs exactly the same course as the sun.
@n The difference of the quarter of the circle of the star is its ascensional difference, the share of chapter 13: sin Δ = tan φ · tan δ, where δ is here the distance of the star from the equator.
