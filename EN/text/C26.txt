Chapter 26. On finding the distances between the stars according to their configuration in the sphere and the positions of one relative to another; by it the true distance of the moon from the sun is found, according to the latitude of the moon
@n The translation follows Nallino's corrigenda in reading «ضرب», «product», for «صرب» (p. 57, line 16).
@p AB01-PDF1095-L08..AB01-PDF1095-L14
Al-Battānī said: We wish to show how the amounts of the distances between the stars are found on the greatest circle, the one that lies between the two poles of the ecliptic, which is the circle of the signs. It has been shown by proof that in every quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, the products of each two opposite sides, one by the other, added together, are equal to the product of one of its diagonals by the other. In every quadrilateral inscribed in a circle in which two of the sides are parallel, the two remaining sides are opposite and, for the reason given above, equal; when they are produced they meet in a point. Its diagonals too are equal, and the product of one of them by the other is equal to the product of one of the parallel sides by the other together with the product of one of the opposite sides by the other.
@n The first theorem is Ptolemy's theorem on the quadrilateral inscribed in a circle (*Almagest* I 10). For «inscribed in a circle», in the second sentence, the codex has «on a sphere or on a plane»; the reading is Nallino's.
@p AB01-PDF1095-L14..AB01-PDF1094-L10
Since this is so, we draw a line for a piece of the ecliptic and mark its ends A and B, and from the points A and B we draw two lines that meet at Z; let the point Z be one of the poles of the ecliptic, whichever it is. Each of the lines AZ and BZ is then the line of a quarter of the circle that passes through the poles of the ecliptic and the positions of the two stars. We put one of the two stars at the point A of the ecliptic, and the other inclined from the ecliptic in latitude, at the point Ṭ; its position on the ecliptic is known to be the point B. The arc BṬ is the latitude of the star, and we draw the line AṬ, which is the amount of the distance between the two stars. The line AṬ and the arc AṬ are found by drawing from the point Ṭ a line parallel to the line BA, the line ṬH, and from the point Z, the pole, a line to the middle of the line BA, the line ZG, and by marking the sign M at the place where it cuts the line ṬH. The quadrilateral ṬBAH is then a figure of four sides, of which the sides BA and ṬH are parallel, and ṬB and HA equal and opposite; produced, they meet at the point Z. It is clear on the sphere that each of the arcs ZA, ZB and ZG is a quarter of a circle; therefore the arcs ZṬ, ZM and ZH are equal, and so each of the arcs ṬB, MG and HA is equal. By what has been said before, the line ṬM has also been shown to be half the line ṬH. Since the right-angled triangle BGZ is similar to the small right-angled triangle ṬMZ, the line ṬM is known in amount from the line BG, which is already known; and since they are in one triangle, the ratio of ṬM to BG is as the ratio of ZṬ to ZB, and also as the ratio of ZM to ZG. When the line ṬM is known, the whole line ṬH is known, since it is twice ṬM.
@n The sentence from «therefore the arcs ZṬ, ZM and ZH» to «is equal» is printed by Nallino in brackets as the addition of an unskilled reader. Its equalities hold if M lies on the circle of latitude through Ṭ and H; but the arc ṬH of the computation below is an arc of the great circle through Ṭ and H, whose middle is nearer to the pole. The computation does not depend on them: the ratio of ṬM to BG is that of ZṬ to ZB, and the sine of ZṬ is the 51;57,42 used below.
@p AB01-PDF1094-L10..AB01-PDF1094-L16
Let the arc AB between the two stars in longitude be 60 parts, so that the arc BG is half of that, 30 parts. We take the latitude of the star whose position in longitude is the point B to be 30 parts: this is the arc BṬ. Then by what has been said about the sphere the arc MG is also 30 parts, and so the arc MZ that remains is 60 parts; its sine, which is the line MZ, is [51 parts] 57 minutes 42 seconds, approximately. The arc BG has been shown to be 30, and its sine, the line BG, is also 30 parts. The whole arc ZG is a quarter of a circle, and the line ZG is its sine, which is 60 parts, the radius. So if we take of the line BG the part given by the ratio of the line ZM to ZG, there remains the ratio of the line ṬM to the line BG; this is what we wished to show.
@n «51 parts» is supplied by Nallino; the codex lacks it. Nallino takes the passage on the arcs MG and MZ to be corrupt, and reads «the arc ZṬ that remains is 60 parts, and its sine, the line ZṬ, 51;57,42»; and in the last sentence Nallino understands: if BG is reduced in the ratio of ZM to ZG, the result is ṬM.

Al-Battānī said: The computation of this is to multiply ZM by BG, which gives 1558 parts 51 minutes, approximately. When this is divided by the line ZG, the result is 25 parts 58 minutes 51 seconds, approximately, which is the amount of the line ṬM. So the arc ṬM is 25° 39½′, and the whole arc ṬH is twice that, 51° 19′. The quadrilateral BṬHA is then known in its sides, and the diagonal ṬA is known too, by what has been said before about finding the full chords that fall in these sides. Since the sine ṬM has been shown to be 25;58,51, the line ṬH, which is the full chord of ṬH, is twice that, 51 parts 57 minutes 42 seconds. The full chord of the arc BA is twice the sine BG, which is 60 parts. The full chord of the arc ṬB is 31 parts 3 minutes 30 seconds: this is the chord of the 30 parts that are the latitude of the star. The line ṬB is equal to it: it is the sine of 15 parts, doubled, and these 15 are half the arc ṬB. When the side BA is multiplied by the side ṬH parallel to it, the product is 3117 parts 42 minutes, and the product of ṬB by HA, which is equal to it, is 964 parts 37 minutes, approximately. Added together, they equal the product of ṬA by itself, since ṬA is equal to HB. So ṬA by itself is 4082 parts 19 minutes, and its root is 63 parts 54 minutes, approximately, which is the amount of the line ṬA. So the arc ṬA, the arc of the full chord, is 64° 19′, and this is the true distance between the two stars, whose difference in longitude was only 60 degrees.
@n Nallino reads ZṬ and ZB for ZM and ZG in the first sentence, with the same numbers. For 51° 19′ the codex has 51° 59′. The true distance is 64° 20′, since cos d = cos 30° · cos 60°.

Further, if the two stars are both at the point B in longitude, and one of them at the point Ṭ in latitude, the distance between them is the amount of the latitude alone, the arc ṬB. If one of them is at the point Ṭ and the other at the point H, and they have equal latitudes in this figure, the distance between the two stars is the arc ṬH. Likewise, if one of them were at the point H and the other at D, the distance between them would be known, by drawing the line DK parallel to the line BA and to the line ṬH and finding the amount of DK in the way we have described: the quadrilateral DṬHK then becomes known in its sides, and so the line HD between the two stars, the diagonal of the quadrilateral, becomes known too. The distance of the star at the point D from the one at the point A is known from the quadrilateral DBAK.
@n The codex has no figure with the points D and K; Nallino adds one to the Latin translation.
@p AB01-PDF1093-L18..AB01-PDF1092-L07
Likewise, if you wish to know the distance between two stars, look. If one of them has no latitude, like the sun or another of the stars that are on the belt of the signs, and the other has a latitude on either side, take the amount of degrees of longitude between them: this is the first side. Then take half of it and find its sine, multiply it by the sine of what the latitude of the star falls short of 90, divide the product by the radius, and keep the result as it is. Then take its arc and double it: this is the second side. Then find the full chord of the latitude of the star by the rule given to you at the beginning of the book, which is to take the sine of half the latitude and double it: the result is the full chord of the latitude. Then find the full chord of the first side and the full chord of the second side as well; the fourth side is equal to the third, whose full chord is the chord of the latitude. When you have done this, multiply the full chord of the first side by the full chord of the second side, and add to it the full chord of the latitude multiplied by itself, which is the product of the third side by the fourth. Take the root of the sum and find its arc as you find the arcs of full chords, that is, take half of it, find its arc and double the arc: the result is the distance between the two stars.
@n In modern terms, with chords in a circle of radius 1: crd² d = crd Δλ · crd s + crd² β, where Δλ is the difference in longitude, β the latitude and s the second side, sin ½s = cos β · sin ½Δλ. The rule is exact: it amounts to cos d = cos β · cos Δλ.
@p AB01-PDF1092-L07..AB01-PDF1092-L16
If both stars have latitude on the same side, and the two latitudes are equal, find the arc of the second side: it is the amount between them. If the latitudes on the same side are unequal, subtract the smaller from the greater: the remainder is the third side, and the fourth side is equal to it; keep it. Then subtract the latitude of each of them from 90, find the sine of the remainder, and multiply it by the sine of half the degrees of longitude between them. Divide what results for each of them by the radius, find the arc of the quotient and double it: the result is the amount of each of the two sides of longitude; the longer of them is the first side and the shorter the second. Find their full chords, which are twice what results from each of them by the division, multiply one of the two chords by the other, and add to the product the third side multiplied by itself. Take the root of the sum, take half the root, find its arc and double the arc: the result is the distance between the two stars.
@n «The third side multiplied by itself» is its full chord multiplied by itself, as in the next paragraph.
@p AB01-PDF1092-L16..AB01-PDF1092-L22
If the latitudes of the two stars are on different sides, add the two latitudes together: the sum is the third side, and the fourth side is equal to it. Then subtract each of the two latitudes from 90 and find the sine of each, which is the sine of what each of them falls short of 90. Multiply it by the sine of half the degrees of longitude between them and divide what results for each of them by the radius; double the quotient: what results for each of them is the full chord of the first side and of the second side. Multiply one of them by the other, and add to the product the full chord of the third side multiplied by itself. Take the root of the sum and take half of it; find the arc of the result and double the arc: the result is the distance between the two stars.
@n The rules of these two paragraphs are exact as well: they amount to cos d = sin β₁ · sin β₂ + cos β₁ · cos β₂ · cos Δλ.
@p AB01-PDF1092-L22..AB01-PDF1091-L02
It is known that when the two stars are together in one degree, and only one of them has a latitude, or both have latitudes, on the same side or on different sides, the distance between them is only the amount of latitude between them; and when neither has a latitude, the distance between them is the amount of the degrees of longitude, whatever it is. This chapter is needed above all for working out the directions in nativities.
@n The directions (*tasyīrāt*) are the astrological progressions of a nativity (Nallino's *profectiones*).
