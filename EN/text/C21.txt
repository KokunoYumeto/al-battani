Chapter 21. On finding how many hours of the night have passed, by measuring a star, and on finding the ascendant
@n The print has «الّني» for «الّتي» on p. 52, line 1, a dot missing.
@p AB01-PDF1102-L18..AB01-PDF1101-L09
He said: If you wish to know how many hours of the night have passed, by measuring a star, find the degree that culminates with the star, half the arc of the day of the star, and the degree with which the star rises, as has been explained to you before this chapter. Find also the altitude of the star in the middle of the sky. That is: take the distance of the star from the equator; if it is northern, add it to the altitude of the beginning of Aries in that locality, and if it is southern, subtract it from it. The altitude of Aries after the addition or the subtraction is the altitude of that star in the middle of the sky. Whenever it comes out greater than 90, subtract it from 180: the remainder is its altitude above the northern horizon, and the star is then on the north side of the zenith. Then find the versed sine of half the arc of the day of the star, multiply it by the sine of the altitude of the star at the time of the measurement, and divide the product by the sine of the altitude of the star in the middle of the sky. Subtract the quotient from the versed sine of [half] the arc of the day of the star, and find the arc of the remainder as a versed sine; keep the arc you obtain. If at the time of the measurement the star is on the east side of the middle of the sky, subtract this arc from half the arc of the day of the star; if the star is on the west side, add it to it. Half the arc of the day of the star, after the addition or the subtraction, is the amount of the sphere that has turned from the rising of the star to the hour of the measurement; keep it.

Then look: if the part with which the star rises is between the degree of the sun and the degree of its counterpart, the star rose by day; if it is between the counterpart of the degree of the sun and the degree of the sun, it rose by night. If it rose by day, take the times of ascension in the given climate of the degree with which the star rose, and subtract them from the times of ascension opposite the counterpart of the degree of the sun, which is opposite to it, in that climate. Subtract the remainder from the amount of the sphere that has turned since the rising of the star, which you have kept; the remainder is the amount of the sphere that has turned from sunset to the hour of the measurement. Divide it by the times of the hours of the night; the result is the number of seasonal hours of the night that have passed. If the star rose by night, subtract the times of ascension opposite the counterpart of the degree of the sun from the times opposite the part with which the star rose in that climate, and add the remainder to the amount of the sphere that has turned since the rising of the star; the sum is the amount of the sphere that has turned from sunset to the hour of the measurement. Divide it by the times of the hours of the night, in the same way; the result is the number of seasonal hours of the night that have passed. If you divide the amount of the sphere that has turned since sunset by 15, the result is the number of equal hours of the night that have passed.
@p AB01-PDF1101-L20..AB01-PDF1100-L04
If you wish to find the ascendant from the amount of the sphere that has turned since the rising of the star, add the amount of the sphere that has turned since the rising of the star to the times of ascension opposite the part with which the star rises in the climate, and with the sum find the ascendant and the middle of the sky by the rule given before. If you wish to find the ascendant in another way, take the arc that you obtained as a versed sine and subtract it from the times of ascension, in the right sphere, of [the part] that culminates with the star, if the star is on the east side, and add it to these ascensions if it is on the west side. With the times you obtain after the addition or the subtraction you find the ascendant in the same way, and the middle of the sky as well.

If you measure with the moon in particular, you need to know its parallax, so that you have its visible position correct, I mean the position in which it is seen, in longitude and latitude. From it you then find its visible distance from the equator, the visible part that culminates with it, half the arc of the day of its visible position on the ecliptic, half the arc of the day of the visible part that culminates with it, and the visible degree with which it rises. When you know all this, take the altitude of the moon and work with it, after finding the altitude of the moon in the middle of the sky from its visible distance from the equator.
