Chapter 31. On the description of the spheres of the five stars and their states
@p AB01-PDF1057-L03..AB01-PDF1057-L19
He said: As for the description of the spheres of the five stars and of their states, which have become known by way of demonstration from the anomaly of their course: each of them has four spheres, in the manner of the spheres of the moon. The first is made like the ecliptic: its centre is the centre of the ecliptic, it lies level beneath it, and its motion is like its motion. The second is the inclined sphere: its centre is the centre of the parecliptic sphere and its size the size of that sphere, and its greatest inclination from the circle of the parecliptic sphere, towards the north and the south, is the whole latitude of the star. Inside this sphere is another sphere, whose centre lies outside the centre of the two spheres; it hangs from it and touches it at a point, which is the point of the farthest distance. From the amount between the centres of the two spheres the equation of the anomaly and the centre of each of the stars is known, as was shown for the moon. The fourth sphere is the epicycle of the star. Its centre runs on this eccentric sphere from the point of the farthest distance in the order of the signs, by the amount of the mean motion of the star in longitude in a day; and the star moves on the epicycle, from the point of the distance that is seen from the centre of the ecliptic, also in the order of the signs, by the amount of the star's own motion in anomaly each day. The radius of each of the epicycles of the stars is the amount of [the sine of] its mean equation. It has a displacement in the lower and the upper part of the circle: it grows in the lower part, exceeding the mean, and shrinks in the upper part, falling short of the mean. This mean equation is what is set down in the sixth column of the tables of the equation of the star; the amounts of the shortfall are set down in the fifth column, and the amounts of the excess in the seventh. What is set down in the fourth column is the minutes in proportion to which [a part] of the excess and the shortfall is taken, in the way that I gave for the moon for the excess. What is set down in the third column is the equation of the anomaly and the centre, which results from the distance between the centres. These are the figures of the spheres that we said we would draw.
@n The words in brackets, «the sine of», are added by Nallino's emendanda (Part II, p. XXXI). The «centre» is the distance of the centre of the epicycle from the farthest distance of the eccentric, counted at the centre of the eccentric; the «equation of the anomaly and the centre», which the eccentricity produces, is applied to both (Nallino).

@n Nallino prints the figure as the codex gives it, «most ineptly» in his words. Schiaparelli, in his commentary, points out that the circle of the ecliptic and the inclined circle cannot be drawn in one plane as two circles about different centres, that the nodes B and D are put 90 degrees from the line of the apsides, which is true of no planet, and that the lines of sight need not touch the epicycles. He suspects that the circle drawn about M as large as the ecliptic was at first the equant of Ptolemy's theory, the circle about which the centre of the epicycle moves uniformly.
@p AB01-PDF1056-L01..AB01-PDF1056-L16
He said: The first thing I drew was the circle of the parecliptic sphere, marked ABGD, about the centre H; another circle, for the inclined sphere, marked ḤBZD, whose centre is also the point H, as it falls on the globe; and a third circle, for the eccentric sphere, marked ḤṬKL, with M at its centre. Clearly the point Ḥ is the farthest distance of the eccentric sphere and the point K its nearest distance. We take the point Ṭ of the eccentric sphere as the centre of the circle of the epicycle and draw about it the circle of the epicycle, marked FQS. We draw from it the line MṬQ and the line HṬF, take the position of the star on the epicycle to be the point ʿ, and draw the line HʿN, along which the motion of the star on the ecliptic is seen. Clearly the diameter AZ passes through the centres. Again we take the point L of the eccentric sphere as centre and draw about it a circle for the epicycle, marked Y LA Sh; we draw the two lines M L Y and H L LA, put the star at the position Sh of the epicycle, and draw the line H Sh Ḍ, along which it is seen on the ecliptic.
@n The text has S for Sh, in the letters of the second epicycle (the codex has ʿ) and for the position of the star on it; the figure and what follows have Sh, as Nallino's translation reads.
@p AB01-PDF1056-L16..AB01-PDF1055-L17
It is clear in these circles that, when the point A is the direction of the farthest distance on the ecliptic, the star is at the point ʿ of the epicycle whose centre is Ṭ, and the centre of the epicycle is at the point Ḥ, the line that goes out from the point H passes through the centre M and the points A and Ḥ, and we find the point of the farthest distance on the epicycle at the sign Q, on the line MṬQ. For the line MṬQ is then in the place of the line HMA, and the place of Q is then the place of A; so the mean and the true farthest distance are the same, without any difference, since the line HṬF, along which the true point of the farthest distance is seen, then also comes into the place of the line HA. But when the centre of the epicycle comes to the place Ṭ, between the points Ḥ and K of the eccentric sphere, which is less than half its circle, the place of the true distance on the epicycle is the point F and the place of the mean farthest distance the point Q; from Q the course of the star on its own epicycle is counted, which is the arc Qʿ, and so its course in anomaly is greater than the arc Qʿ by the arc QF, which is the difference. Likewise the position of the centre of the epicycle that is seen from the point H on the ecliptic is less than the one seen from the centre M, again by the arc QF, since the point F is nearer to the point M than the point Q is. Likewise, when we put the centre of the epicycle in the second half of the circle, at the point L, the position of the centre of the epicycle in which it is seen on the ecliptic from the centre H becomes greater than the position in which it is seen from the centre M, by the arc LA Y, and the true farthest distance, which is seen from the centre H, becomes less than the mean farthest distance, which is seen from the centre M, again by the arc LA Y. For the star is at the mark Sh of the epicycle; its mean motion on its own epicycle is from the point Y to the point LA and then to the point Sh, and its true motion is from the point LA, so that the arc LA Sh of the epicycle is smaller than the arc Y LA Sh by the arc Y LA. Therefore the equation of the anomaly and the centre is added to the centre when the motion of the centre of the epicycle is between the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric sphere and its point of the nearest distance on the side of the sign L, and it is subtracted from the anomaly; and when the centre of the epicycle is in the second half of the eccentric sphere, on the side of the sign Ṭ, the equation of the anomaly and the centre, which is the arc Y LA, is subtracted from the centre and added to the anomaly. When the true position of the centre on the ecliptic is known, it gives the shares of the difference, of the excess and the shortfall, that belong to the epicycle at its displacement, since it is on the eccentric sphere.
@n The text has the centre of the epicycle move uniformly about the centre M of the eccentric, and so counts the «centre» at M; in Ptolemy's theory the uniform motion is about the equant point, which lies as far from M as H does, on the other side (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary). On the side of Ṭ the equation is the arc QF; the text names the arc Y LA of the other half (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1055-L17..AB01-PDF1054-L03
Further, when the star is at the position ʿ of the epicycle, its distance from the point Q is less than half the circle of the epicycle, so the position in which it is seen on the ecliptic is greater than the position of the centre Ṭ on the ecliptic by the arc on the perpendicular ʿṬ. When the star is at the position Sh, the arc LA Sh of the epicycle is more than half its circle, so the position in which it is seen on the ecliptic is less than the position in which the centre L is seen by the arc that falls on the perpendicular L Sh. Therefore the mean equation of the star, corrected for the displacement of the epicycle, is added to the corrected centre when the corrected anomaly of the star is less than 180, and is subtracted from it when the corrected anomaly is more than 180; what results after that addition or subtraction is the distance of the star on the ecliptic from the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric sphere, whose position on the ecliptic is determined.
@n «The arc on the perpendicular ʿṬ» is the arc whose sine is the perpendicular from Ṭ to the line of sight Hʿ, that is, the angle at H between the centre of the epicycle and the star; in the figure the line of sight touches the epicycle at ʿ (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF1054-L03..AB01-PDF1054-L19
As for the cause of the retrogradation of the wandering stars, it is that the change of the equation of the star, in its increase and decrease in one day, is at some of its places on the epicycle greater than its mean course, which is the motion of the centre of the epicycle on the eccentric sphere in a day. When the true position of the star is computed for a day, and its course for another day is then added to its mean position, and from that is subtracted an equation that exceeds the first equation by more than its mean course in that day, or to it is added an equation that is less than the first equation by more than the mean course of the star in the day, the position of the star on the ecliptic is less than the position in which it was at first. This can happen only when the star is in the nearer half of its epicycle, which is necessarily smaller than the upper half, since the arc of the eccentric sphere bounds these two halves: the lower half is the arc Sh W, and the upper half the arc W LA Y Sh. So when the course of the star from the point of the farthest distance on the epicycle reaches the point W, it is there like one standing set up in the circle, for it then falls on the line that touches the epicycle and goes out from the point H. The decrease and increase of the change of its equation in a day are then equal to its mean course in a day, and no motion appears in it until it passes the point W and falls in the lower half. Then its motion on the ecliptic continues to be seen towards the preceding side of the signs until it reaches the sign Sh and falls on the tangent line a second time, when it is like one standing raised in the circle on that side. When it rises from the point Sh, its motion begins to be seen in the order of the signs, as long as it is in the upper half of the epicycle. Yet the star in itself has no retrogradation in its course; this befalls it only for us, because of the difference of the centres of its spheres and its state on the epicycle.
@n In modern terms, the motion in longitude in a day is the mean motion together with the change of the equation; when the change is negative and greater than the mean motion, the star goes backwards. The sentence from «When the true position» restates this obscurely (Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary).
@n Delambre, cited by Nallino, points out that at the point of contact the star moves along the line of sight, so that its motion on the epicycle adds nothing to the motion of the centre: the star is not stationary there, and the stations fall between the points of contact and the nearest point of the epicycle. The points of contact are not where the epicycle cuts the eccentric either, as the figure itself shows (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1054-L19..AB01-PDF1054-L22
As for the sun and the moon, this does not befall them for us, because the course of each of them in a day is many times greater than the change of its equation in a day. So the state of retrogradation is not evident in them, although they too must in themselves have a course in the nearer half of their epicycles; but it is not perceived in them.
@p AB01-PDF1054-L22..AB01-PDF1053-L10
We have tested the motion of each of these five wandering stars many times at places on their epicycles: at one, when the star was at their point of the farthest distance; at a second, when it was at the point of the mean distance; at a third, when it was at the point of the nearest distance; and at other places of the centre of the epicycle on the eccentric sphere, farther from and nearer to its point of the farthest distance. So we established what appeared of their mean motions in longitude in excess of the motions set down in Ptolemy's book, and also what appeared of the anomaly of their motions and of the positions of their farthest distances on their spheres eccentric to the ecliptic. We corrected it and entered it in the tables, after adding to their course in longitude the correction that we found in each of them. Their equations we found close to those in Ptolemy's book, and the positions of their distances likewise, and we entered them as they were, except the farthest distance of Jupiter: we measured it by the moon many times, according to the visible position of the moon at the times of the measurements, and found that it was less than the amount set down by about 8 parts.
@n In the 740 years between the epochs of Ptolemy's tables (AD 137) and al-Battānī's (AD 880), al-Battānī's mean motions in longitude exceed Ptolemy's by 2° 54′ for Saturn, 3° 5′ for Jupiter and 4° 12′ for Mars, and are better than his (Nallino). Al-Battānī's farthest distances of Saturn and Mars are 20° 18′ greater than Ptolemy's (for Mars with Nallino's correction of the minutes), that of Jupiter only 12° 19′, the 8 degrees of the text. He had raised Ptolemy's longitudes of the fixed stars by 11° 10′; Nallino does not know why he used 20° 18′ for the farthest distances, and supposes that he relied on his own observations.
@p AB01-PDF1053-L10..AB01-PDF1053-L21
Since the motions of the upper stars on their epicycles are what remains of the mean course of the sun when the mean motion of the star in longitude is taken from it, and the mean course of Venus and Mercury is equal to the mean course of the sun, their anomaly is obtained from the tables. We found the anomaly of Venus greater than its anomaly as set down by about 4½ parts, and that of Mercury by about 2½ parts. We divided that over the time between us and Ptolemy, and added what fell to one day to the course in anomaly of each of them for a day. As far as we were able, we have left nothing from which we saw that a defect might arise without putting it right, although their motions cannot be grasped exactly, as the motions of the two luminaries can, since their observations were made only when they came close to some of the fixed stars. Since their farthest distances also move with the motion of the sphere of the fixed stars, we have had no need to set down tables of the motions for the anomaly of each of the upper stars and for the course of the two lower stars, or of the measurement of their distances from the Heart of the Lion or from other fixed stars. We have also left out the mention of the observations of them that we made at the places mentioned, for the sake of brevity and so as not to lengthen the discourse in the explanation that we are attempting for each of them.
@n Over the 740 years between the epochs of the two sets of tables, al-Battānī's anomalies exceed Ptolemy's by 4° 28′ for Venus and by 0° 57′ for Mercury; Nallino's translation therefore has 1 degree for Mercury, where the codex has 2½ and Plato of Tivoli 11½. Such tables, with the positions measured from the Heart of the Lion (Regulus), are in Theon's tables (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF1053-L21..AB01-PDF1052-L04
As for the latitudes of the three upper stars, I mean Saturn, Jupiter and Mars, they come close to the amounts that we found in Ptolemy's book, and we kept them as they were for finding their latitudes. But in the latitudes of Venus and Mercury we found a great discrepancy, which arose, as we think, from the procedure used to find the latitude; so we changed the method of working that we found for them in Ptolemy's book to one that we saw comes close to and agrees with the latitudes that we find for them by observation. It may be that what happened in the procedure in Ptolemy's book came from the translator of his Greek words, or from a fault in the copy from which the book was translated; and God knows best.
@n Al-Battānī's procedure for the latitudes of the five stars is in chapter 47.
