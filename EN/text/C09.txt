Chapter 9. On finding the excess of the longest day, and the lesser excesses of the day, from the altitude of the pole
@p AB01-PDF1121-L03..AB01-PDF1121-L11
He said: Suppose you wish to find the amount of the excess of the longest day, and of the shortfall of the shortest day, with respect to the equinoctial day, from the altitude of the pole when it is given. Take the latitude of the locality and find its sine. Then subtract the latitude of the locality from 90 and find the sine of the remainder: this is the sine of the complement of the latitude of the locality. Then find the sine of the whole inclination, and the sine of what the whole inclination falls short of 90. Multiply the sine of the latitude of the locality by the sine of the whole inclination, divide the product by the sine of the complement of the whole inclination, multiply the quotient by the radius, divide the result by the sine of the complement of the latitude of the locality, and find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain is half the excess of the longest day, and half the shortfall of the shortest day is the same. Double it: the result is the whole excess of the longest day and the whole shortfall of the shortest day. Every 15 degrees of it make one equal hour. Add the hours you obtain to the 12 hours that are the length of the equinoctial day; the sum is the hours of the longest day. Subtract the same excess from 12; the remainder is the hours of the shortest day.
@n In modern terms, sin e = tan φ · tan ε, where e is half the excess of the longest day, φ the latitude and ε the inclination.

If you wish to know the excess of the day at degrees of the ecliptic other than these two, take the declination of any degree of the signs you like and work with it in place of the whole inclination. What you obtain at the end of the work, done in the same way, is the difference of the day at that degree: if the declination of the degree is northern, it is an excess of the day; if it is southern, it is a shortfall at that degree.
