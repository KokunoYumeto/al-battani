Chapter 55. On knowing the ascensions of the signs between the cardines, in the quarters of the sphere
@p AB01-PDF0954-L03..AB01-PDF0954-L09
He said: What must follow what we have described of the amounts of the rays on the circle of the signs is the knowledge of the ascensions of the signs between the cardines. Their ascensions have been known only in the right sphere, which are their ascensions at the cardine of the middle of the sky and the cardine of the earth, and in the climes, which are their ascensions and settings at the two horizons, the cardine of the ascendant and the cardine of the setting point of the circle of the horizon of each town; and what we have described differs in amount. So what remains of the kind of the ascensions is that you know the ascensions of the signs between these cardines, in the regions of the sphere, so that you know in how many times of the equator one of the signs rises at any place of the sphere. By this you know the amount of the times of the equator that lie between the preceding degree of the sphere of the signs and the following degree, in times of the passage of the preceding degree at that place.
@n The translation follows Nallino's emendanda (Part II, p. XXXI) in reading «وفي الاقاليم», «and in the climes», for «في الأقاليم» (p. 198, line 5). The cardines are the four points of the ecliptic on the horizon and the meridian (chapter 35). The ascensions between the cardines are what the astrologers need for their directions: to direct one point to another is to find the arc of the equator after which the first reaches the circle of position drawn through the second (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0954-L10..AB01-PDF0953-L10
For example, the whole sign of Aries rises at the middle of the sky with 27;53 of the times of the equator and passes the cardine of the earth with as many; in the fourth clime it rises with 19;12 of the times of the equator, and in this clime it sets with the amount with which Libra rises in it, which is 36;34. Between each of these cardines and the cardine next to it there are 6 seasonal hours, which are the hours of one quarter of the quarters of the day and the night: in the two quarters above the earth its hours are hours of the day, and in the two remaining quarters, below the earth, its hours are hours of the night. When the sign of Aries moves away from one of these cardines, the amounts of its ascensions vary, becoming more or less than these amounts that we have mentioned, according to the number of seasonal hours by which the beginning of Aries is distant from the cardine with which the comparison is made. Let us first suppose the beginning of Aries to be 2 seasonal hours from the middle of the sky towards the east: the ascensions of Aries there become less than its ascensions at the middle of the sky by 2;54, which is a third of the difference between its ascensions at the middle of the sky and its ascensions in the clime at the eastern horizon, as the 2 hours by which it is distant from the middle of the sky are a third of the 6 hours between the middle of the sky and the ascendant. When the beginning of Aries is 3 seasonal hours from the middle of the sky on this side, its rising there is less than its rising in the right sphere by 4;20, which is half the difference between its rising in the right sphere and its rising at the horizon of the clime; until it comes to the ascendant, where its rising is less than its rising in the right sphere by 8;41, the whole difference between them in the ascensions. Let us suppose too the beginning of Aries to be 2 seasonal hours from the middle of the sky towards the west: since its settings in this western half are like the ascensions of Libra, its settings at this distance are greater than its ascensions in the right sphere by a third of this difference, which is 2;54; when its distance is 3 hours on this side, its passage there is greater than its passage in the right sphere by half the difference, which is 4;20; until it comes to the western horizon, where its passage and crossing in its setting is greater than its rising in the right sphere by the whole difference, 8;41. Likewise, when its distance is from the cardine of the earth towards the ascendant, it goes the way it goes between the middle of the sky and the ascendant; and when its distance is from the cardine of the earth towards the setting point, the matter is as it was between the middle of the sky and the west.
@n The translation follows the corrigenda in reading «الباقِيَيْن», «remaining», for the misprint «البافِيَيْن» (p. 198; the corrigenda give line 15 for line 17). 27;53 is the right ascension of Aries with al-Battānī's obliquity of 23;35; 19;12 is its ascension at a latitude of 36° with Ptolemy's obliquity of 23;51, which with al-Battānī's own gives 19;21. The rule makes the ascension change in proportion to the seasonal hours from the meridian: 27;53 − 19;12 = 8;41, of which a third is 2;53,40 and a half 4;20,30, and 27;53 + 8;41 = 36;34.

If you wish to know the ascensions of any degree you like, in whatever region of the sphere you want, begin by finding the distance of the degree you want, or of the star you want, from one of the cardines. This is found by looking at the degree or the star you want. If it has no latitude, it goes the way of the degree of the signs in which it is: find the times of the hours of the day and of the night of the degree, whichever degree of the sphere you like, which is the degree in which the star is, or another of the degrees of the signs. If the star has a latitude, find the degree that culminates with it and the times of its hours above the earth and below it: find half its stay above the earth and take a sixth of it, which is the times of an hour above the earth; subtract that from 30, and the remainder is the times of its hours below the earth, as we explained at the beginning of the book. You use the degree that culminates with it in place of the degree in which it is [if it has a latitude, and you use the degree in which it is] if it has no latitude; and likewise the times of the hours of the star in place of the times of the hours of its degree that [culminates with it]. If whichever of the two degrees you use is above the earth, take its distance from the part of the middle of the sky by the ascensions of the right sphere; if it is below the earth, take its distance from the part of the cardine of the earth, again by the ascensions of the right sphere. Subtract the ascensions of the degree of the middle of the sky from the ascensions of the degree that you use if it is on the eastern side of the middle of the sky, and subtract the ascensions of that degree from the ascensions of the degree of the middle of the sky if it is on the western side; and do the same with its ascensions and the ascensions of the part of the cardine of the earth in the right sphere, so that you know the distance between the degree you want and the degree of the middle of the sky or of the cardine of the earth in the right sphere. Divide the times of the distance that you have by the times of the hours of the day if the degree used is above the earth, or by the times of the hours of the night if it is below the earth: the hours that result are the distance of the star or of the degree from one of the two cardines, either the cardine of the middle of the sky or the cardine of the earth. Whether the star or the degree is below the earth or above it is known as I shall describe: look at the part that you want to use of the two parts; if it lies between the degree of the ascendant and the degree of the setting point in the order of the signs, that degree is below the earth, and if it lies between the degree of the setting point and the degree of the ascendant in the order of the signs, it is above the earth. You know this another way too: look at half the stay of the star above the earth; if it is more than the times between the degree of the middle of the sky and the degree that culminates with the star, in the right sphere, you know that the star is above the earth, and if half its stay above the earth is the less, you know that it is below the earth. When you know how many seasonal hours the star, or the degree in which it is, is distant from the middle of the sky or from the cardine of the earth, and you wish to know its distance from the ascendant or from the setting point, subtract those hours from 6: the remainder is its distance from one of these two cardines, I mean the cardine of the east or the cardine of the west.
@n The words in the first brackets are supplied by Nallino after Plato of Tivoli; for the words in the second the codex has «in which it is», and Nallino follows Plato. For a star with latitude al-Battānī takes the degree that culminates with it for the right ascensions and the degree that rises with it for the oblique ones, where Ptolemy, like Alcabitius and others, treats the points as if they had no latitude (Nallino's commentary). The hours above and below the earth together make 30 times, a twelfth of the circle, since the arcs of the day and of the night make 360.
@p AB01-PDF0952-L15..AB01-PDF0951-L12
Whenever you wish to know the ascensions of any degree you like at the place of the regions of the sphere where that degree happens to be, it is clear from what we have described that by this the amount of the times of the equator between the preceding degree of the sphere of the signs and the degree on the side following it is known, as the amount between two degrees is known by the ascensions of the clime and the ascensions of the right sphere: that is, to know in how many times of the equator the following degree of the sphere of the signs travels to the place where the preceding degree was. Look: if the given preceding degree is between the middle of the sky and the cardine of the earth on the eastern side, it is in the eastern half of the sphere, and if it is between the middle of the sky and the cardine of the earth on the side of the west, it is in the western half. If it is in the eastern half of the sphere, find how many seasonal hours it is from the middle of the sky, the cardine of the earth or the ascendant, whichever you like, and keep them. Then look at the following degree: if it is with it in the eastern half of the sphere, take the distance between the two degrees by the ascensions of the right sphere and the distance between them by the ascensions of the given clime. If the two numbers are equal, that is the distance of the preceding degree from the following degree in times of the equator. If they differ, subtract the smaller from the larger and take a sixth of the remainder, which is the share of one hour of the difference; multiply it by the hours of the distance of the preceding degree from whichever of the cardines you like to make the comparison with, I mean the middle of the sky, the cardine of the ascendant or the cardine of the earth. If you multiplied it by the hours of the distance of the degree from the middle of the sky or the cardine of the earth, add the product to the times of the ascensions between the two degrees in the right sphere if they are less than those between them by the ascensions of the clime, and subtract it from them if they are the greater; and if you multiplied it by the hours of the distance of the degree from the ascendant, add the product to the times between the two degrees by the ascensions of the clime if they are the less, and subtract it from them if they are the greater. What the times of the ascensions of the cardine with which you compared come to, after the addition to them or the subtraction from them, is the distance between those two degrees by the ascensions of the place of the preceding degree, where it is in the eastern half of the sphere.
@n Nallino's commentary works out the rule as: arc = α + (β − α) × the seasonal hours of the preceding degree from the meridian ÷ 6, where α is the difference of the right ascensions of the two degrees and β the difference of their ascensions in the clime.

If the given preceding degree is in the western half of the sphere and the following degree is with it in this half too, take the times of the ascensions between them in the right sphere and the times of the ascensions in that clime between the two degrees opposite those two degrees, which is the amount between the two degrees by the times of the settings of the clime. Then take a sixth of the excess between these two numbers and multiply it by the hours of the distance of the degree from whichever cardine you like, the cardine of the earth, the cardine of the west or the cardine of the middle of the sky, whichever you want. Add the product to the times of the ascensions or the settings that you have from the cardine with which you compared, if they are the less, and subtract it from them if they are the greater, in the manner given before: that is, if your comparison is with the cardine of the west, add it to the settings between the two degrees in the clime if they are less than what is between them in the right sphere, and subtract it from them if they are the greater; and if you compared with the cardine of the earth or the middle of the sky, add it to what is between the two degrees by the ascensions of the right sphere if they are the less, and subtract it from them if they are the greater. The result is the distance between the two degrees by the settings of the place of the sphere where the preceding degree was.
@n In every clime a sign sets with as much as its counterpart, the opposite sign, rises with (chapter 13).

If the place of the given preceding degree is in one of the two halves of the sphere and the following degree in the other half, find the amount between the preceding degree and the middle of the sky if it is in the western half, or, if it is in the eastern half, between it and the cardine of the earth, by this method that I have described to you; to the result add what lies between the part of the middle of the sky or of the cardine of the earth and the following degree by the ascensions of the right sphere: the sum is the amount between those two degrees.
@n Beyond the meridian the following degree must go on, along its own parallel, as many of its own seasonal hours as the preceding degree is from the meridian; Nallino notes that the codex and Plato of Tivoli do not say that the hours of the preceding degree are to be multiplied by the times of the hours of the following one, and that without this the rule is not right (Nallino's commentary).

If you wish to know this another way, find the hours of the distance of the given preceding degree from the cardine, as I have described to you. Then look: if the preceding and the following degrees are between the middle of the sky and the ascendant, or the preceding part is there and the following part between the ascendant and the cardine of the earth, that is, both in the eastern half, multiply the times of the hours of the day of the following part, which are the times of the hours of that part itself, by the hours of the distance of the preceding part from the middle of the sky, and subtract the product from the times between the part of the middle of the sky and the following part by the ascensions of the right sphere. If the preceding part and the following part are in the western half of the sphere, which is from the cardine of the earth to the middle of the sky on the side of the west, multiply the times [of the hours of the night of the following part by] the hours of the distance of the preceding part from the cardine of the earth, and subtract the product from the times of the ascensions between the part of the cardine of the earth and the following part in the right sphere. If the preceding part is in one half and the following part in the other, that is, the preceding part between the ascendant and the cardine of the earth and the following part between the cardine of the earth and the setting point, multiply the times of the hours of the night of the following part, taken from its counterpart, by the hours of the distance of the preceding degree from the cardine of the ascendant, and subtract the product from the times of the ascensions between the rising part and the following part by the ascensions of the clime. If the preceding part is between the west and the middle of the sky and the following part between the middle of the sky and the ascendant, that is, in different halves, multiply the times of the hours of the day of the following part by the hours of the distance of the preceding part from the cardine of the west, and subtract the product from the times of the ascensions, in the given clime, between the degree opposite the degree of the setting point and the degree opposite the following degree. What remains, whichever of the numbers it is, is the distance between the two degrees in times of the ascensions of the first degree or of its settings. In the same way, conversely, one also knows how many times of the equator ago the preceding part left the place of the following part. These amounts are needed above all in nativities, in directing the significators in their places; this is what Ptolemy mentioned in the book of the Four Treatises that he composed on foreknowledge of events from the science of the stars; and in the same way the haylājs are directed for the lengths of life.
@n The bracketed words are supplied by Nallino after Plato of Tivoli. This rule is Ptolemy's, who sets it out with a worked example in the *Tetrabiblos* III 10 (Nallino's commentary). Ptolemy counts as the same place of a point the place that has the same position with respect to the horizon and to the meridian, that is, the same number of seasonal hours from the meridian; on that definition both rules of this chapter are exact and give the same arc. Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary, takes the circle of position to be the great circle through the north and south points of the horizon, on which Ptolemy says such places lie only nearly, and holds the error of the rules small for the declinations of the planets in the latitudes of al-Raqqa, Antioch and Baghdad. At a latitude of 36°, three hours from the meridian, that circle departs from the seasonal hours by 3.8° of the equator for a point on the equator and one 24° north, and by almost 10° for points 24° south and 24° north; in a direction for a nativity a degree of the equator counts as a year (Nallino's commentary). The significator is the star or point that governs a life, which the Arabs also call by the Persian name haylāj; Nallino takes the last words, on the haylājs, to be probably an interpolation.
