Chapter 29. On finding the inequality of the days with their nights, and on converting the one kind into the other
@p AB01-PDF1079-L17..AB01-PDF1078-L11
He said: Many people, and the common folk, think that the days with their nights are equal in length, I mean that each day with its night is 24 hours for them; but in truth it is not so. One mean day with its night is the rising of all the 360 times of the equator from the horizon circle or the meridian circle, together with the further times of the equator that rise with the 59 minutes that the sun moves by its mean motion in a day and night. One irregular day with its night is the rising of all the 360 times of the equator together with what further rises with the irregular course of the sun in a day and night, which is necessarily either more or less than 59 minutes. A beginning taken from the horizon circle changes and differs in every place with the difference of the ascensions of the signs there, while the beginning from noon is fixed in one state, without change or difference, because the signs pass through the meridian circle alike in every locality. Therefore the beginning of the days in the reckoning of the stars and the computation of their positions is not taken from sunrise or sunset, but from noon or midnight. Further, all the motions of the stars set down in the tables are set down for mean days of equal length. If what accumulates of the difference between the irregular days with their nights and the mean days with their nights is neglected, in the course of the sun and of the other stars it does not amount to anything from which a perceptible error appears; but in the moon it is very evident, because of its speed.
@p AB01-PDF1078-L11..AB01-PDF1078-L20
For the greatest amount that accumulates between the irregular days and the mean days is about half an hour, and in this time the motion of the moon is at times 18 minutes; between the days that exceed the mean days and the days that fall short of them it is twice that. This anomaly is composed of two parts: one is the anomaly of the motion of the sun, which is the equation; the other is the anomaly of the passage of the signs through the middle of the sky, since they do not all rise there with the same amount. The greatest amount that accumulates from the anomaly of the motion of the sun is about 3 parts and a quarter and a tenth, and the greatest amount that then accumulates from the passage of the signs through the middle of the sky about 4 parts and a quarter and a fifth; so what accumulates from the two together is 7 parts 48 minutes, which is about half an hour and a fifth of a tenth of an equal hour. The place of the shortfall is from about two thirds of Aquarius to about the beginning of Scorpio, and the place of the excess from about the beginning of Scorpio to about two thirds of Aquarius.
@n This is the inequality that is now called the equation of time. Al-Battānī counts it from its least value: 7;48 times of the equator are 31 minutes of an hour, close to the range of about 31 minutes over which the equation of time varies.

In this book we have set out the mean motions in the tables on the basis that the assumed position of the sun is at 18 degrees 19 minutes [of Aquarius] by its mean motion, and at 20 parts of it by its true motion, in which it is seen; and with this day and its night you compare the other days of the year in this book.
@p AB01-PDF1077-L01..AB01-PDF1077-L13
He said: If you wish to convert irregular days into the mean days with which the mean motions are taken from the tables, take [the arc] between the first, assumed mean position of the sun and the second position to which it moves, also by its mean motion, and keep the number of degrees. Then take also [the arc] between its first true position, in which it was, and the second position to which it moves by its true motion, in times of the ascensions of the signs in the right sphere. If the number of these times is greater than the degrees of the mean motion that you kept, find what part of an equal hour the difference between them is, and add it to the given irregular days; if the number of times is smaller, subtract it from them. The days that you obtain after the addition or the subtraction are those converted from the irregular days into the mean days, in whichever of the two reckonings is used for the beginning of the days, I mean from noon or from midnight. If you wish to convert from the mean days obtained from the tables into the irregular days that are found, do the reverse: add the difference to the mean days when the number of times is smaller, and subtract it from them when the number of times is greater. The mean days after the addition or the subtraction are those converted from the mean days into the irregular days that are found.
@p AB01-PDF1077-L13..AB01-PDF1077-L20
On the basis that we have laid down in this book for the assumed position of the sun, the number of times is always the smaller, until a long period of time has passed in which the position of the farthest distance of the sun, as we found it on the ecliptic, has changed much, and so what arises from the anomaly of the motion of the sun has changed. Since this is as we have described, we have added 18 minutes to the mean position of the moon at the root of the computation. We have taken the share of each degree of the signs in the amount of the inequality of the days with their nights and entered it in the tables of the ascensions of the right sphere, in the column that follows the ascensions in each sign. When we take what stands opposite the true degree of the sun in that column, find its amount in equal hours and subtract it from the irregular days, what remains is the mean days with which the motions are found from the tables; and when it is added to the mean days, the result is the irregular days that are found by measurement.
@n The root of the computation is the epoch of the tables.
