He said: Having finished with the length of the year and the mean motion of the sun, we try to explain the anomaly that appears in the course of the sun, and its greatest amount, and what appears with it of the position of the point of its farthest distance from the centre of the earth on the ecliptic. In this we follow the method that Ptolemy used in his book, from the sun's passage through the quarters of the ecliptic, by measurements that we made in successive years, renewing the observations as carefully as we could. We established that the sun passes from the autumnal equinoctial point to the spring equinoctial point in 178 days and about 14½ hours, and that it passes from the spring equinoctial point to the autumnal equinoctial point in a longer time: what we found by observation, as far as our diligence reached, is that its true course, which is seen by measurement, takes 186 days and about 14¾ equal hours. It became clear from what we have said that the point of its farthest distance is in this half.
@n Al-Battānī has not shown before why the farthest distance lies in the half that takes longer; Nallino, after Schiaparelli, suspects that something has fallen out. Ptolemy's method is that of *Almagest* III 4.

Then we observed and found that the sun passes from the beginning of Aries to the beginning of Cancer, that is, from the spring equinoctial point to the summer solstitial point, in 93 days and about 14 equal hours, a little less than that. It is also clear that its passage from the spring equinoctial point to the summer solstitial point takes a longer time than its passage from the summer solstitial point to the autumnal equinoctial point. So we knew that the point of the farthest distance, and the centre of the eccentric sphere on which the point of the farthest distance lies, fall, on the ecliptic, in this quarter, which takes longer than the remaining quarter. We found the mean motion of the sun in the 186 days 14¾ hours to be 183 parts 56 minutes 12 seconds, and in the 93 days 14 hours 92 parts 14 minutes 10 seconds, approximately.

We take the point A as the spring equinoctial point; then the sign B is the summer solstitial point, the sign G the autumnal equinoctial point, and the point D the winter solstitial point. In the quarter of the circle on which AB lies, for the reason given above, we mark the sign Z and take it as the centre about which we draw the circle of the sun's eccentric sphere, inside the first circle. We write on it KLMN, on the diameters KM and LN, which intersect at the centre Z at right angles. It is established that the arc AB is 90 parts, and that the arc KL of the eccentric circle is also 90 parts. The point LA of the eccentric circle is its point of the beginning of Aries, and the point Ḍ the place of the beginning of Cancer. So the arc LA K L Ḍ M S of the eccentric is the amount of the eccentric circle that the sun passes over by its mean motion between the beginning of Aries and the beginning of Libra, which, as was said before, is 183 parts 56 minutes 12 seconds. The arc K L Ḍ M is half the eccentric circle, 180 parts; so each of the two arcs KL and SM is half of the 3 parts 56 minutes 12 seconds by which the sun's mean motion exceeded 180, that is, 1 part 58 minutes 6 seconds: the arc LA K is this amount, and the arc MS is equal to it. It is known too that the arc LA K L Ḍ is what the sun passes over on the eccentric by its mean motion from the beginning of Aries to the beginning of Cancer, so that the arc LA L Ḍ of the eccentric circle is of the 92 parts 14 minutes 10 seconds; and since the arc LA K L of it is known from what was said before, 91 parts 58 minutes 6 seconds, the arc LḌ is 16 minutes 4 seconds. Clearly the perpendicular LA Y is half the chord of twice the arc LA K, and the perpendicular ḌḤ half the chord of twice the arc LḌ; so the perpendicular LA Y is about 2 parts 3 minutes 39 seconds and the perpendicular ḌḤ about 16 minutes 45 seconds, the sines of the arcs LA K and LḌ. Since the line KM is parallel to the line AG, the line HF is equal to the line LA Y; and since the line LN is parallel to the line BD, the line ZF is equal to the line ḌḤ. So the side HZ of the right-angled triangle HFZ is known. The line HF multiplied by itself is about 4 parts 14 minutes 48 seconds, and the line ZF multiplied by itself 4 minutes 41 seconds; and the line HZ, which subtends the right angle, multiplied by itself is equal to HF and ZF, each multiplied by itself, added together. So HZ multiplied by itself is 4 parts 19 minutes 29 seconds, and its root is 2 parts 4¾ minutes: this is the line HZ between the two centres. In the measure in which a quarter of the circle about the right-angled triangle HFZ is 90 parts and its radius 60 parts, the arc HZ is about 1 part 59 minutes; this is the whole anomaly of the motion of the sun that appeared from these observations.

What remains for us is to distribute this anomaly over the degrees of the signs, to find its share degree by degree and to enter it in the tables, so that the equation of the motion of the sun is easily found when it is needed. Ptolemy has shown and made clear that the irregular motions are seen and supposed in two ways. One is to imagine that the star has a sphere whose centre is the centre of the ecliptic, and on this sphere another sphere attached to it, whose centre runs on the circle of the first sphere and turns on it. This second sphere is a small sphere that does not surround the earth, and the large sphere is the one that carries the centre of the small sphere in the order of the signs, by the amount of the motion in longitude that is known for the star in the order of the signs, in either of the two ways. The star itself moves on the epicycle, which is the small sphere, either towards the preceding side or towards the following side; or this small sphere carries the star to either side, whichever it is. This motion is the motion of the proper anomaly of the star. The second of the two ways is to imagine that the star has a sphere whose centre is the centre of the ecliptic, and another sphere of the same size, whose centre is not the centre of the first sphere but outside it, and whose circle cuts the circle of the first sphere in two places; the star is on this eccentric sphere, the sphere carrying the star or the star moving on it. Whichever of the two ways you imagine, it comes to the same for this anomaly and what appears from it. We begin with the first way and give an example of it.

He said: In this way we have computed it degree by degree and entered it in the tables, counted from the point of the farthest distance. In this way also the single equation of the moon is computed, and the mean equation of the planets, which is the radius of the epicycle of each of them, when its sine is taken and it is distributed in this way. Whenever you wish to compute this, look at the degrees that the star, or the sun, or the moon, has passed over on the epicycle from the point of the farthest distance, which is called the anomaly of the sun, the moon and the other stars. If this anomaly is less than 180, work with it; if it is more than 180, subtract it from 360 and work with the remainder. The procedure is to take the degrees that you obtain in either way. If they are less than 90, take their sine and the sine of what remains of them to 90, and multiply each of the two sines by the radius of the epicycle of the star, which is the sine of the whole equation. Divide each product by the radius. Add what results for the sine of the complement of the degrees to the 60 that are the radius, multiply the sum by itself, add to it what results for the sine of the degrees multiplied by itself, take the root of the sum and keep it. Then return to what resulted for those degrees, multiply it by the radius, and divide it by the root that you kept. If the degrees with which you work are more than 90, take 90 from them, take the sine of the remainder and the sine of what remains of it to 90, multiply each of the two sines by [the radius of] the epicycle, and divide each product by the radius. Subtract what results for the degrees from 60, multiply the remainder by itself, add to it what results for the complement of the degrees multiplied by itself, take the root of the sum and keep it. Then return again to what resulted for the complement of the degrees, multiply it by the radius and divide by the root that you kept. Find the arc of the quotient. The arc you obtain, in whichever of the two ways, I mean from the first way, which results from the division of the first quantity, or from the division of this second quantity, is the share of those degrees of the anomaly with which you worked, for whichever star you intended, in the anomaly of its motion: it is the equation of the star. The radius of the epicycle of the sun is 2;4,45, the radius of the epicycle of the moon 5;15, the radius of the epicycle of Saturn 6;29,50, the radius of the epicycle of Jupiter 11;30,0, the radius of the epicycle of Mars 39;25,22, the radius of the epicycle of Venus 43;9,0, and the radius of the epicycle of Mercury 22;30,30. This is what has been shown by the observations, and the computation agrees with it; it is the sine of the mean equation of all the stars, if God wills.
@n In modern terms, with r the radius of the epicycle and a the anomaly, the rule is sin q = r sin a / √((60 + r cos a)² + (r sin a)²), the radius being 60; it is exact, and its second form, for a > 90°, is the same rule written with a − 90°. The single equation of the moon is its first anomaly; the mean equation of a planet is the equation produced by its epicycle at its mean distance.
