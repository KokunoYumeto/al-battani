Chapter 57. On the close of the book, and the making of the egg, the brick and the alidade for observation
@p AB01-PDF0943-L03..AB01-PDF0942-L02
He said: What we have mentioned and set down in our book of the causes of things and of the derivations of the principles of the computation, which proceeds by the way of geometrical proof, is in a state that does not change, and no doubt touches it in any circumstance, through all the ages. But what is established by measurements, observations, trials and tests may be found to need correction, by excess or by defect. What of it comes from establishing the very truth of the thing, and falling short of it, becomes small when it is divided over a long time, even if it is perceptible, and what is divided over a short time becomes large, even if it is small. As for an error that arises from the instrument, in its division, its setting up and its adjustment: if one tests with that same instrument while it is in its first state, the error is the same at both times; and if the error comes from the division alone, its setting up and adjustment may be corrected if they are changed, while the error from the division remains as it was, and when one observes with another instrument the difference appears. It may also happen that over a long time the instrument changes from its state, in widening, contracting, bending and the like; the error that comes from this may increase or decrease, according as these are small or great. When one measures after that with a correct measurement in which there is no doubt, there must accumulate in it later, over a period like the one between the two times, an error like the first, if it proceeds in one way without changing. Things of this kind are sound only when the measurement is made with two instruments perfect in all respects, or with one correct instrument that has not changed from its first state in anything; and what there is in them of the shortcoming of man, by his nature, in reaching the truths of things in act as he reaches them in potency, is slight and imperceptible with diligence and care, above all over long periods. Talent helps, and so do zeal, true observation, the use of thought and patience with things, even when they are hard to grasp; and much of this is hindered by impatience and by the love of glory and of favour with the kings of men, in grasping quickly what cannot truly be grasped, or in grasping what by its nature no one can grasp.
@n Ptolemy makes the same points on the errors of instruments and on long intervals (*Almagest* III 1; Nallino).

Since we have come in this book to this place [and have dealt, as far as we could, with everything that had to be mentioned first, we think it right to close at this place with our description of] the instrument whose form is the form of the sphere, which is called the egg, and of the two instruments described for observation, if God wills.

@p AB01-PDF0942-L03..AB01-PDF0942-L18
The making of the instrument in the form of the sphere, on which the stars of the ether are drawn, which is called the egg. He said: We take a ball of copper, perfectly round, true on every side, with a smooth surface, turned on the lathe, of any size you like. We mark on it two poles opposite each other on its diameter, halve the distance between the poles on the outside of the ball, and draw at the halfway mark a circle that cuts the ball in halves; we divide it into equal quarters and mark a point at each quarter. We take one of the points as a centre and draw about it a circle as large as the first, which passes through the first two poles of the ball and cuts the first circle into two opposite halves. We divide one of the quarters of the first circle into 90 and take from it the amount of the whole declination, which is 23 parts and 35 minutes; we take with the compasses as many of the parts of the quarter, put one of its points on one of the two poles and turn the other point to the second circle, whose pole is one of the points, and mark a point on it. We do the same with the other pole, turning the point of the compasses to the side opposite the first, so that one of these two points is opposite the other on the diameter of the circle as well. We take one of them as a pole and draw about it a circle midway between these two points; so we have drawn two circles that cut each other at two opposite points. We make one of the two circles the circle of the equator and the other the circle of the sphere of the signs. It is known that the pole of the circle of the sphere of the signs lies below the pole of the equator, towards the north, and that the circle that passes through the poles is the circle of Cancer and Capricorn; the point of the circle of the sphere of the signs that is above the equator is the point of the head of Cancer, and the point below the equator the point of the head of Capricorn; of the two points at which the circle of the sphere of the signs and the circle of the equator cut each other, one is the point of the head of Aries and the other the point of the head of Libra.
@n The instrument joins the uses of Ptolemy's spherical astrolabe (*Almagest* V 1) and of his solid sphere (*Almagest* VIII 3); al-Battānī may have been the first to devise it (Nallino's commentary). 23;35 is al-Battānī's obliquity of the ecliptic.
@p AB01-PDF0942-L19..AB01-PDF0941-L01
We draw the signs in their order, making each quarter three signs, divided equally, each sign into six cells with 5 parts in each cell, and we write on the cells the totals of the count in the letters of the abjad, up to 30 parts. We complete the division of the circle of the equator into 360 parts, in which there are 72 cells, and write in each cell its number in the letters of the abjad, up to 360 parts, beginning the writing from the point that cuts the head of Aries, so that the end of the 360 also falls at the beginning of this point, which is the end of its twelfth sign.
@n The letters of the abjad have the values of numbers.
@p AB01-PDF0941-L01..AB01-PDF0941-L11
We draw the positions of the fixed stars in all the constellations, or those of them that we like, in the way that I shall describe. We take with the compasses from the circle of the equator the amount of the latitude of the star, put one point of the compasses on the part in which the star is and turn the other point towards the side of the latitude, drawing a faint line that leaves no lasting trace on the ball. Then we take other compasses, opening their points to a quarter of the circle that goes round the ball, and put one point on the quadrature of the star's part on the circle of the signs, 90 parts from the star's degree; the other point then necessarily falls on the part in which the star is. We turn it towards the line that we drew for the latitude with the other compasses: where the two lines cut each other is the centre of the star, and we draw it there; and so until we have finished all that we want of them in this way, each according to its place in longitude and latitude, having drawn first, if we like, through each sign a circle passing through it and through the two poles of the sphere of the signs, to show the divisions of the signs more clearly. So there are twelve circles on the outside of the ball, passing through the poles of the sphere of the signs and dividing the signs.
@n The second compasses, opened to a quarter of a great circle with one point 90 parts from the star's longitude, draw the circle through the poles of the ecliptic and the star's degree; on it the star lies at its latitude from the ecliptic.
@p AB01-PDF0941-L11..AB01-PDF0941-L21
Then you take a ring of copper with square faces, true in its roundness and its edges, its thickness the breadth of a thumb and its breadth as much as its strength requires, so that it does not shake; and you take another ring like it, made in the same way, striking its inside with the compasses of the inside of the first and its outside with the compasses of its outside, and you file it until it is even on every side and true in its roundness. Make the width of each of these two rings the amount of the diameter of the ball, so that the ball turns inside these two rings, fitting them closely. You take two other rings, making the thickness of one of them a third of the thickness of one of the two [first] rings and the other two thirds of that thickness, so that when the smaller of these two is set in the larger they are like one of the two rings: that is, you strike the inside of the smaller with the compasses of the inside of the two rings and its outside as is fitting, and the inside of the larger with the compasses of the outside of the smaller and its outside with the compasses of the outside of the two rings. You take also a fifth ring, striking its inside with the compasses of the outside of the large rings mentioned above and its outside as is fitting, so that these rings turn inside this ring, fitting it closely, without play or shaking in any of these rings; and let its faces be even.
@n The text passes from «we» to «you» here and back to «we» below, as the codex does (Nallino).
@p AB01-PDF0941-L21..AB01-PDF0940-L12
Then you take one of the two first rings as the ring of the horizon, and divide it and the rest of the remaining rings into equal quarters, dividing each quarter into 18 cells of 5 parts each, so that each quarter has 90 parts; you write in the cells, in the letters of the abjad, what is due to them, beginning the count from one of the quarters up to the end of the 90 on both sides; you divide the opposite quarter in the same way and write it in the letters of the abjad as well, so that the 90, which would fall in four places of the ring, falls in two places of it, fixed at the end of each quarter. On one of the two places where the 90s meet you write «the point of the north», and on the place opposite it «the point of the south». You set a mark on the small ring at one of its quarters and make it the north pole, and the one opposite it, half the ring away, the south pole; you bore this ring at these two opposite places with a hole in the middle of its breadth and thickness, and likewise you bore the two poles of the equator in the ball, and fix the ball in this small ring at these two places. We nail it with two nails filed flush with the outside of the ring, so that the ball turns on the two poles of the equator, which are these two poles. Then we fit on it the ring inside which this one lies, after dividing it into 360 parts and 72 cells and writing on it in the letters of the abjad as we wrote before, except that the letters written in the cells reach to the edge of the ring and those that fall in the circle of the horizon two thirds of that; we make the writing in the manner given before, so that the 90s meet in two opposite places, twice in each place.
@p AB01-PDF0940-L13..AB01-PDF0939-L06
Then, from the place where the count began, towards the lower part of the ring, we cut a notch sunk into this ring to half its thickness, making the width of the notch the thickness of the ring of the horizon; this notch is on the outside of this ring; and we cut a like notch at the opposite place as well. Then we cut in the ring of the horizon, on its inside, a notch as deep as the notch in the other ring together with the thickness of the small ring, making the notch on both sides of the line of the north and the south, evenly, as wide as the thickness of the ring in which we cut the first notch. Then we fit one of the two rings into the other on the ball, so that the plane of the circle of the horizon bounds the upper half of the ball, the thickness of the ring lying towards the lower half, and from every side of the plane of the ring of the horizon to the top of the dome there are 90 parts. Then we cut on the outside of the ring of the horizon, on both sides of the line of the east and the west, two even, opposite slots, half its thickness deep, and in the inside of the other remaining ring, on both sides of its two opposite quarters, a notch as deep as the notch of the other ring, and we fit it on the ring of the horizon, having also cut on the outside of the ring of the middle of the sky, on both sides of the line of the dome and of the cardine of the earth opposite the dome, a notch half the thickness of the upper ring deep, and cut in this ring, on its inside, on both sides of the two remaining quarters, a notch as deep as that notch together with the thickness of the small ring in which the poles are. When we have done this, the ring that stands on the ring of the horizon and cuts between the north and the south is the ring of the middle of the sky, with the line of the middle of the sky in the middle of its thickness; and the other ring, which cuts between the east and the west, bounds the part of the ball between the north and the south, with the line of the east and the west in the middle of its thickness.
@n The top of the dome is the zenith, 90 parts from the horizon all round, and the line of the dome the upper end of the meridian, opposite the cardine of the earth below; Nallino compares the dome of the earth of chapter 6.
@p AB01-PDF0939-L06..AB01-PDF0939-L20
Then you divide the quarters of the great ring, in which these rings turn, into 90 parts and 18 cells, and we set in each cell its number in the letters of the abjad up to 90, as we did before. We bore in the middle of the thickness of this ring a hole going through, on both sides of the line of the quarter from which we began the division, and above it we cut a slot in the top of the ring, on both sides of the line, as long as a quarter of the ring. We make a square piece of copper, of the thickness of the ring and the width of the slot, and cut in its middle a straight line dividing it into two equal halves; we file it on both sides of this line with the file and thin it towards the lower part of the piece, filing it round, and make its lower end sharp like a nail; its length is such that it goes through the edge of the ring and its sharpened lower end touches the face of the ball. We notch the rest of its square part, in the slot, to the depth of the slot; and what shows of it above the ring is the size of a thumb, or as is fitting, so that this part that shows is an indicator of the ray and of the altitude; we fix it in its place whenever we wish. Then we mount this ring in two clamps like the pin of the astrolabe, with two pointed ends, for which we bore holes in the middle of the thickness of the ring of the middle of the sky and of the ring between the east and the west; the ring runs in the housings of these two pins, as with the horse in the pin of the astrolabe, towards the north and the south. We make the upper of them a loop and a ring, by which the ball is hung as the astrolabe is hung. We contrive to fasten the ends of the two pins so as to fix the ring in its place, and it turns with the turning of the pins towards the east and the west; we contrive this by cutting in the great ring a slot as long as the end of the pin that goes into the hole, so that when the ring is set right in its place we fasten it with a piece of copper that fills it, and it does not move from its place, if God wills.
@n The horse is the wedge through the pin of the astrolabe that holds its parts together (Nallino's commentary). Schiaparelli, in Nallino's commentary, explains the instrument with a drawing: the globe turns on the poles of the equator in the smallest ring, a movable meridian, which turns in its own plane inside a fixed meridian joined to the horizon and the prime vertical; this frame turns about the vertical line inside the great ring, a vertical circle that carries the indicator of the sun's rays.
@p AB01-PDF0939-L21..AB01-PDF0938-L09
When we wish to take the altitude in any town we like, we raise the north pole of the equator, marked on the small ring, above the northern horizon by the latitude of the town and fix it in that state; then we fit the indicator of the ray and the altitude in its place, hang the ball by our hands as the astrolabe is hung by its handle, and turn the indicator towards the sun in the quarter of the horizon in which it is; we turn the ring towards the north and the south until the indicator shades itself, which happens only when it points straight at the sun. Then we find the part of the signs in which the sun is, turn that part to the quarter in which the sun is, and keep the ring as it is: the parts of the quarter that rise above the circle of the horizon are the amount of the altitude. When we move the ring towards the part of the sun, we keep moving it and moving the part of the sun until the sharp end of the indicator that touches the ball falls on the part of the sun drawn on the line of the sphere of the signs; and this can happen as we have described only at the place of the sphere where the sun is at that time, according to its altitude above the horizon. When it falls so for us, the sphere stands for us in its configuration at that time: the part of the sphere of the signs that the ring of the horizon cuts on the side of the east is the part rising at that time, and the part that it cuts on the side of the west is the part setting; the part of the sphere of the signs cut by the middle of the thickness of the ring of the middle of the sky is the part in the middle of the sky, and likewise the cardine of the earth opposite it.
@p AB01-PDF0938-L09..AB01-PDF0938-L18
When we wish to know the hours that have passed of the day, we look at the arc of the equator that the ring of the horizon cuts from the time when the sun's part rises on the ball until the part rising at that time rises: that is what has turned of the sphere from sunrise to the hour of the measurement, and every 15 degrees of it are one equal hour; divided by the times of the hours of the sun's part, it shows the seasonal hours. Whenever we raise the pole, the turning of the signs and the increases of the day become clear to us, until we raise it 90 parts; and the ascensions of the signs in every town become clear in the usual way, and other things. We should write on the ring of the horizon, in the third of it that remains, towards the north, «the summer risings», and towards the south of the line of the east «the winter risings»; likewise from the line of the west, towards the north, «the summer settings», and towards the south «the winter settings»; so that we have shown all that is needed of the azimuths of the risings and the settings. When we have set the part of the sun, with the indicator in its state on it, facing the sun, the ring of the middle of the sky faces the meridian line.
@n Nallino takes «the third» for an error for «the quarter».

He said: You take a square brick of copper, of stone or of wood, its side two cubits; the larger it is, the more accurate. It is the brick A B G D. We take the point A as a centre and draw about it, with the amount of A B, A G, the arc B G, and divide it into 90 divisions, the parts of the quarter, by lines that pass through the centre and the divisions marked on the arc, and between the parts into as many minutes as possible. Let the face of the brick be smooth, perfectly level, neither inclined nor shaking, so that the divisions on it are true.
@p AB01-PDF0937-L10..AB01-PDF0936-L03
Then we take two pegs of copper of equal size, turned on the lathe, pointed at the ends, and fix one at the centre of the point A and the other at the centre of the point G. We have first found the meridian line, which is the line H Z, letting the plumb line down from the end of the rod at the centre A onto the end of the peg at the centre G, so that neither the face of the brick nor its setting is inclined; the face with the drawings and the divisions faces the east, and its side on which A B are lies along the direction of the south. We observe the shadow at the times of noon and note the place of the shadow of the peg at the centre A among the divisions of the quarter each day. We take a piece of copper fitted to the curve of the arc B G, the piece Ṭ, and draw in its middle a line, the line at the place Ṭ, so that this piece is put under the place of the shadow and its place among the parts shows clearly, so that its marking is not confused for us; the line Ṭ is on the middle of the width of the peg's shadow, and so we know on which line of the parts of the divisions and of their minutes it falls. From that we know the extreme distances of the sun from our zenith in summer and in winter: let the point Ḥ be the summer limit and the point K the winter limit; so the arc K Ḥ is the arc between the two solstices, and its half is the mark L. Whenever the sun passes the vernal or the autumnal equinoctial point, the shadow of the peg at the place A falls on the point L of the arc B G. By this the distance of the sun from the point of the zenith is always known, every day, and its altitude above the horizon, if God most high wills; success is from God. The brick must also be truly square, its angle a right angle in agreement with the count, if God most high wills.
@n For G the codex has B, here and below; «the rod» is, Nallino thinks, the peg; for «the south» the codex has «the qibla», and for «autumnal» «winter» (Nallino). Nallino takes «which is the line H Z» for a wrong addition of the codex, which Plato of Tivoli has too: in the codex's figure H and Z stand near B and D, and the meridian line, along which the brick stands, can only be A B. He said: We take three rulers of wood, straight, with square faces, and draw along the middle of each of them a line running along the length of its face; we make this figure a picture of the lines that run along the middles of the rulers: they are the ruler Z Ḥ, the ruler Z L and the ruler Ṭ M. We put on the ruler Z Ḥ, on its line, the mark Ṭ, making the line Z Ṭ five cubits, and fix the line Ṭ Ḥ, the rest of the ruler, in a stone or a pillar, firmly, so that it neither moves nor changes nor shakes. Then we take the second ruler, which is smaller than the third, and make its line Z L equal to the line Z Ṭ; we put on it two sights of copper of equal size, like the sight of the astrolabe, across its breadth, which passes over the face of the ruler Z Ṭ, fixing them firmly on the face of the ruler, and make in their middles two holes opposite each other; we fix one of the two sights near the point Z and the other near the point L. We bore these two rulers at the mark Z and join them with a pin, fastening it as we fasten the pin of the astrolabe, so that we can move the ruler Z L towards the north and the south as we like, without shaking, play or twisting. Then we take the ruler Ṭ M and make its line Ṭ K equal to each of the lines Z Ṭ and Z L; we divide the line Ṭ K into 30 parts, and between the parts into as many minutes as possible, a true division of equal sizes, and divide the line K M, the rest of the ruler, by the size of those divisions, as finely or as coarsely as we like, up to the halved chord of 45 parts, so that the most that the divisions of the line Ṭ M reach is about 42½ parts; what remains of the ruler we cut off. Then we bore the rulers Z Ṭ and Ṭ M at the point Ṭ with two round holes, as we bored the first ones, and join them with a pin, fastening it as we join the pin of the astrolabe, so that we can move the ruler Ṭ M to the north and the south as we like, without play or shaking.

A. On finding the beginnings of the years of the Arabs and of their months, which are worked with in dating by the tables
@n Eight short chapters without numbers follow chapter 57 in the Escorial codex; Nallino marks them A to H. Plato of Tivoli has none of them. Nallino holds them genuine except D and probably F: A, B and C complete chapter 32, at whose end al-Battānī promised them, E is announced at the end of chapter 53 and H in chapter 42, and they explain al-Battānī's own tables, beside which some of them probably once stood (Nallino's commentary).
@p AB01-PDF0934-L09..AB01-PDF0933-L06
He said: If you wish to know the sign of the year you are in, of the years of the Arabs, which are the years of the Hijra, look at how far the years of the Hijra exceed 210 years: cast them off by 210s and work with what remains below that, for in every 210 years they return to their first order, always. When you know the years that result, with the year you are in, even if only one day of it has begun, look for the same number in the rows of the numbers of the tables of the collected years and take the sign of the years opposite it; if you do not find the same number in the collected years, look for the nearest to it below it, and where you find it take the sign of the years under it. Then see what years remain up to the year you are in, enter them in the row of the single years and take the sign of the years under them, and add it to the first sign, which you kept from the collected years: if the sum is more than 7, cast off 7 from it, and what remains is the sign of the year you want. If you entered the number in the collected years and nothing remains to enter in the single years, always add one to the number you find under the collected years; or, if you like, take in the collected years what is 30 less than the number you have, take what stands opposite 30 in the single years and add the one to the other: you will hit the right result whichever way you work, and it is the sign of the year you are in. Cast off the number from Sunday: the day at which your count ends is the day on which the year begins, the first day of al-Muḥarram of that year. If you want another of the months, take the sign of the month you want from the table of the months and add it to the sign of the year; if the sum is more than 7, cast off 7 from it, and cast off what remains, or what you have below 7, from Sunday: the count stops at its end at the name of the day on which the month you want begins.
