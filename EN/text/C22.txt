Chapter 22. On finding the altitude of the stars from the hours of the night, in every locality

He said: If you wish to find the altitude of any star you like from the hours, take the degree that is in the middle of the sky at that time, and the rising and the setting degree. Then find the distance of the star you want from the line of the middle of the sky at that time. That is: take the times between the part of the middle of the sky and the part that culminates with the star, in the right sphere. If the degree that culminates with the star is to the east of the degree of the middle of the sky, subtract the times of ascension of the degree of the middle of the sky from the times of ascension of the degree that culminates with the star; if it is to the west, subtract the times of ascension of that degree from the times of ascension of the degree of the middle of the sky. The result after the addition or the subtraction is the distance of the degree that culminates with the star from the line of the middle of the sky.
@p AB01-PDF1100-L19..AB01-PDF1099-L05
If you wish to find this in another way, take the times opposite the part with which the star rises in the climate, and take the times opposite the counterpart of the part with which the star sets. If the part that culminates with the star is to the east of the middle of the sky, subtract the times opposite the degree with which the star rises from the times opposite the rising part; if the degree is to the west of the middle of the sky, subtract the times opposite the rising part from the times opposite the counterpart of the part with which the star sets. What you obtain in either case is its distance from the horizon. Subtract it from half the arc of the day of the star; the remainder is the distance of the star from the line of the middle of the sky.
@p AB01-PDF1099-L05..AB01-PDF1099-L14
When you know the distance of the star from the line of the middle of the sky, on whichever side it is, find the versed sine of this distance and subtract it from the versed sine of half the arc of the day. Multiply the remainder by the sine of the altitude of the star in the middle of the sky, divide the product by the versed sine of half the arc of the day of the star, and find the arc of the quotient as a direct sine, as you take the arc of the sines; the arc you obtain is the altitude of the star at that hour. It is known that if the part with which the star rises follows the part that is rising at that time, the star has not yet risen; if it precedes the rising part, the star has risen and is above the earth. If the part with which the star sets follows the setting part, the star has not yet set and is above the earth; if it precedes it, the star has set. For a star does not rise until the part with which it rises has risen, and does not set until the part with which it sets has set. As for the moon, you find its visible altitude from its distance from the equator, if God wills.
