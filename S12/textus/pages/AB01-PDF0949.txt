(And most of what) is needed of the knowledge of these amounts is in nativities, in the directing of the indicators in their places; it is what Ptolemy mentioned in the book of the four treatises that he composed on the foreknowledge of things to come from the science of the stars; and in the like of this the hīlājs are directed for the lifetimes. Chapter fifty-six: on the making of a plane instrument and an upright one, with each of which one knows what has passed of the day in seasonal hours in every town; it is also called the rukhāma (the marble slab, the sundial). He said: if you wish to know what has passed of the day in seasonal hours, from the time of sunrise to sunset, with the plane instrument, from the surface of the shadow of the sun, take a slab of marble or a plate of brass, of level surface and smooth face, of whatever size you wish; the best that you can take is one whose breadth is about two thirds of its length. Mark, at the amount of two thirds of the breadth, in the middle of the length, a point, and take it as a centre; describe on it a circle of whatever size you wish; then quarter the circle by two lines that cut each other at its centre at right angles and divide the circle into four equal quarters; then divide each quarter into ninety parts, a true division, by steps of one degree or more, according to what you can manage and what is possible in the width and the narrowness of the circle. Then find the shadow of the beginning of Cancer and of the head of Capricorn for one hour, for two, for three, for four, for five and for six seasonal hours, and the azimuth of the shadow at each of those hours on the circle of the horizon, in the directions that were set out for you earlier in the book, in the chapter on knowing the azimuth of the shadow and the altitude in the parts of the signs in every town; that is, you find the altitude of each of these hours and then find from it its shadow and its azimuth, in the way set out before, in whatever town you wish. Then take a ruler with straight edges, one of whose faces is divided into equal divisions, as many as you wish, provided that they are as many as the number of the shadow of the head of Capricorn or more. Then put the first point, from which you began, of the edge of the ruler on the point of the centre of the circle, and lay the edge of the ruler on the azimuth of the shadow of one hour of the hours of Capricorn, toward the wide side of the slab; make the beginning of the number of the azimuth from the point of the east on the circumference of the circle; then you count from (the sentence runs on).
