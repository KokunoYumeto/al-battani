% Printed p. 183: chapter 50 continued: Venus, the epicycles of Mercury and of Venus, and the beginning of Mars. [When the hundred and sixty-six,] which are the farthest distance of Mercury and the nearest nearness of Venus, are multiplied by the six and a half that are the amount of the difference of the magnitude of Venus to one, the farthest distance of Venus is a thousand and seventy, and that is the nearest nearness of the sun; its mean distance is therefore six hundred and eighteen. They measured the diameter of Venus against the diameter of the sun when it was at its mean distance, and found it one part of ten of the diameter of the sun. When a tenth of the six hundred and eighteen parts is taken, it is sixty-one parts and four fifths; and when that is divided by the two hundred and one and a half, it is of the diameter of the earth a quarter and half a tenth and a little something that cannot be confined. Therefore its diameter subtends thirty-two minutes and twenty-seven seconds of the circle of the sphere. When that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the magnitude of Venus is one part of thirty-six of the magnitude of the earth, approximately. As for the centre of the epicycle of Mercury and of Venus, their course on the eccentric circle is like the course of the centre of the epicycle of the sun, and the magnitude of the diameter of the epicycle of each of them is known from the farthest distance to its first station, which is its greatest distance from the sun on the forward side of the signs. The greatest distance of Mercury from the sun is twenty-six degrees, when the sun is opposite the centre of the epicycle and the centre of the epicycle is at the farthest distance of the eccentric; Mercury is once before the sun and once behind it. As for the greatest distance of Venus from the sun, it is 46 degrees, when the sun is opposite the centre of the epicycle and the centre of the epicycle is at the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric; Venus is once in front of the sun, once before the sun and once behind it. The least distance of Venus from the sun, from the first station to the second station, is 41 degrees, and the least distance of Mercury from the sun, the amount between the two stations, is 21 degrees. From that it became plain that the diameter of the epicycle of Mercury subtends 47 degrees and the diameter of the epicycle of Venus subtends 87 degrees. As for «Mars», its distance and the magnitude of its body are according to what was said of it when they measured the difference of its magnitude; they said also that they found its magnitude at its nearest distance seven times its magnitude when it is at its farthest distance. Its nearest distance is the farthest distance of the sun, which is, as we found by measuring, 1146; when that is multiplied by the seven times, it comes to eight thousand and twenty-two, and its mean distance is four thousand five hundred and eighty-four. They measured it when it was at its mean distance and found its diameter one part of [the next page continues].
