% Printed p. 185: chapter 50 continued: Saturn, the fixed stars, the order of the bodies of the world by size, and the beginning of the testing of the diameters. [The farthest distance of Saturn being eighteen thousand and ninety-four,] its mean distance is therefore fifteen thousand five hundred and nine. They found the diameter of Saturn at its mean distance one part of eighteen of the diameter of the sun; when its mean distance is divided by that, the diameter of Saturn comes to eight hundred and sixty-one and a half and an eighth, approximately; when that is measured against the two hundred and one and a half which is the diameter of the earth, its diameter is like the diameter of the earth four times and a sixth and an eighth, approximately. When that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the magnitude of Saturn comes to be like the magnitude of the earth nearly seventy-nine times, and the diameter of Saturn subtends 8;12 of the circle of the sphere. The width of its epicycle is known [from its motion] from its first station to its second station, and the motion of its epicycle, which is two minutes in a day, and its motion at the bottom of the epicycle, 0;57 in a day, as though it were seen toward the west: the diameter of its epicycle subtends 12;26. And the diameter of the sun ought to subtend 39;48 of the circle [of the sphere]. As for the distance of the fixed stars and their magnitude: they mentioned 15 stars of the first magnitude and said that their distance is, in the way we have described, nearly nineteen thousand times the like of half the diameter of the earth; they measured their magnitude against the sun and found it one part of twenty of the sun; when their distance is divided by that, the diameter of each of those stars is nine hundred and fifty; when that is measured against the diameter of the earth, it is like it four times and two thirds of a time and half a tenth of a time, approximately; when that is multiplied in length, breadth and depth, the magnitude of each of those stars is like the magnitude of the earth nearly a hundred and five times. We have divided the fixed stars that are in the constellations into six magnitudes; each class below these fifteen stars lessens in magnitude until the sixth magnitude is reached, where the magnitude of a star is like the magnitude of the earth sixteen times. So the greatest of the created bodies of the world is the sun; the second, the fifteen stars of the first magnitude; the third, Jupiter; the fourth, Saturn; the fifth, the rest of the fixed stars, which are of the second magnitude [to the sixth]; the sixth, Mars; the seventh, the earth; the eighth, the moon; the ninth, Venus; the tenth, Mercury, which is the smallest of the bodies of the world. «Whoever wishes to repeat» the test of any of these diameters, let them take an alidade and mount on it two sights pierced with holes that face each other, and make the hole next to the eye small and the one next to the star of the size in which the whole body of the star is gathered, no more [the next page continues]. The notes: the codex has «and seven» for «and nine» and «and ninety» for «and seventy»; for 8;12 the codex has another reading; the words in the first square brackets are a conjecture like that of p. 184, line 17 (compare p. 184, lines 5-6); the codex has 0;40 for 0;57 and 32;26 for 12;26; on the sun's numeral Nallino refers to the notes on the translation; the words «to the sixth» are added from conjecture, Plato having only «quinto reliquae fixae stellae».
