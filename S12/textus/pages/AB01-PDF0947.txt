(So that the north point falls on the line of midday) at what adjoins the width, and the south point on the line of midday at what adjoins the narrow side, and the east point and the west point fall in their place on the line that quarters the line of midday, which is the line between east and west. You begin the hours from the side of the west, in the width of the slab, and write the first hour, the second and the third under each point of the points of the hours of Capricorn, to the end of the eleventh; one cannot know with the slab more than what lies between one hour past of the day and the end of eleven hours, because of the stretching of the shadow and its length at the two ends of the day; it would need a great instrument for the surface of the shadow to fall on. If you wish to divide what lies between the hours into halves and thirds, and more or less, you learn the azimuth of every fraction that falls between those hours and its shadow, and mark it as you wish; that is not difficult. When you have finished making the slab, go to a place where the horizon is open from one hour of the day to the end of eleven hours; describe on it a circle and find on it the line of midday in the way mentioned earlier in this book; then put the line of midday drawn on the slab on the surface of the line of midday that you found with the circle, lying on it, neither inclined nor turned aside, so that the direction of the south of the slab, which is the narrow side, faces the south of the line, in its direction, and the northern, wide side then lies in the direction of the line of midday toward the north. Let the upper surface of the slab be parallel to the surface of the horizon, levelled with the plumb line, inclined to no side. Then from the place where the end of the shadow of the gnomon falls on the lines of the hours one knows what has passed of the day in seasonal hours, in every town whose latitude is like the latitude for which you made the slab. The setting up of the slab can also be made right in another way: you find the altitude that has no declination in its azimuth, in the way that I set out for you earlier in the book; then you observe the altitude until, when it has come to the amount of the altitude for which you worked, you turn the slab so that the shadow of the gnomon falls on the line between east and west; when that is so, the setting up of the slab is right, provided that its face is level and not inclined. If you wish to find the altitude of an hour, or of two hours, or three, then, when you have found it by reckoning, you observe the shadow, and when it has come to that altitude which you wanted you turn the slab so that the shadow of the gnomon falls on the line of the hour for which you found the altitude. One can also find the azimuth of the altitude that you want, observe the altitude, and when it has come to the altitude whose azimuth you found, turn the slab so that the shadow of the gnomon falls (the sentence runs on).
