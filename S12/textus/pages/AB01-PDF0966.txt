% Printed p. 186: the end of chapter 50: the test of the diameters with two alidades, and the arcs which the diameters of the sun, the planets and the moon subtend at their farthest, mean and nearest distances. [The hole next to the star is made of the size of the whole body of the star, no more] and no less; they measure the sun with another alidade in the same way and so know the diameter of the hole with which they measured the sun, and they measure against it the hole with which they measured the star; let the measuring be at one place of the horizon. What is left of what we have described of the magnitudes of the stars is [the magnitudes] which their diameters subtend of the circle of the sphere at their mean distance, and we set out also the magnitudes which they must subtend in the measuring when they are at their farthest distance and their nearest distance. «The sun»: as for the diameter of the sun, we have shown that it is 39;48, and we leave it as it is in all the grades of its distances, since no sensible amount comes of its change. «The Fighter»: as for the diameter of Saturn, it subtends at its farthest distance 16;22, at the mean 36;39 and at the nearest 33;36. «Jupiter»: as for Jupiter, its diameter subtends at its farthest distance 35;36, at the mean 14;45 and at the nearest 29;36. «Mars»: as for Mars, its diameter subtends of the circle of the sphere at its farthest distance 36;10, at the mean 16;14 and at the nearest 33;36. «Venus»: as for Venus, its diameter subtends of the sphere at its farthest distance 0;10, at the mean 0;39 and at the nearest 0;36. «The Scribe»: as for the diameter of Mercury, its diameter subtends of the circle of the sphere at its farthest distance 0;33, at the mean 0;6 and at the nearest 0;14. «The moon»: as for the diameter of the moon, it has been made plain that it subtends of the circle of the sphere at its farthest distance 0;29,40, at the mean 0;39,36 and at the nearest 0;36,22. These magnitudes differ between the distances named according to the grades of the stars in their distances, and that is known from their equations. The distance of the star from the point of the farthest distance of the epicycle and from the point of the nearest distance is known from the mean equation, corrected, by the fifth column or the seventh; the distance of the centre of the epicycle from the point of the farthest distance of the eccentric sphere is known from the equation of the anomaly; and from these two equations the grade of the star in its distance and its nearness from the earth is known, when that is measured against the sixty which are the half diameter, as we have shown in the knowledge of the distances of the moon from the difference of its motions. The notes: on line 2 perhaps «that we show» is to be added; the codex has «and it is», after which «the magnitudes» seems to have fallen out in the archetype; as many numbers, so many errors, and those read in Plato are no less faulty.
