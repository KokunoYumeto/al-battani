The example of Aries goes on: at three seasonal hours from midheaven toward the east its rising is less than its rising in the right sphere by 4;20, half of what lies between its rising in the right sphere and its rising at the horizon of the climate; and so until it reaches the ascendant, where its rising is less than in the right sphere by 8;41, the whole difference between the two ascensions. Let the beginning of Aries next be two seasonal hours from midheaven toward the west: since its settings in this western half are like the ascensions of Libra, its settings at this distance are more than its ascensions in the right sphere by a third of the difference, 2;54; at three hours on this side its passage is more than its passage in the right sphere by half the difference, 4;20; and at the western horizon its passage in setting is more than its rising in the right sphere by the whole difference, 8;41. When its distance is taken from the cardine of the earth toward the ascendant, its way is the way between midheaven and the ascendant; when it is taken from the cardine of the earth toward the setting, the matter is as between midheaven and the west. When you want to know the ascensions of whatever degree you wish, in whatever region of the sphere, begin by knowing the distance of the degree or the star from one of the cardines. Look at the degree or the star: if the star has no latitude, its way is the way of the degree of the signs in which it stands; find the times of the hours of that degree, by day and by night. If the star has a latitude, find the degree that culminates with it and the times of its hours above and below the earth: take half of its stay above the earth and a sixth of that, which is the times of one hour above the earth; subtract it from thirty, and the remainder is the times of its hours below the earth, as was explained at the beginning of the book. Use the degree that culminates with it in place of the degree in which it stands when it has a latitude, and the degree in which it stands when it has none; and likewise the times of the hours of the star in place of the times of the hours of its degree that culminates with it. If the degree that you use is above the earth, take its distance from the part of midheaven by the ascensions of the right sphere; if it is below the earth, take its distance from the part of the cardine of the earth, also by the ascensions of the right sphere, by subtracting the ascensions of the degree of midheaven from the ascensions of the degree that you use.
