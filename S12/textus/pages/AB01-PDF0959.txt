[Then what] results of the days passed of the month and of the hours are the days of the computed position and its hours; find with them the mean place of the sun in the year that you wished, in the way set out before: the mean place of the sun comes out the same as the mean place that was at the root. If you like, multiply the years that you have by three degrees and twenty-four minutes, which is the amount by which the length of the year falls short of the full quarter of a day over 365; keep what results; then cast out fours from the years that you have: if one remains, you take ninety degrees for it; if two remain, you take 180 for them; if three remain, you take 270 for them; and if four remain, you take 360 for them; from whichever of these you have got, you cast what you kept, and for what remains you reckon an hour for every 15 degrees and add it to the hours of the computed position, in the same way. The sense of these two ways is one. What comes out for you by either of the two ways is the date; compute for it the mean place of the sun in the first way, as usual, and then its true place: if the true place of the sun comes out the same as the first place, that is the time of the revolution; if it exceeds the true place of the sun at the root, see how much time the excess is by the motion of the sun in an hour, and subtract it from the hours of the computed position; and if it falls short, see how much time the defect is by the motion of the sun in an hour, and add it to the hours of the computed position, until you have made right the hours of the computed position by which you bring the sun to the true place in which it was at the root. For those hours you compute also the place of the moon and of the other planets. Then you turn them into the hours of the unequal days that are found, by subtracting what stands against the degree of the sun in the equation of the days, in the ascensions of the right sphere, after you know its amount in equal hours: what there is, is equal hours after midday; by them you know the ascendant and the cardines in the usual way. This difference comes about in the true place of the sun only through the motion of its apogee in the years between the year of the root and the year of the revolution: when the sun is near the apogee, the change is only slight and not perceptible, and so too near its perigee, and the farther it is from these two points, the greater is the difference in them. It is known that the times of the revolutions, whenever one hundred and six years have passed, go forward by one day from the day of the month on which they were at the root. We have made, for the times of the mean revolutions of the years and for the mean places of the stars at them, new tables, the use of which we have explained at the end of the chapters, at the close of the book, so that it be easy to work with them when they are needed.
